# How many code bits suffice to fit a finite SAT suite?

**Finite-suite theorem.** Let $S$ consist of $m$ distinct $n$-bit strings with arbitrary specified Boolean labels, with $1\le m\le n^h$ for fixed $h$. A deterministic word RAM program with $O_h(m+\log(n+2))$ **bits of code** returns all those labels in $O_h(n/w+\log^3(n+2))$ steps, where $w=\Theta(\log(n+2))$ is the fixed machine family's word width. The program can depend on the suite. In particular its clock on the suite is $o(n)$ as $n$ grows. The linear dependence on $m$ is optimal in the worst case over arbitrary labellings: a fixed suite admits $2^m$ labellings and there are fewer than $2^m$ binary programs of length less than $m$. For $m\ge2$ the width term is necessary too. If programs of at most $C$ bits realize every labelling of every $m$-element subset, every two distinct strings must have different output signatures over those programs, since an oppositely labelled pair extends to an $m$-set. There are at most $2^{C+1}$ programs and at most three deadline outcomes per program (zero, one, timeout or error combined). Thus $2^n\le3^{2^{C+1}}$, giving $C\ge\log_2 n-O(1)$. Together these give the tight order $\Theta_h(m+\log n)$ in the worst case over suites and arbitrary labels. Neither counting argument implies that SAT realizes a worst-case labelling.

The model uses packed input words, addition, multiplication, shifts, Boolean operations and direct finite-control branches. A program's finite binary encoding charges its literals, opcodes and targets. Constant-size opcodes and register names, self-delimiting binary integers of $O(1+\log(2+|v|))$ bits, and ordinary binary instruction labels suffice. There is no array initialized from the program text. The argument adapts polynomial fingerprints and two-level perfect hashing to retrieval of one promised label. Static retrieval data structures and linear-space perfect hashing have an extensive prior literature. [Fredman, Komlós and Szemerédi](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall09/cos521/Handouts/storingasparse.pdf) · [Dietzfelbinger and Pagh, *Succinct Data Structures for Retrieval and Approximate Membership*](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3693)

## Two reductions of the universe

Choose a prime $p>\max(2^w,4nm^2)$ smaller than twice this bound plus two. The packed words of each input give the coefficients of a distinct polynomial of degree less than $n$ over $\mathbb F_p$, adding zero padding to the last word. Fewer than $n\binom m2$ field points are roots of any pairwise difference. Some fixed evaluation point $\alpha$ therefore distinguishes the entire suite. Given a prime $p$ and range $M$, write

$$H_{a,b,M}(x)=((ax+b)\bmod p)\bmod M,\qquad a\ne0.$$

Uniform $a,b$ send a given pair of distinct field elements to a uniform ordered pair of distinct values. At most $\lfloor(p-1)/M\rfloor$ other residues share the first one's remainder, so collision probability is at most $1/M$. With $M=2m^2$, one such affine hash distinguishes all $m$ fingerprints by a union bound. Their images now lie in $[0,2m^2)$. The evaluation point, prime and affine coefficients take $O_h(\log n)$ bits together. All choices here and below can be found deterministically by finite search in constructing the **program**, before running it on an input.

Horner evaluation reads the $\lceil n/w\rceil$ packed words in reverse order if the first word is the constant term. The field elements have $O_h(w)$ bits. Addition, comparisons and products on this fixed number of words take $O_h(1)$ operations by splitting into limbs of at most half a word and propagating carries. For the streaming reduction by the fixed modulus $p$, precompute $\mu=\lfloor 2^{2b}/p\rfloor$ for $b=\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$. For $0\le X<2^{2b}$, the quotient $\lfloor X\mu/2^{2b}\rfloor$ differs downward from $\lfloor X/p\rfloor$ by at most one. One conditional subtraction after multiplying back suffices. Reduce $ab$ before adding another field element to stay within this range. All constants occupy $O_h(w)$ code bits. If width is not supplied, the program can compute it with shifts at startup; this costs $O(w)$ steps. Outside the streaming loop logarithmically many bit steps for an ordinary division also fit the query bound.

## An $O(m)$-bit table of labels

Use a second prime $q$ just above $2m^2$. From now on use the displayed affine family over $\mathbb F_q$. Select a first-level hash with $M=m$. If the $m$ bucket counts are $b_i$, then the expectation of $\sum_i b_i^2$ is at most $m+2\binom m2/m<2m$. Fix a choice attaining at most $2m$.

For a nonempty bucket of size $b_i$, reserve $2b_i^2$ bits. An affine hash with $M=2b_i^2$ maps its keys into these local slots without collisions with probability at least $3/4$. Use a **shared list** of $J=\lceil\log_2(4m)\rceil$ independently sampled affine parameter pairs; the range $M$ still varies by bucket. The probability that any bucket exhausts the list is at most $m4^{-J}$. The expected sum of truncated one-based first-success indices over all nonempty buckets, with $J$ charged on failure, is at most $4m/3$. By Markov's inequality and the union bound, one list serves every bucket and has sum of first-success indices at most $8m$. Fix that list. Its seed bits cost $O(\log^2(m+2))=O(m)$. Empty buckets have chosen index zero.

Store the given label at the chosen slot of every key, and arbitrary bits elsewhere. The total payload is at most $4m$ bits. For each bucket encode its size and selected hash index, each plus one, by Elias gamma code. Their aggregate length is $O(m)$ because the length for $v+1$ is $O(1+v)$, $\sum_i b_i=m$, and the chosen indices sum to $O(m)$. Every individual code has $O(\log(m+2))$ bits. Partition the directory into groups of $\lceil\log_2(m+2)\rceil$ buckets. Each group has a fixed-width entry storing its bit offset in the directory and the total number of payload slots of preceding groups. These entries cost $O(m)$ more bits. A lookup starts at the right group, inspects $O(\log^2(m+2))$ directory bits, accumulates the preceding bucket capacities within its group, and locates the one label bit via the selected secondary hash. Empty buckets can return zero immediately.

## Encoding the table as charged program text

Pack the labels, seed list, group entries and directory into consecutive chunks of at most $w$ bits. A balanced binary decision tree of direct branches dispatches a requested chunk index to a leaf that moves its fixed literal into a register. Control returns to a fixed query continuation. A constant set of call sites can use a fixed return selector. Query instructions reuse this tree. There are $O(1+m/w)$ leaves and internal nodes. Each has $O_h(w)$ bits of literals, branch labels and opcodes because $m\le n^h$. The tree has $O_h(m+w)$ bits of text and returns a chunk in $O_h(1+\log(m+2))$ steps. Indices and thresholds exceeding a word use a constant number of limbs; code labels are ordinary finite-control targets. The additional fixed query routine, arithmetic constants and their targets cost $O_h(w)$ bits. An instruction set limiting total program size to the word-addressable range can use the statement with $m\le n$ and the usual $2^w\ge n$.

Even retrieving every directory bit through a separate tree traversal costs only $O_h(\log^3(n+2))$ steps. A variable bucket capacity or offset needs at most $O_h(w)$ bits. Schoolbook bit division and scalar operations after scanning the input fit within the same additive time bound: there are $O(\log(m+2))$ bucket records parsed, and $O(1)$ field hash evaluations per final lookup. No $\Omega(m)$ charged initialization is hidden in the query time. The theorem concerns what a finite test suite can refute. The fitted program is guaranteed to return SAT labels only on the supplied formulas; it can be wrong elsewhere.

The same argument handles suite lengths in $[n,n^2]$ with $m\le n^h$. Use chunks of a fixed size at most the smallest word width in that interval, yet of order $\log n$. Mark the length by appending a one bit to the string before chunking, which makes its packed polynomial unique even when the original ends with zeros. Missing high coefficients are zero. For the first prime bound replace $n$ by $n^2+1$. The word widths on the interval differ by only a fixed factor. Constant-count arithmetic limbs sized for the smallest width and at most two native reads per extracted chunk implement the same calculation. On an input of actual length $t$, scan only its $O(t/\log n)$ chunks. The $O_h(m+\log n)$ code and its $o(t)$ deadline on the suite are preserved.

Consequently a suite refuting **every** program of at most $C$ code bits, at any fixed positive linear multiplier and sufficiently large lengths in such a range, must satisfy $C<A_h(m+\log(n+2))$ for a fixed model-dependent constant $A_h$. If $C$ dominates the logarithmic term, a number of examples linear in $C$ is necessary. If the examples span more widely separated scales or $\log n$ itself dominates $C$, the estimate gives no such linear requirement. This measures a cost of finite exhaustion; the existence of a defeating suite of *any* size for every code budget remains unproved. ## A globally correct decider at a different code budget

For a suite of $m$ labelled SAT files of the same length $n$, there is also a globally correct program taking at most $\log_2 m+5\lceil n/w\rceil+O(\log(n+2))$ executed instructions on each file in the suite. This second construction pays for the actual file contents in its finite program. A lookup label that is wrong on a file outside the suite is never returned. If $m\le 2^{O(n/w)}$, the clock is linear in the number of input words on the entire suite, even for a correct decider. Both constructions are nonuniform in the choice of the finite suite.

Write the suite as a trie of packed words and skip positions with only one possible next word. At a remaining node with $z$ files, the sorted distinct next words have positive child counts $z_j$. A word reaching child $j$ can be identified using at most $\lceil\log_2(z/z_j)\rceil+1$ binary threshold comparisons on the loaded word. To see this, allocate consecutive intervals of lengths $z_j/z$ in the unit interval. The dyadic cell containing the midpoint at depth $\lceil\log_2(z/z_j)\rceil+1$ lies in that interval. These disjoint cells give an alphabetically ordered prefix code. Each fork among their codewords separates two ordered sets of possible word values, so it is a single unsigned comparison to an immediate threshold. Unary forks can be bypassed. The logarithms telescope along a root-to-leaf path. Including one LOAD at each branch position, identification costs at most $\log_2 m+3\lceil n/w\rceil$ steps. [Gilbert and Moore, alphabetic binary codes](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1959.tb01583.x)

At the reached leaf compare the whole input to the leaf's literal file in at most $2\lceil n/w\rceil$ more steps by direct loads and comparisons. A match returns the known true label. Any mismatch, or any other length, goes to a fixed exhaustive SAT algorithm. A guard for the public length can compare its bits to the hardwired length by repeated one-bit shifts and a final zero check. This works even when a long literal would have been truncated on a smaller input. The validation paths cost $O(m\lceil n/w\rceil)$ stored instructions with their literals and targets charged separately. This statement puts no small bound on that code. For a lower bound on every fixed program, new witness lengths must escape such finite specializations.
