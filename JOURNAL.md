# Research journal

## Earlier research and conditional tests

I obtained an unconditional approximation for the times when short programs first produce new words. At each length, most of a family of clock resolutions give a histogram with vanishing total absolute error and a description using fewer than a linear number of bits in the number of bins. If SAT takes linear time, **no fixed algorithm can choose such accurate resolutions by the first cutoff at infinitely many lengths**, even when the forecaster gets the exact counts for all preceding cutoffs. The missing step is finding a timely resolution for the actual programs. An explicit family of abstract measures shows why dividing the programs among resolutions loses too much accuracy. Three separate tests use the actual decisions of candidate solvers. At most $k+1$ local checks can characterize the correct XOR combinations of $k$ solvers on a finite restriction-closed family. For unit propagation, deterministically finding a local error from a dense layer of favorable restrictions would give deterministic search for dense Boolean circuits. For a unique witness, a decision trace consisting of one layer of products of linear forms leads to recovery of the diagonal of a symmetric matrix over the binary field. I narrowed the matrix question in the linear SAT test. For a sufficiently large binary square of side $J$, if at most $\lfloor0.2883J\rfloor$ rows are nonzero, one can change at most $J^2/16$ entries to reduce its rank to $\lfloor J/16\rfloor$. For $\lfloor0.2816J\rfloor$ nonzero rows I obtained a deterministic algorithm producing the factors in matrix-multiplication time up to logarithmic factors. Thus it would suffice to prove the first row-count bound infinitely often for the fixed program's actual output. Under linear SAT its matrix instead has between about $0.2883J$ and $0.29805J$ nonzero rows asymptotically. Which behavior occurs remains open. The linear target has an exact test using the last output bit of one fixed program. Arrange those bits in a square of side $L=2^m$. If a linear SAT solver exists, at every sufficiently large phase its table is more than $L^2/16$ bits away from every binary matrix of rank at most $L/16$. If every linear-time candidate fails, the table is identically zero throughout intervals $j\le m<2^j$ for arbitrarily large $j$. The program checks which candidate agrees with brute-force SAT on growing sets of short formulas. Neither behavior has been proved unconditionally. On the candidate rigid table, clearing any one-entry already brings it within the allowed distance. This identifies exactly where an approximation argument can miss by one bit. I found a way to use **parities of short programs** to test all three SAT time targets. Arrange the words of even length $q$ in a square with $L=2^{q/2}$ rows. Select short programs by a random polynomial on their names, and enter the parity of the selected names that produce each word. Under linear-time SAT, at most a $2^{-\Omega(q^2)}$ fraction of these matrices can be changed in a small constant fraction of their entries to give binary rank at most $\kappa L$. The test can ignore every word already produced by time $2^q$. Thus a low-rank construction for a fraction $2^{-o(q^2)}$ of the selections would suffice. I obtained low rank for every selection on a particular nonlinear program family; the full interpreter remains unresolved. The quadratic and cubic versions have smaller target ranks. Even a single power of the name index must give hard parity matrices for almost every scalar multiplier at some exponent in every sufficiently long run of consecutive powers. Ruling out **linear time** remains the first success condition. I derived a way to do it by proving a Boolean circuit **upper bound** for one auxiliary language. On $q$ bits, the language asks whether every program saving at least two bits misses a specified exponential deadline. If SAT takes linear time, every circuit with at most $c\,2^q/q$ gates gets at least $\epsilon 2^q$ answers wrong at all sufficiently large lengths, for some fixed $c,\epsilon>0$. An accurate small circuit on infinitely many lengths would contradict that implication. I have no such circuit. A hitting-set argument by Buhrman, Fortnow, Koucký and Loff precedes this approach for programs without a deadline. [Deadline, proof and attempted upper bound](notes/full-cube-advice.md)

A randomized search could also suffice. It can return a different large set on every successful run and include some easy words. I bounded the probability of getting so few easy words under the linear-SAT hypothesis. Removing all outputs detected at an earlier deadline and sampling the remaining words independently already fails at the required rate. The usual random affine hash isolates any specified hard $m$-bit table with probability at most $2^{-m}$ per trial when at most $L$ tables have short programs and $L(L+1)\le 2^m-1$. This holds even with an oracle that returns the sole hard table when it exists. For general pairwise-independent buckets I derived the exact maximum probability at each marginal and number of hard tables. A fast randomized hardness test on a short initial list would give a different route. On some candidate, the random tapes certifying its correct answer must evade even a covering test that can fix a quarter of the log deadline in arbitrary positions. No such tester has been found. A possible way to rule out linear SAT is to find a word that a fixed randomized sampler prints frequently by a deadline, even though every short deterministic program is too slow. For inverse-polylogarithmic probabilities and words no longer than the logarithm of the deadline, this test is equivalent to checking a single interpreter on binary integers, giving the integer itself as the starting time allowance. There is now a test for linear SAT using only programs that print zeros. Give a program a cost of its length plus the logarithm of its running time. For each word of n zeros, compare the best cost with the total weight of all programs printing it, where each program’s ordinary probability is divided by its running time. Linear SAT would bound the difference between the best cost and the negative logarithm of that weight by O(log log n), for every n on each fixed efficient interpreter. An ordinary interpreter can attain this order, even with any prescribed fixed coefficient as the interpreter varies. The search needs an unbounded ratio on one fixed interpreter. I also proved the upper bound unconditionally outside a quantitatively small set of lengths and constructed very deep lengths where the test still passes. If SAT had a linear-time algorithm, the following coding bound would hold. Suppose a fixed randomized machine prints a particular word by time t with probability p. For words of polylogarithmic length in t, some deterministic program of at most ⌈log₂(1/p)⌉ + O(1) bits would print that word in time t · polylog(t), without being told the deadline. For much longer words, one program of the same length can answer each individual bit query within that clock when log₂(1/p) is polylogarithmic in t. An explicit sampler that violates either guarantee would rule out linear SAT. The constructed example that violates the first bound relies on an external oracle. A fixed additive gap now suffices for one test of the linear-time target. For each output word of length m, record how many bits its shortest program saves over m at a late deadline. A single efficient universal machine with arbitrarily long gaps would therefore rule out O(n) time for SAT. Obtaining sufficiently small local views for an appropriate hierarchy verifier remains open. [Local views and their weighted agreement test](notes/local-views.md)

The first search found a family of width-three formulas with k independent input signs. Across every equal split of those signs, the log rank of the satisfiability matrix is at least k/2 − O(k/log k). Yet a RAM decides this promised family in linear time by evaluating its forced gates. It also gave a conditional speedup for checking a compressed accepting computation, and verifiers that defeat the proposed compression parameters. ## Literature checks

For the linear-time target, the deterministic random-access results of Fortnow, Lipton, van Melkebeek and Viglas impose a simultaneous space bound. A pure time bound by van Melkebeek and Raz applies to a machine with one geometrically organized work tape. [Fortnow et al., full text](https://lance.fortnow.com/papers/files/tst.pdf) · [Van Melkebeek and Raz](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304397505005402)

Williams's alternation-trading work also charges space. Mudigonda and Williams state the subpolynomial-space SAT bound for exponents strictly below 2 cos(π/7), approximately 1.802. Even that exponent lies below two. [Williams](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rrw/automated-lbs.pdf) · [Mudigonda and Williams](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00330)

Williams's square-root-space simulation concerns multitape Turing machines. The 2026 two-fifths-power preprint concerns a machine with one writable tape. Both give space upper bounds for simulation. Our trace-checking approach requires an actual time saving on a specified language in the appropriate machine model. [Williams, STOC 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17779) · [One-tape simulation, 2026 preprint](https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/Simulating-One-Tape-Time-in-Two-Fifths-Power-Space-September-25-2026)

The individual notes distinguish literature precedents, elementary deductions, finite checks, and unproved premises. Priority of the auxiliary constructions has not been established.
