# Can two competing witnesses certify a missing output?

**Research journal, 2026-11-24.** A referee with one private question to each of two competing provers can have very different verification costs depending on how its questions are generated and which errors it allows. Two quantitative tests below apply to the marked-address and parity constructions. The first also gives an explicit candidate based on locally checkable proofs of bounded program outputs.

## One refuter that never loses

Fix a candidate z. A finite uniform coin string r specifies a question u(r) for the left prover and v(r) for the right prover. On receiving answers a and b the referee evaluates a Boolean predicate D(r,a,b). The left prover wants one and the right prover wants zero. The payoff may use private randomness included in r.

A right strategy that wins on **every** coin against every left strategy exists exactly when

    ∀v ∃b ∀r,a   [v(r) ≠ v or D(r,a,b)=0].

Indeed an action b chosen separately for each v gives the required policy. In the other direction, if a proposed action loses for some r,a, the constant left strategy returning that a defeats it on r. A mixed right strategy guaranteeing zero expected payoff can use only safe actions on right questions with positive probability. Negating the display gives the three-block test

    ∃v ∀b ∃r,a   [v(r)=v and D(r,a,b)=1].                    (1)

When (1) holds, uniform independent left answers give strictly positive payoff against every right policy. For r of length R and a of length A the payoff is at least 2^(−R−A). Thus even an arbitrarily small positive gap above zero suffices. Conversely every ordinary three-block predicate ∃v∀b∃y H(v,b,y) has this support interpretation. The referee draws v,y uniformly, sends v to the right prover, and tests H(v,b,y), with a dummy left answer. These claims concern positive versus zero value, a different promise from the bounded-error one-round games for PSPACE. [Feige and Kilian](https://weizmann.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/making-games-short/) · [Lean formulation](../lean/RefereeSupport.lean)

Let w be the first length-d string missing from the bounded universal machine at source parameter n, with d=n or 2(n−1) and long cutoff 2^(20n). Suppose at infinitely many n, (1) holds for w and fails for all but o(2ᵈ) points **preceding** w. On larger points the game can behave arbitrarily. Assume all message and coin strings and the deterministic referee calculation fit T=2^(n/8)poly(n) steps. A cubic SAT algorithm decides (1) on a word RAM in T^(27+o(1)) time. Use a single fixed referee and encodings computable from n and z within the budget. At each of the three blocks compile the bounded existential RAM computation into quasilinear-size SAT, complementing answers at the universal block. Pad the input of each bounded simulation to its clock when needed so that logarithmic words can address its workspace; writing that padding fits the stated reduction cost. Simulating on tapes and listing the accepted candidates costs 2ᵈ T^(54+o(1)) native tape time. Even quadratic universal simulation fits 2^(20n), since 2(2+54/8)<20. The lexicographic rank of w on this list can be encoded with d−ω(1) bits. This contradicts its construction. The reduction allows an entirely different perfect right policy at every preceding position and no efficient way to find it. [Marked-address construction](address-diagonal.md) · [Efficient SAT completeness](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rrw/automated-lbs.pdf)

One may tolerate e bad random strings per right question and reply by replacing the final witness r,a in (1) with e+1 distinct strings r and respective left answers. Sorting prevents double counting. This recognizes the existence of a v such that **every** b has more than e bad coins. The clock now charges for all e+1 records. It does not suffice merely to show nonexistence of a flawless computation history when e>0.

## A concrete trial using locally checkable histories

The bounded-output language consisting of pairs (1ⁿ,z) for which some description shorter than d prints z by the long cutoff belongs to nondeterministic exponential time. Fix a probabilistic oracle-proof verifier for this language with polynomial-time verification, polynomially many random bits, and a constant completeness–soundness gap. Classical NEXP oracle proofs supply such a verifier. Amplify to completeness at least 7/8 and soundness at most 1/8 by a constant number of repetitions against the same fixed proof. [Babai, Fortnow and Lund](https://lance.fortnow.com/papers/files/mip2.pdf)

Use a circuit C with description length at most s to answer the verifier's oracle queries. Count all description bits, including the indices of gates and the constants. A fixed candidate's deterministic evaluation on a given random tape costs s·poly(n) on a RAM for s≤2^n, loading its topologically ordered gate list once and charging even adaptive evaluations. A circuit is useful at z if its acceptance probability is at least 2/3. At w, every such probability, for *every* circuit, is at most 1/8.

Here is a deterministic test, under cubic SAT, which accepts every z with a useful C and rejects w. For a proposed C amplify its verification test with O(log(n+2)) independent repetitions and majority, until its error on acceptance probabilities ≥2/3 or ≤1/8 is less than 1/(4(R+1)), where R is the resulting polynomial random-tape length. On the cube of random tapes, ask if there are R+1 XOR shifts of the accepting set whose union is the entire cube. When its complement has density at most 1/(4(R+1)), independent uniform shifts leave fewer than one point uncovered in expectation. When the accepting set has that small density, no R+1 shifts can cover the cube. Guess C and all shifts in the same existential block. The universal block chooses one tape and checks whether any shift accepts by deterministic evaluation. This is the classical covering argument for bounded-error randomness. Its test matrix costs s·poly(n), and only two SAT reductions are needed. [Lautemann](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0020019083900443)

In particular fix s=⌊2^(δn)⌋ for any fixed rational 0<δ<4/9 when d=2(n−1), or δ<1/2 when d=n. Under cubic SAT, tabulation over all z costs 2^(d+18δn+o(n)) on tapes before universal simulation. Scoring all intervals in the table costs 2^(2d+o(n)) by sequential scans and sorting. Both fit the long clock even after a quadratic slowdown. Apply the one-sided maximal inequality for the first hole to the accepted set. For some c>0 at every sufficiently large length, every interval [w−K,w) in range contains at least c(K+1)−1 predecessors **on which no size-s description of a proof circuit attains acceptance 2/3**. All these predecessors really have short output programs halting by the cutoff, so all have unrestricted proofs with acceptance at least 7/8. The statement concerns the best circuit among all possible proof oracles at each position. It allows arbitrary behavior by the test between the two probability thresholds. [Simultaneous interval estimate](predecessor-batches.md)

The required advantage can even be exponentially small. Write ε=2^(−⌈γn⌉), with a fixed rational γ>0. Define a useful C by acceptance at least 1/8+ε and distinguish it from acceptance at most 1/8. Use h=⌈n^J ε^(−2)⌉ trials and a threshold of 1/8+ε/2, for a sufficiently large fixed J. Keep the amplified random tape polynomial in n by using pairwise independent trials. If the original verifier uses r random bits, choose a uniform binary r-by-⌈log₂ h⌉ matrix and an independent uniform r-bit offset, and apply that affine map to the h distinct trial indices. Every two images are uniform and independent since a nonzero index difference maps uniformly and the independent offset randomizes the first image. Chebyshev bounds either classification error by 1/(hε²). The new seed length R=r(1+⌈log₂ h⌉) is polynomial and satisfies 1/(hε²)<1/(4(R+1)) for large n by choosing J larger than the polynomial degree of r plus two. The two-block shift test still applies. Its deterministic matrix now takes s ε^(−2) poly(n) RAM time, including all shifts. Thus the same simultaneous interval conclusion holds with this new threshold whenever δ+2γ<4/9 for d=2(n−1), or δ+2γ<1/2 for d=n. For example δ=1/3 and γ=1/24 work in both cases. This variant tests even very weak success by the best proof circuit on a true predecessor.

This identifies a positive route as well. A construction of sufficiently good proof circuits on all but o(K) points of even a single trailing interval with K tending to infinity, along infinitely many lengths, would refute cubic SAT. The PCP theorem supplies the long proof but gives no circuit bound of this scale for its bits. The easy-witness literature derives small witness circuits from additional circuit upper-bound hypotheses. It does not supply the needed construction on these particular lengths. [Murray and Williams](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rrw/easy-witness-nqp.pdf) A single short program that outputs z after a long computation need not provide cheap random access to a locally checkable proof of that computation.

## Sampling independent or weakly correlated challenges

There is also a bounded-error simulation that does not require small circuits for the provers. Let the question distribution have marginals μ,ν and joint likelihood ratio W(u,v)=Pr[u,v]/(μ(u)ν(v)) between zero and L on their supports. Assume the marginals can be sampled separately and W and the conditional mean payoff F(u,v,a,b) in [0,1] can be evaluated efficiently as rationals of charged bit length. Independent questions have W=1. For a sampled pair use W·F as its score. Suppose the high case admits a left strategy guaranteeing expected score in the original game at least 2/3 against all right strategies. In the low case a right strategy guarantees at most 1/3. Let A and B be the maximum answer lengths in bits, using fixed-length padded alphabets.

Independently sample k row questions from μ and l column questions from ν, with repetitions retained as separate indices. For an absolute sufficiently large constant, take

    k = ⌈C L(B+1)⌉,        l = ⌈C L(A+1)⌉.

On these fixed questions the high certificate gives one answer for each row index and requires the average cross score to be greater than 1/2 for **every** tuple of column answers. The low certificate gives one answer for each column index and requires the average to be at most 1/2 for **every** tuple of row answers. The certificates exclude each other. In either promised case the correct certificate exists with probability at least 9/10. The required counts do not depend on the number of possible questions. Sampling sparse strategies and subsampling games have considerable precedents. Here the two players have opposed objectives and the finite certificate uses one answer per sampled occurrence. [Lipton and Young](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0205035) · [Aaronson, Impagliazzo and Moshkovitz on cooperative free games](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.6848)

For the high case draw the left strategy's response independently with each row question. Fix v,b and write X=W(u,v)F(u,v,a,b) for this draw and g(v,b)=E[X]. Then 0≤X≤L, E[X]≤1, and E[X²]≤L. For θ≤1/L the moment generating functions for either sign of X−E[X] are at most exp(θ²L), using exp(t)≤1+t+t² on [−1,1] with t=±θX and then 1+t≤exp(t). For the average of k copies, the log-sum-exp bound over the at most 2ᴮ possible answers gives

    E[max_b |avg X − g(v,b)|] ≤ ln(2·2ᴮ)/(kθ) + θ L.

Choose θ=1/(1000L) and C large enough for the right side to be at most 1/500. This holds for each v, so the expected mean of these errors across sampled columns is also at most 1/500. With probability greater than 0.95 it is below 1/20 by Markov's inequality. Meanwhile the independent column draws estimate E_v min_b g(v,b) to within 1/20 with probability at least 0.95 by Chebyshev for large C. This expectation is at least 2/3 by optimal per-question response to the chosen strategy. The sampled row answers therefore defeat every column tuple at the cutoff. Averaging over the draws of the row answers proves the claimed probability for the questions alone. In the low case, draw column answers from the guaranteeing right strategy and use maxima over row answers. The same moment bounds hold since W integrates to one also in the other coordinate. Shared public information visible to both provers can first be conditioned on; sampling per public value and averaging requires accounting for that outer sampling as well.

For the earlier noisy coded diagonal H, suppose such games distinguish the true coded bit at infinitely many lengths, on all valid queries there. One fixed algorithm must specify the game, samplers, weight and conditional payoff from n and the coded query, with the same sampled questions for the two proposed answer bits. Let h bound the sampled data, total random bits, lengths of both tuples, the query-specific time to generate all sampled questions from the advice tape, and the time to build and evaluate the empirical decision predicate once, including all kl score evaluations and rational arithmetic. If h≤2^(n/3)poly(n) uniformly, cubic SAT refutes this game premise. Supply a fixed sample-generating tape as advice of length o(2ⁿ). For each query guess the certifying tuple for the proposed bit. An ordinary SAT call checks whether some adversarial tuple violates its inequality. A quasilinear encoding and cubic SAT put this inner call within 2ⁿpoly(n) RAM time for the resulting nondeterministic verifier. If the coded construction is formulated on tapes, the nondeterministic verifier can check the RAM transcript by sorting its recorded reads and writes within a polylogarithmic factor. At a good length some tape gives the correct certificate on at least 9/10 of queries by averaging. The signed predictor never certifies both bits, so correlation is at least 4/5 even if all remaining outcomes are wrong. This exceeds the coded diagonal's vanishing upper bound. The missing step is an efficient game with the required question distribution for H's actual bits. Short communication in a general competing-prover protocol leaves the private question correlations uncontrolled. [Coded diagonal and advice bound](coded-diagonal.md) · [Kol and Raz, competing provers for circuit evaluation](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~ranraz/publications/PCompeting-Provers.pdf)

There is one way to disclose correlated information without assuming the provers stay honest after disclosure. Suppose a common label c is already determined by the right question, and for each left question u at most K labels c are possible. Give c to the left prover too. If the enhanced game has value v′, the original game has value at least v′/K. The original left prover guesses c uniformly from the possibilities at its own question, independently of all other coins, and uses the enhanced strategy for that guess. Against any original right policy the matching-guess contribution is at least 1/K times the enhanced payoff, and all other contributions are nonnegative. Therefore original low value ≤s implies enhanced low value ≤Ks, while original high value ≥c₀ still gives enhanced high value ≥c₀. If c₀−Ks has a constant positive gap and conditional questions are independent and efficiently samplable given c, one can sample the public components and run the panel argument inside each. For precision here, the sampling proof works on a fixed component of arbitrary value V_c: sampled pure high and low guarantees hold relative to V_c up to any fixed additive error, using any desired fixed failure probability by increasing the sample constants. The public game's value is E_c V_c since both players see c. Draw a constant number of independent public values to estimate that mean, demand all their panel approximations succeed by a union bound, and average the sampled payoff over the components. Giving c to the left for free without a loss bound would be unsound: if its question is constant and it must guess a uniform right question among K values, disclosure raises 1/K to 1.

Here is a limitation on the likelihood-ratio calculation. A row question is (v,ξ) for independent uniform v in [M] and ξ in {0,1}, and the right question is that very same v. The left sends nothing. Pay one when the right answer differs from ξ. The true value is 1/2 and the likelihood ratio on compatible pairs is M. On the empirical panel the right player selects the majority observed ξ at each sampled v. For a given v with counts n₀,n₁ among the k independent row draws, the minimized contribution is M·min(n₀,n₁)/k. Since min(n₀,n₁)≤n₀n₁ for nonnegative integers, its expectation is at most (k−1)/(4M). This remains true for any number of independent column draws. Thus even one-bit answers can require order M row samples to estimate the value by this particular cross-product procedure. The example itself is easy to evaluate if the algorithm can sample conditionally on the shared v. A consistency check on a hidden common address needs a separate cost analysis.

## Constructing the game directly from the actual computation

There is a succinct game with independent questions for the coded bit of H, with a weaker gap. A fixed deterministic exponential-time tape program computes the coded bit by making the source table and taking its requested residue. Its time-by-cell tableau is a Boolean circuit with G=2^{O(n)} gates and wiring computable in poly(n) time from the gate indices and the input. For example, unroll a fixed single-tape simulation, use a fixed local transition circuit for each cell and time, propagate a halted state, and collect its answer by an output circuit. A quadratic tape simulation and a quadratic tableau size preserve 2^{O(n)}. This direct construction needs no competing-prover theorem.

For a fixed claimed output, put one variable xⱼ in [0,1] at each gate. Pin the inputs and claimed output, and impose the local gate hull inequalities. For z=x OR y these are z≥x, z≥y and z≤x+y. For z=x AND y use z≤x, z≤y and z≥x+y−1, and for complementation use z=1−x. Given the Boolean inputs, exact feasibility forces the correct values gate by gate. Let ℓᵢ(x)≤0 be these sparse inequalities and include a zero row. This is the classical circuit-value approach to linear programming. [Dobkin, Lipton and Reiss](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/linear-programming-is-log-space-hard-for-p/)

Pad the number of variable positions to a power of two G. The left prover, with no private question, names a constraint i, defaulting to the zero constraint for invalid indices. The right prover is asked a uniform gate j and supplies a bit b. For ℓᵢ(x)=cᵢ+Σⱼ aᵢⱼxⱼ, pay

    1/2 + (aᵢⱼ b + cᵢ/G)/8.

This lies in [0,1] for these gate inequalities. With xⱼ=Pr[b=1 at j], its expectation on row i is 1/2+ℓᵢ(x)/(8G). Every x in the unit cube is available to a randomized right strategy. By finite minimax the game value is 1/2+minₓ maxᵢ ℓᵢ(x)/(8G). A correct output claim gives exactly 1/2. An incorrect claim gives strictly more by compactness and infeasibility. Use the claim “output zero” to get the strict inequality for a true coded bit of one. The referee and the entire transmitted data now cost poly(n). The exact-support criterion (1) applies to zero probability of an accepting event. In this gate game the baseline is a signed expectation after subtracting 1/2, and a correct claim can have both positive and negative single-coin excesses.

A gate calculation tests whether the gap is automatically large. Start with x₀=0. At each stage form a separate copy yᵢ=xᵢ AND 1 and then xᵢ₊₁=xᵢ OR yᵢ. There are h stages, the true output is zero, and the claim is one. Set xᵢ=yᵢ=(2ⁱ−1)/(2ʰ−1), with xₕ=1. All copy, pin and lower OR inequalities hold exactly or with room to spare. Each upper OR violation is just 1/(2ʰ−1). The game gap is therefore at most 1/(8G(2ʰ−1)) even though the branches entering each OR come from separately named gates. If the endpoint pins are exact and all inequalities have slack at most η, lower inputs cannot grow arbitrarily quickly: xᵢ₊₁≤2xᵢ+2η using the copy upper inequality. This gives η≥1/(2(2ʰ−1)) on this example. With the input and output pins charged as inequalities, the exact minimum maximum violation is η=1/(3·2ʰ−1). Indeed x₀≤η and xᵢ₊₁≤2xᵢ+2η imply xₕ≤(3·2ʰ−2)η, while the output pin needs xₕ≥1−η. Equality is attained by xᵢ=(3·2ⁱ−2)η, yᵢ=xᵢ+η for i<h, with the constant-one input fixed at one. Consequently the exact game gap on this circuit is 1/(8G(3·2ʰ−1)). Adding any nonnegative combinations of the constraint rows with total multiplier weights at most B can increase their maximum violation at this assignment by at most B. Polynomially weighted combinations cannot eliminate the exponential loss. A direct Boolean implication from the output to its input would simplify this particular chain. The open question is whether a uniform change of constraints on the actual hierarchy computations gives a gap large enough for the sampled verifier, while preserving cheap coefficient access and the encoded clock. Priority for the auxiliary game bounds has not been established.

For a complementary lower estimate on the margin, unravel a backward proof of the actual Boolean output. At a true OR or false AND, keep one forcing child. At a false OR or true AND keep both children. At a NOT keep its child. Count every occurrence after unfolding the resulting tree; let its size be P. If all row violations are ≤η, the error of each relaxed gate from its actual bit is at most η plus the sum of the errors at the kept children, by the same gate inequalities. At a pinned input the error is at most η. Thus pinning the wrong output requires 1≤(P+1)η. This explains which reconverging paths the numerical bound pays for and gives a sufficient margin whenever a small semantic simplification makes both G and P affordable. Finding such simplifications with cheap local access for the hard source is unresolved.

Lean checks the logical support characterization, the incompatibility of the two empirical certificates, and the scaled inequality giving the gate chain’s lower bound. The probabilistic, circuit-complexity and time estimates are manuscript proofs.
