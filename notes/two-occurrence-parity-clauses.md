# How two occurrences affect mixed parity and clause checks

Let a finite Boolean system contain individual parity equations of any arity and individual signed binary disjunctions. Count how many rows actually contain a bit. Assume this count is at most two for every bit. Cancel repeated variables in parity equations, discard tautologies, and write unit clauses as parity equations. Keep a constant false equation as its own component. Identify connected components by shared bits between rows.

**Theorem.** In an unsatisfiable connected component, any specified row can be made the only violation. With nonnegative row weights, the minimum violated weight of the entire system therefore equals the sum of the lightest row weights in its unsatisfiable components. In particular, if every assignment violates weight at least a fraction $\delta>0$ of the positive total weight, an unsatisfiable subset uses at most $\lfloor1/\delta\rfloor$ original rows. This counts the number of row names, including when the weights are unequal.

For a sharp example, pin the first and last vertices of a path to one, and along the path equate adjacent bits except for one distinguished adjacent pair, where a clause forbids both bits being one. Omitting any single row suffices. With $L$ equally weighted rows the gap is exactly $1/L$ and its shortest contradiction uses $L$ rows. The parity rows by themselves and the clause by itself are each satisfiable.

## Proof

Represent each binary clause by a pair of ports. A port carries one when its literal is false. The clause permits choosing at most one of the pair. Rewrite occurrences shared by clauses and parity equations as follows.

- A variable used in a clause and a parity equation becomes the clause port in the parity equation, complementing it if its literal is positive. Move any constant to the right-hand side.
- For a variable shared by two clauses, make separate ports and impose one auxiliary parity equation equating the two underlying variable values. Its right-hand side accounts for their literal signs.
- A variable used only by one clause gives a free port. Give it a separate dummy vertex with no parity requirement.

Contract connected groups of parity equations joined by variables that occur in two parity equations and no clause. A group incident with a variable that occurs only once and only in a parity equation has no residual parity requirement. The other groups each require one specified parity on their incident ports, obtained by summing their right-hand sides. Indeed, toggling an internal bit toggles its two incident equations. Toggling bits along a path transfers an error from one endpoint to the other. In the presence of a parity bit of occurrence one, such an error can be removed; otherwise any even error pattern can be removed. An odd error can be concentrated on any one equation in the group. This argument applies to auxiliary groups as well. Free-port dummy vertices are also unconstrained.

The resulting connected multigraph has a vertex for each group or dummy, and an edge for each original clause joining the groups of its two ports. An unused edge gives no port a one. A used edge gives a one to exactly one endpoint. Loops and parallel edges are permitted. A component with a cycle can give every vertex a distinct incident edge, by orienting one cycle cyclically and a forest leading away from it. Keep only edges assigned to vertices requiring odd parity. A tree with an unconstrained or even-demand vertex can root there and assign each odd vertex its parent edge. These assignments satisfy all rows after choosing the internal bits.

The remaining case is a tree in which every vertex requires odd parity. It has fewer edges than vertices, and each odd vertex requires at least one port. To violate a specified clause, allow its edge to supply both endpoints; orient the trees attached at those endpoints away from the edge. Every parity row can then be satisfied. To violate a specified original parity row, root the tree at its group. The other vertices obtain a one from their parent edges, and the error in the root group can be placed on the specified row. With no edges, this argument is the ordinary inconsistent parity component. Auxiliary wire equations always hold in the resulting port assignment, so the assignment defines values for the original variables. This proves the component claim. If every unsatisfiable component had at least $\lfloor1/\delta\rfloor+1$ rows, paying its lightest row would give total violated weight at most the total weight divided by this larger integer, a contradiction.

For any two-bit Boolean relation, four accepted tuples impose no restriction, three give a single clause, and two give an affine equality or a pin. One accepted tuple gives two pins on distinct bits. An empty relation is itself a contradiction. Expanding thus preserves the two-occurrence condition and increases total row weight by at most a factor of two if each pin carries the original weight. Consequently arbitrary binary relations mixed with individual parity rows have an unsatisfiable subset of at most $\lfloor 2/\delta\rfloor$ original rows. The sharper component statement above concerns single clauses and parity rows.

## Application to the priority verifier

Take the uniform constant-query PCP for the signed [priority computation](priority-pinned-sat.md) at parameter $M=2^j$. Assume ordinary SAT runs in $O(n^k)$ time on the stated RAM. Let $g=\lfloor M^{1/k}/(j+1)^B\rfloor$ for a sufficiently large fixed $B$. On every sufficiently late false scale and for **every** weighted two-occurrence collection of individual parity or binary clause consequences of the seed tests, one proof table satisfies all but $1/(g+1)$ of the total weight. The assignment satisfies the two types jointly.

Indeed, guess at most $g$ original names, regenerate each seed relation and verify that the named equation or clause is entailed. Check the occurrence bound on the guessed rows. Contraction, parity accumulation and the tree test above decide compatibility in time $(g+1)\operatorname{poly}(j)$ using sorted addresses and graph traversal. On a true claim every such collection is compatible. A guessed contradiction therefore certifies the opposite sign. Encoding this nondeterministic computation and applying the hypothesized SAT decider gives an $o(M)$ signal. Bi-immunity excludes successes on sufficiently late false scales. If every incompatible component has at least $g+1$ rows, its lightest weight is at most its total divided by $g+1$. Collections can depend nonuniformly on the scale. [PCP and scale](low-randomness-free-winner.md) · [Encoding](packed-band-transfer.md)

The same certificate test allows named high-occurrence addresses. Guess up to $h$ distinct addresses, universally quantify their Boolean values, then guess and check a short incompatible two-occurrence subset after substituting the values. A true proof defeats this certificate by setting the addresses to its values. Three successive bounded-run encodings using an $O(n^k)$ SAT decider, with complementation for the universal block, cost at most $(h+g+1)^{k^3}\operatorname{poly}(j)$ for fixed $k$. If this is $o(M)$, then at every sufficiently late false scale, any mixed collection whose nonexceptional addresses have at most two occurrences, for some set of at most $h$ exceptions, has a proof meeting all but $1/(g+1)$ of its weight. Otherwise every fixing of those exceptions would preserve the gap and give a short inconsistent residual. Simplify a residual clause to a unit parity, a constant, or its original form. This argument never charges the total size of the original collection to the checker.

## The boundary at three answers

There are degree-two systems of signed copies of a *single* three-answer Boolean predicate, with constant gap, in which every unsatisfiable subset contains linearly many original tests. The predicate is
$$
 S(p,x,y)=[p=x+y]
$$
with ordinary integer addition of bits. Thus $S$ entails the affine equation $p=x\mathbin{\oplus}y$ and the clause forbidding $x=y=1$. Their conjunction recovers $S$. Counting bit occurrences before this expansion changes the theorem.

For arbitrarily large $N$, take a bipartite multigraph with $2N$ left vertices of degree three and $N$ right vertices of degree six. Require that every set of at most $\alpha N$ left vertices has at least its own size in distinct neighbors, for an absolute $\alpha>0$. To construct it, split the left side into two layers of $N$. For each layer, choose three independent uniform bijections onto the right side. For a left set of size $s$ and a fixed right set of size $s$, all its edges land in that set with probability at most $(s/N)^{3s}$, by sampling without replacement within each bijection. The union bound over choices is at most $(2e^2s/N)^s$. Summing from $1$ to $\sqrt N$ gives $o(1)$, and choosing $2e^2\alpha<1/2$ makes the tail $o(1)$ as well. This even excludes having only $s$ neighbors, which is stronger than needed. Parallel edges receive distinct bit names.

Give each incidence edge a mark bit. At each left vertex require exactly one of its three marks, which is the signed test $S(1-a,b,c)$. At each right vertex use five $S$ tests on the internal nodes of a binary tree with its six marks as leaves and a fresh free root bit. All five tests can be satisfied exactly when at most one leaf is marked. Each edge bit appears twice, each nonroot internal bit twice, and each root bit once. There are $7N$ tests.

Write $a$ and $b$ for the numbers of failed left and right tests. At least $2N-a$ marks are present. In each bin, sum the integer differences “left child plus right child minus parent” over the internal nodes. Internal marks cancel, leaving the number of marked leaves minus the root bit. Each failed split contributes at most two and each passed split contributes zero. Consequently the total marked leaves are at most $N+2b$, giving $N\le a+2b\le 2(a+b)$. The gap is at least $1/14$. In the other direction, any collection of at most $\alpha N$ tests contains at most $\alpha N$ left demands. Hall's theorem matches those demands into distinct right bins. Mark one matching edge per demand and zero all other edges. Every selected left test and **all** right tests can now be satisfied. Thus the minimum incompatible subset is linear.

This counterexample uses the graph pigeonhole principle and bounded-degree expansion. The statements about large cores concern a verifier that names individual seed occurrences. The counting inequality above is a short mathematical explanation of unsatisfiability for this family.
