# Most checksum queries are a constant distance from every different selector

For the [ordinary checksum 3-CNFs](sat-on-random-checksums.md), include a base variable pinned to zero for every padding position, so the table has exactly $V=2^d$ variables to be checksummed. The remaining checksum gates and optional header are as in the physical construction. This increases size by at most a constant factor in the number of table slots and preserves the assignment count. Use the equivalent trace form of the public selector over $K=\mathbb F_q$, $q=2^s$:
$$
 c_i(p,\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\lambda\prod_{k:i_k=1}p_k\prod_{k:i_k=0}(1-p_k)\right).
$$
The actual SAT verdict compares the binary inner product of this selector with the genuine table to a fair public pin. All distances below refer to **selector bits**, not to distances between SAT verdicts or encoded formula strings.

For $q$ sufficiently large, a uniform $(p,\lambda)\in K^d\times K$ has the following property except with probability
$$
 O\!\left(\frac{d+1}{q}+dq\,2^{-\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\right).
$$
Every other parameter tuple, including those with Boolean field coordinates, either gives the same selector or changes at least $V/64$ of its bits. In the equality case the other tuple is obtained by applying one field automorphism $a\mapsto a^{2^h}$ simultaneously to every coordinate and to $\lambda$. There are exactly $s$ such presentations of this selector. At the checksum field size $q=\operatorname{poly}(j)$ chosen there, $d=\Theta(j)$ and $q/d\to\infty$, so the exceptional probability tends to zero. The comparison is simultaneous over all alternative tuples, including an adversarial choice after inspecting the first one.

## Distribution of the field products

Call $p$ interior if no coordinate is zero or one. Discard non-interior tuples and zero masks at probability at most $(2d+1)/q$. Split address bits into two halves. On a uniform binary address in each half write $A,B$ for the products of interpolation factors in that half, absorbing $\lambda$ into $A$. Thus $c=\mathrm{Tr}(AB)$ with the two addresses independent. In each block, changing address bit $i$ from zero to one multiplies the product by $r_i=p_i/(1-p_i)$. For random interior parameters the $r_i$ are independent uniform on $G\setminus\{1\}$, where $G=K^*$.

With the stated exception probability, each block product is within $\nu=1/32$ in total variation of the uniform distribution on **all** of $K$. The same bound holds conditional on either value of any single address bit in that block. To see this, for a block with $m$ unfixed bits divide out its known scalar, leaving the product of a uniformly chosen subset of $m$ independent ratios. Its Fourier coefficient at a nontrivial complex multiplicative character $\chi$ is $\prod_i(1+\chi(r_i))/2$ up to a phase. Each factor has expected squared magnitude $(1-1/(q-2))/2\le1/2$. By Parseval on $G$ and Cauchy–Schwarz, the expected squared total variation from uniform on $G$ is at most $(q-2)2^{-m}/4$. Markov and a union bound over the blocks and deletions give the displayed exceptional probability using a fixed bound such as $1/64$ on $G$. A fixed nonzero scalar, even chosen as a function of the ratios, preserves the distance from uniform. Passing from $G$ to $K$ adds $1/q$. This proves the assertion for $q\ge64$. This product calculation is an instance of the collision-probability method for random hashing. [Impagliazzo, Levin and Luby](https://doi.org/10.1145/73007.73009)

## Recovering the trace pairing from approximate agreement

Fix good parameters, so the distribution bounds now hold deterministically. Denote the second tuple's block products by $A',B'$. If its mask is zero the second word is all zero. If it has a Boolean coordinate it is zero on at least one half of the address cube. On that half the honest output has probability at least $1/2-\nu$ of one: fix the address in the half-block containing the Boolean coordinate and use the other, nearly uniform, nonzero honest product. These cases exceed the claimed distance. Thus in seeking agreement within $1/64$ we may assume all alternate products nonzero as well.

For $a\in K$ write $w_a(y)=\mathrm{Tr}(aB(y))$ as a word on the second block's addresses, and similarly $w'_a(y)=\mathrm{Tr}(aB'(y))$. Distinct honest words have relative distance at least $\alpha=1/2-\nu$, by the nondegenerate field trace pairing. Both families are binary-linear in their labels. Assume overall relative disagreement $e<1/64$ and put $\tau=1/16$. At least $1-e/\tau$ of first-block addresses have their alternate row within distance $\tau$ of the true row. The corresponding honest row symbols represent a fraction at least $1-e/\tau-\nu>1/2$ of $K$, by near-uniformity. Each alternate symbol within $\tau$ of an honest word has a unique such honest symbol because $2\tau<\alpha$. Therefore the set $G_0$ of close alternate symbols also occupies more than half the field.

Every $z\in K$ is the sum of two elements of $G_0$: the set and its translate intersect. Adding the two corresponding word approximations puts $w'_z$ within $2\tau$ of an honest word $w_{D(z)}$. This label is unique since $4\tau<\alpha$. It agrees with the original nearest label on $G_0$. Moreover $D(z+t)=D(z)+D(t)$ since otherwise two different honest words would be at distance at most $6\tau<\alpha$. Its image contains more than half the field, so $D$ is a binary-linear isomorphism. This uses no distributional assumption on the alternate parameters.

Whenever $D(A')\ne A$, the alternate row incurs error at least $\alpha-2\tau$. Hence the fraction of first-block addresses where $D(A')\ne A$ is at most $b=e/(\alpha-2\tau)$. Write $T=D^{-1}$. For coordinate $i$ in the first block consider pairs of addresses differing only in bit $i$, starting at bit zero. The probability that either endpoint fails $A'=T(A)$ is at most $2b$ under a uniform such pair. At both good endpoints,
$$
                         T(r_i A)=r'_i T(A).
$$
This is equality of two binary-linear functions of the field argument $A$. Given bit zero, $A$ is within $\nu$ of uniform on $K$. Distinct linear functions agree on at most half of $K$, and $1-2b>1/2+\nu$. Thus the two functions coincide on every field element. Starting at any one good address, this identity propagates the relation $A'=T(A)$ along every cube edge in either direction. It holds at all addresses. Reversing the block roles gives another binary-linear isomorphism $U$ with $B'=U(B)$ at every second-block address.

If the two bilinear forms $\mathrm{Tr}(ab)$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(T(a)U(b))$ differ, their difference has a proper left kernel. A nearly uniform $A$ lies outside that kernel with probability at least $1/2-\nu$. Given such an $A$, a nearly uniform independent $B$ gives different bits with probability at least $1/2-\nu$. This would cost at least $(1/2-\nu)^2>1/64$. The forms are therefore identical, and all selector bits agree.

## Why only simultaneous Frobenius powers give equality

Normalize $T$ to $S(a)=T(a)/T(1)$. This map is binary-linear, bijective and unital. The elements $r$ for which $S(ra)=S(r)S(a)$ for every $a$ form a subfield, by additivity, multiplicativity and closure under inverses. Each first-block ratio belongs to it by the coordinate identities, and $S(r_i)=r'_i$. A proper subfield cannot contain all the ratios: the first-block products would be confined to its scalar multiple, which has uniform mass at most $1/2$, contrary to their near-uniform distribution. Hence $S$ is an automorphism of $K$. The same holds for the normalization $R$ of $U$. Let $k=T(1)U(1)$. Equality of the forms gives $\mathrm{Tr}(ab)=\mathrm{Tr}(kS(a)R(b))$ for all $a,b$. Setting $b=1$, invariance of trace and nondegeneracy force $k=1$. Varying $a$ then forces $R(b)=S(b)$ for every $b$. The inverse odds transform now yields $p'_i=S(p_i)$ in both blocks. The second block has no extra scalar and its products are all nonzero, so $U(1)=1$, hence $T(1)=1$ and $\lambda'=S(\lambda)$. Conversely Frobenius preserves the trace of every product and changes no bit. The ratios in one block generate $K$, so different automorphisms give distinct presentations. Identifiability of trilinear tensor decompositions has an extensive literature. This proof gives a distance guarantee for these particular binary trace selectors; priority of that formulation is unverified. [Kruskal on tensor identifiability](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-3795(77)90069-6)

## Consequences for an attempted proof of the SAT time lower bound

Consider an edge that claims a parity relation between two genuine checksum answers. Known public terminal pins can be removed. A certificate consists of selected clauses of the **same native base formula** whose conjunction implies the claimed relation on every table satisfying those clauses. The verifier may use the complete definitions of the two checksum circuits. Let $h$ be the genuine base assignment. For every coordinate where the two selectors differ, flipping that bit of $h$ reverses their parity relation. At least one selected base clause must be false on that flipped table, even when arbitrary checksum auxiliaries are recomputed. A clause true on $h$ can be falsified by a single-coordinate flip at at most one coordinate. The certificate thus needs at least the Hamming distance in base clauses. This applies even when its author knows $h$ and adaptively chooses the second selector and the clauses. [Critical-clause proof for one query](local-clauses-and-checksum-symbols.md)

Consequently, from a good query, **arbitrarily many successive edges each certified with fewer than $V/64$ base clauses reach only its identical selector**. Every descriptor in its Frobenius orbit has the same product-distribution bounds, also directly by the field automorphism. A short proof outside this clause-subset model could use global properties of the actual RAM program. Higher-arity relations also escape the argument: for any fixed $p$, the selectors for masks $\lambda,\mu,\lambda+\mu$ have bitwise XOR zero identically. This supplies a true three-query equation without base clauses but does not identify any of its three labels.

There is a small positive consequence. For a given good selector, $O((d+1)\log q)$ random bit positions distinguish it from all inequivalent descriptors with constant probability, by the distance bound and a union bound over $q^{d+1}$ descriptors. Even if a presented selector oracle has arbitrary corruption of relative weight at most $1/256$, the same sample order suffices: empirical distances simultaneously approximate all candidate distances to better than $1/256$ with high constant probability by increasing the absolute constant. Then a descriptor of sufficiently small empirical distance must be equivalent. For instance accept empirical error at most $1/128$; the true descriptor fits with high probability while each inequivalent one has expected error at least $3/256$. A test of guessed field parameters at these sampled positions costs $\operatorname{poly}(j)$. Under polynomial-time SAT an accepting descriptor can be found by Boolean search within polynomial time in $j$ with the sampled bits supplied. This recovers the public selector; the hidden linear checksum of the actual run is still unknown.

The full variable domain matters. If only $2^{d-1}+1$ positions are checksummed, varying the last field coordinate $t$ and replacing $\lambda$ by $\lambda(1-t)/(1-t')$ leaves the entire first half unchanged. The second half has only one slot. At interior parameters one can flip that remaining bit for $q\ge8$ by choosing $t'$: its new coefficient is the trace of a fixed nonzero scalar times $t'/(1-t')$, which takes both bits even excluding odds zero and one. Hence the constant-distance conclusion would be false for arbitrary omitted padding. The explicit padded base used here charges certificates for using the zero pins. A general solver may take advantage of the known encoding and padding directly. No bound on its time follows from the clause count.
