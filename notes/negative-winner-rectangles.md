# A verifier can miss an entire rectangle of false two-date claims

At an eligible start of the [two-date test](public-challenges-for-two-future-verdicts.md), one would like to exclude two negative winners. Consider the following specific event: rank $f$ wins now with the all-zero word, then rank $g$ wins on the next date with its all-zero word. A verifier may certify that this event is impossible. It gets the true history through the preceding date, the identities $f,g$, and the identity of a designated positive reporter $e$ that is active, ready and unretired. We can even promise that both named ranks really do win with zero at some date on or after the start. Soundness is required on every genuine eligible input satisfying that promise, on every accepted branch. Only the dates of the two wins are at issue.

For each fixed sound deterministic verifier, there are infinitely many **actual winning dates of positive reporters** with the following property. For arbitrarily large $m$ there are at least $m$ possible first ranks and $m$ possible second ranks, all distinct, still live and smaller than the positive winner. The verifier cannot exclude **any** of the $m^2$ ordered events on these dates within $2^i/(i+1)^B$ work, for a sufficiently large fixed $B$ and start date $i$. Every one of the named ranks eventually wins with zero strictly after this date. Under unrestricted deterministic $O(n^k)$ explicit SAT, $k=1,2,3$, the assertion extends to nondeterministic verifiers within $2^{\lfloor i/k\rfloor}/(i+1)^B$ branch work. Each group depends on the verifier, and in the nondeterministic version it can depend on the hypothesized solver.

There is nevertheless a **single new deterministic rule** that recognizes these groups and soundly excludes all their cross pairs in time polynomial in $i$. It works even before the positive reporter wins. Thus there are specific cheap certificates for arbitrarily large collections of real competing-rank exclusions. This also gives a strict extension of every sound deterministic polynomial-in-$i$ test. The new rule proves something about the old test's source code and uses its soundness. Its coverage of the remaining earlier programs is the unresolved issue.

## Two groups of ordinary programs

For group size $m\ge1$ give $m$ reporters the role of answering on the second date. Write $R$ for their ranks and $q=\max R$. Put $q+1$ first-date reporters in a set $L$, all with ranks strictly above $q$. Place a designated positive reporter $e$ above all of them. They are ordinary programs in the existing length-ordered word-reporter list. Distinct literal group and member tags keep the texts distinct. Each family can reproduce the text of its fixed template with literal arguments. Form the right texts first, read their ranks in list order, then pad the left texts beyond the longest right text, and finally pad the positive text beyond every member text. This also permits the independent programs below to be placed below $e$. Group size tends to infinity with the tag. All identities can be recovered by scanning and checking prefixes of the actual program list. A trial can bound such a scan by its current public stage and idle on an incomplete construction. No reporter emits before the whole group is activated.

Take the public start $J$ sufficiently late that $e<R_0(J)$ for the scheduler's activation bound $R_0$, $2^{e+1}\le J+2$, and the uniform search estimates below hold. For example take the first stage meeting those monotone conditions and a fixed startup threshold. A tag can recover that date by a scan polynomial in the current stage. The positive reporter offers a fixed public trace-positive word on every eligible date from $J$ until its win. It must win by $J+e$, since each earlier preemption retires a different smaller rank. Distinct tags have distinct winning dates. The other groups may operate concurrently. [Native scheduler](learning-from-earlier-sat-verdicts.md) · [Public start and deadline](winner-informed-positive-stages.md)

On each eligible start in this window, every live first reporter searches for the same lexicographically first pair $(f,g)\in L\times R$ of currently live ranks for which the verifier accepts the zero-zero exclusion at that start within its clock. All searches use the same genuine history, original ranks and designated tag. If a pair is found, only its first reporter $f$ offers zero. On the next date a right reporter reads the preceding winner. If it was a left reporter for this group and actually returned zero, reconstruct the previous start input from the true prefix and repeat the previous clocked selection. Only the selected $g$ offers zero in response to the actual win by the selected $f$. The first group stops its search after $e$ wins. Pending responses retain the whole next date, including $J+e+1$ if necessary. All other members of the right group idle on that date. Once the phase and its final possible response have passed, every remaining member of $L\cup R$ repeatedly offers zero until it wins.

The eventual-winner promise used by the verifier follows independently of its soundness. Each member either wins in the phase with zero, its only possible output, or offers zero at all sufficiently late stages until it wins. Finitely many lower indices can ever preempt a fixed program. At a start we search only on live members, so their genuine wins are still in the future or on the start date. A verifier given the *current* winners would have a different promise. ## Why all the indicated pairs escape the search

If $f$ wins on a search date, a particular $g$ was selected by a timely acceptance. It is still live on the successor date. Were $g$ to win there, both specified zero outputs would be genuine. The verifier would have falsely excluded exactly this event. Thus $g$ gets preempted by a rank strictly below $g$. No other right reporter in this group offered on that successor. In fact no right reporter can win at all during the response phase: each of its offers follows an actual selected first win. This argument uses the real second-stage priority outcome, not a hypothetical run on a modified history.

Each left win before $e$ wins therefore charges a different retiring rank in $\{0,\ldots,q-1\}\setminus R$. The $m-1$ remaining right ranks below $q$ cannot pay. There are at most $q+1-m$ charges, and previously retired ranks can only reduce the number. At the true positive win there are at least $(q+1)-(q+1-m)=m$ surviving left ranks and all $m$ right ranks. If the verifier accepted an exclusion for even one of these pairs at that start, the common search would find a pair and its selected first rank would offer ahead of $e$. This contradicts the actual win. All surviving pairs are false, and their named members really do win with zero at later dates.

The $q+1-m$ charge bound is sharp for the scheduling argument. In an abstract phase, choose a fresh left rank on each attempt and always select $g=q$. Allow that left rank to win, then let a fresh outside rank below $q$ win on the successor. Keep the remaining outside ranks idle. Each such claimed exclusion is true in this schedule. Any number of attempts from zero to $q+1-m$ can be realized this way before leaving the search empty and letting $e$ win. More specifically, if the proposed right ranks for a list of attempts are $g_1,\ldots,g_s$, the distinct successor preemptors must fit in the nested sets of available outside ranks below those $g_h$. In the abstract schedule with freely chosen outside offers and all left ranks above $q$, the prefix inequalities $\#\{h:g_h\le u\}\le\#\{b\text{ initially available outside }R:b<u\}$ for every proposed $u$ suffice as long as distinct left ranks are available. Sort the attempts by increasing $g_h$ to assign distinct preemptors greedily, then play the attempts in the originally prescribed order. This explains exactly what the rank count can certify without the program values.

The common search costs a fixed polynomial in $i$ times the verifier clock for a deterministic verifier, by enumerating rank pairs. For a nondeterministic one, use the [packed bounded-computation reduction](packed-band-transfer.md) and the hypothesized SAT program to test for a successful branch for each pair. The cost is at most $(T_i+\operatorname{poly}(i))^k\operatorname{poly}(i)$. Group construction, interpreting the verifier, handling its physical reads through the local generator, and reproducing the old prefix and word width on the successor cost polynomial factors in $i$. All group ranks are below $e$ and their individual budgets are at least $2^i/(i+2)$. A sufficiently large fixed $B$ leaves the uniform search cost below that cap, with time to emit. A fixed startup threshold accounts for the simulation constants. The group tags vary inside fixed templates; the polynomial exponents do not depend on $m$. Without a SAT assumption, one may also enumerate all witnesses of length at most $i-B\lceil\log_2(i+2)\rceil$ for a fixed polynomial-in-$i$ witness checker. This gives the same missing rectangle for those short witnesses. These estimates measure the native clock $2^i$. Converting a verifier for general RAM computations into one within that clock is still open.

## A cheap rule that proves every cross exclusion

A new verifier checks the phase, the group texts, the true history up to the start, the membership of $f$ and $g$, and that $e$ is ready and unretired. On a match it certifies the zero-zero exclusion; on other inputs it can fall back to the old verifier. It need never simulate the old search on the matched inputs. If a left reporter wins at this start, only its selected right reporter could offer on the next date. That reporter's win would falsify the old verifier's accepted exclusion. Thus no member of the right group can win there. The rule works at all these starts, including those at which some other pair of earlier programs really does produce two negatives. The syntactic check and stage arithmetic cost $\operatorname{poly}(i)$ work. Assuming the old test is sound is essential to the proof. Applying this construction to the extended test yields different groups.

In particular, in the deterministic polynomial-in-$i$ class the new verifier contains every previously accepted exclusion and accepts new ones on infinitely many genuine positive stages. For general capped tests the separation concerns timely acceptance. This comparison is about partial tests on a promised sequence with genuine history; it makes no assertion about simulation between full propositional proof systems. Related frameworks study resource-bounded genericity and time-efficient approximation of sets. Historical priority for this two-date construction is unverified. ## A single checker for all rules whose soundness has a short arithmetic proof

There is an unconditional version that reads evidence about the old programs. Fix a sound arithmetical theory $T$, for example Peano arithmetic with a conventional binary proof presentation, assuming its soundness in the standard model, whose proof checker runs in polynomial time. For program text $V$ let $S(V)$ be the arithmetic sentence that whenever the input is genuine, the promise holds, and $V$ accepts, the stated zero-zero event fails. Quantify over all dates, rank inputs, and finite accepting computation records. History and retirement are arithmetically definable from the fixed computable scheduler. The promise that both ranks eventually win with zero can itself be expressed by two finite witnessing computations. Use one fixed formula with the program code substituted as a binary numeral, so printing $S(V)$ is polynomial in the code length. We use standard truth of the sentences proved by $T$.

Use a common version of the deterministic cohort templates across all program texts, with $V$ a literal data field. Run each candidate call for at most $2^{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}$ simulated steps. This catches every fixed polynomial-in-$i$ verifier eventually. Uniform simulation, enumerating candidate pairs, and recognizing the canonical tags cost a polynomial in $i$ factor. In the tag-recognition procedure cap all generation and rank scans by the current active prefix, reject malformed or overlong codes, and use stage guards as above. Reject out-of-range rank fields and malformed or overlong history records before doing the search, with a fixed polynomial-in-$i$ input-access cap. Checking group membership treats the embedded $V$ as a string. Neither running $V$ nor testing its semantic soundness is part of the syntax check. Use a common fixed startup constant for the universal bounded interpreter.

Define one deterministic test $U_T$ on a proposed exclusion. It can accept by either check below, in time polynomial in $i$.

* If the ranks belong to a recognized active cross pair of the template for $V$, search the binary strings of length at most $\lfloor\log_2(i+2)\rfloor$ for a $T$-proof of $S(V)$. Accept if one checks. This invokes the cross-pair rule just proved.
* On arbitrary rank pairs, enumerate program/proof records of that same bounded length. For each proof of $S(D)$ for the included program $D$, run $D$ on this input for at most $(i+2)^d$ steps, for any one fixed $d\ge1$. Accept any genuine acceptance within that simulation. This includes fixed, provably sound rules about unrelated earlier programs once the date is large enough to contain their proof and their run fits this clock.

The enumeration has $O(i+1)$ strings. Both checks are uniformly sound. In the first, $T$ proves soundness of the embedded program, and every responding right rank would have had to complete the same selected search as the preceding left winner. In the second, the proved assertion applies directly to the simulated program. These arguments also apply when a simulation happens to use the text of $U_T$: its bounded simulation defines an ordinary finite computation, and the basis for accepting its soundness would have to be an actual $T$-proof.

In fact $S(U_T)$ is true and $T$ has no proof of it. Were there such a proof, its fixed length would fit the enumeration at every sufficiently late stage. Apply the preceding rectangle theorem to the actual cohorts containing the text of $U_T$ and to their genuine positive wins. Its runtime is polynomial in $i$, below the half-native cutoff. The first branch of $U_T$ would accept on every surviving pair there, while the search and the positive win force it to accept on none. This is an incompleteness argument for a particular partial winner test. It does not separate SAT time bounds or give independence of a SAT time bound from $T$. The extension to arithmetic proofs gives a way to include more rules in the verifier while exhibiting a precise remaining gap in that approach. ## Genuine runs with two negative winners in the very same window

To test whether this rule could cover the whole window just by counting ranks, include another reporter $b$ below $e$ and $b+1$ additional reporters distinct from all earlier group members, ranked above $b$ and below $e$. The latter reporters all offer zero throughout the window until retirement. Reporter $b$ offers zero only on a date directly following the win of one of those $b+1$ reporters, with the same guard on the final successor. If $b$ is still live after each such win, either it wins on the successor or a distinct rank below $b$ retires. All $b+1$ first reporters must retire before $e$ can win, since they are ready and lower. There are only $b$ smaller ranks to spend. Therefore $b$ really wins immediately after one of these reporters, both with zero and before the positive win. Neither rank's offer needs a claimed proof. The construction permits $m$ disjoint such groups below $e$, forming them successively, so the same window contains at least $m$ disjoint genuine double-negative pairs. Group size and startup can grow freely here. This inserts ordinary reporter texts; all verdicts come from the original scheduler and its explicit SAT encodings.

The reflected test can remove an unbounded rectangle of false edges without replay on these inputs. A route to the SAT lower bound would need a uniformly sound test covering the remaining possibilities to the required extent. In particular, a fixed test whose unexcluded zero-zero graph is free of a fixed $K$-by-$K$ complete bipartite graph on **every** eligible positive-tag win of this construction, for every group size, would already contradict the corresponding clock under the fast-SAT assumption. The ordinary two-date criterion asks for coverage on infinitely many eligible starts and on all possible negative words. The rectangle result concerns a verifier-dependent subsequence and the single genuine zero choice. It does not preclude successes of the old test elsewhere.
