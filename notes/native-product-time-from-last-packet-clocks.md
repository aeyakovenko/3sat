# Native multiplication time and the last packet of a signed matrix file

For a fixed deterministic word RAM, let $M(d)$ bound the time to output the explicit product of two binary $d$-square matrices over $\mathbb F_2$. Use the logarithmic-word model with a sufficiently large fixed width coefficient and unrestricted workspace. For any regular budget $F(d)\ge d^2$ with $F(2d)=O(F(d))$, the following are equivalent.

* Some uniform deterministic RAM computes these products in $O(F(d))$ steps.
* Some globally correct deterministic SAT RAM takes $O(F(d))$ steps on **every permutation** of every signed product-entry file described below and takes $O(1)$ steps after the last packet load on all of them.
* Some globally correct deterministic SAT RAM has, for every pair of factors, $a+\sum_{i,j}\min(b_{ij,0},b_{ij,1})=O(F(d))$ in the deterministic last-discovery experiment. Here $a$ is the common prefix cost and the two $b$'s are the actual suffix clocks on the opposite signed files.

In particular $F(d)=d^2$ asks for linear time in the encoded bit length on this family under a constant-finish constraint. The implication from the final condition to multiplication is the [existing race of actual continuations](literal-profiles-at-the-last-clause.md). The reverse direction has to handle a missing **data** packet as well as an unseen query. A second consequence below shows why even small expected clocks on every fixed file admit a different interpretation for randomized solvers.

## Identifying the missing packet

Take the [compact entry-selector encoding](compact-bit-projection-of-matrix-mismatch.md) for power-of-two $d$, with the signed row flags and full column flags of the [last-discovery reduction](literal-profiles-at-the-last-clause.md). Use one packet per data or fixed circuit clause and one packet for the two query pins. The packets are interchangeable in the SAT file. Each baseline packet has a second, always-true record consisting of the three negated false anchors. For an explicit layout, order its three literals one way for fixed clauses and another way for matrix chunks. These orders use identical counts and names and give disjoint syntactic formats. Use a fixed order within each first record and fixed-width decimal fields. The query's second record is a column pin. The header and anchor block are public. The power-of-two dimension can be recovered by padding to $N=K d^2$ bits for a sufficiently large byte-aligned constant $K$. For a general product pad its inputs to the next power of two. Requiring the two pins to select ordinary row and column indices remains part of validating the encoding.

Write $p$ for the number of baseline packets and $w=\Theta(\log d)$ for the native width. The $A$ packets are $b$-bit chunks of a column indexed by consecutive rows. The $B$ packets are $b$-bit chunks of a row indexed by consecutive columns. Here $b=\Theta(\log d)$, and $p=\Theta(d^2/\log d)$. The chunk ID consists of a bank and a selector-group ID. The changing ASCII mask digits are the final $b$ characters of the middle, right-aligned identifier in the first record. Mask their low bits to obtain a public word key for the chunk ID. Check every other bit by exact comparison with its canonical word. Restore a missing occurrence of a fixed packet by accounting for multiplicities of identical keys.

The canonical decoder can be built in $O(p)$ steps. All fixed clauses and public names together number $O(d10^b+db2^b+d)$. With the compact choice $b\le (\log_2 d)/8$ these can be constructed, formatted and counted even with several logarithmic factors in $o(p)$ steps. Pack each public identifier field in advance. For each of the $O(p)$ zero-mask chunk packets assemble its word from a fixed number of prepacked fields. Split a native packet key into pieces of at most $\frac14\log_2 N$ bits. A fixed-depth radix tree identifies the key in constant time. For each new node reserve a fresh block of at most $N^{1/4}$ zero-default child slots by advancing a counter, writing only used pointers. There are $O(p)$ nodes and $O(p N^{1/4})$ reserved addresses inside the sufficiently large native address space. This uses no initialization proportional to a block size and keeps the physical packet width unchanged. Compression of the low ASCII lanes to a packed mask, together with checking and decoding the two query pins, takes $O(1)$ per word using fixed piece tables of size $o(p)$ built by subdividing into pieces of at most $(\log_2 N)/4$ bits. Padding and the public region can also be checked in one packed scan. [Aligned fixed-width parsing](literal-profiles-at-the-last-clause.md)

## A solver for every physical ordering

Scan all packets except one fixed physical word, checking types, IDs, multiplicities and punctuation. If the prefix cannot be completed canonically, run a complete SAT procedure on the original file. Otherwise there are two cases.

1. **No query was present.** All factor chunks are available and every baseline occurrence has been checked. Compute $AB$ in $M(d)$ steps after at most $O(d^2)$ steps to transcribe its inputs. Prepare a directly indexed answer map on signed query words, or use the constant-time two-field decoder. The last load identifies an entry and a tag.
2. **The query was present.** Exactly one occurrence is missing. If it is a fixed clause, its expected word is known, and the two requested factor vectors are available. If the missing occurrence is a chunk, the scanned IDs identify its required bank and group. Compute the requested dot product with this chunk zero-filled. For a missing $A$ chunk in column $h$, the correction is zero unless the requested row $i$ lies in its group, and otherwise equals $A_{ih}B_{hj}$. Thus there is only one unresolved bit, multiplied by a known bit. For a missing $B$ chunk use $A_{ih}B_{hj}$ with the other factor known. The fixed-clause case has zero correction. Obtaining that dot product takes $O(d)$ from the packed chunk arrays, irrespective of when the query was encountered.

After the final original load, validate the remaining packet against the expected type and missing ID, apply the one-bit correction or table lookup, and compare with the query's sign. The work after the load is $O(1)$ on the entire template. A failed final check sends an arbitrary other input to the complete fallback. On the missing-query branch the total clock is $O(d^2+M(d))$; on the entire missing-data branch it is $O(p+d)=O(p)$. These are worst-case bounds within each branch. In particular one need not precompute a set of products for all repairs to a missing factor. The tagged inputs always force a genuine query discovery in the last-discovery experiment, including on RAMs with writable input. The race lemma then supplies the converse with $O(d^2+a+\sum\min b)$ total work. All three conditions are assertions about one uniform algorithm across dimensions.

## Most orders can be cheap even on the hardest product

On a uniformly random permutation of the $p+1$ packets for **any fixed** $A,B,i,j,e$, the full-product branch has probability $1/(p+1)$. Even schoolbook multiplication gives expected total time $O(p+(d^2+d^3)/(p+1))=O(p)$, and the finish is constant on every permutation. This average is sharp up to constants. The two sign choices force each correct deterministic solver to read the query's original word. Until it finds the query, the remaining packet locations are exchangeable given all its observations. Its rank among distinct discoveries is therefore uniform on $1,\ldots,p+1$. A blind overwrite of an unseen word at this stage would lose the query in a completion consistent with that history and cannot occur. Padding and nonpacket probes cannot improve the expected number of packet loads.

Randomizing only which physical packet to leave until last gives the same expected total on **each individual file**, with a constant finish on every coin outcome. Sample the location before the scan by rejection. This takes $O(1)$ expected random-word operations, or $O(\log(p+1))$ expected fair-bit operations, within the total scan bound. There is also a solver with $O(p)$ total time for every order that computes just one dot product in $O(d)$ after a last-position query and has the constant finish whenever baseline data is last. Randomize its last location and its expected suffix is $O(1+d/(p+1))=O(1)$ on each file; a single uniform native word reduced to an almost-uniform location suffices for a strict worst-case sampling clock (map to the next power-of-two range and subtract $p+1$ from out-of-range samples to give at most two preimages per location). In the adversarial placement experiment the query is last on **every** run. Fixed-file mean clocks cannot be substituted into that replay estimate by simply averaging over random seeds.

## How many fast executions suffice?

For any source dimension $d$, including non-powers via zero padding, draw $A,B$ independently uniformly over binary $d$-square matrices, then choose $i,j$ uniformly from the original $d$ rows and columns. Suppose a fixed deterministic solver satisfies
$$
 \Pr[a(A,B)\le U(d),\ \min_e b_{ij,e}\le h(d)]\ge \alpha
$$
for a fixed $\alpha>0$ and all sufficiently large $d$, with computable time caps available at the stated cost. Cap the prefix at $U$ and both suffixes at $h$. Keep only entries whose race finishes. These are exact, and the uniform expected fraction retained is at least $\alpha$. Filling erasures with independent fair bits gives expected agreement at least $1/2+\alpha/2$ in worst-case time $O(d^2+U+d^2h)$. The optimal error-correcting reduction of Hirahara and Shimizu therefore gives a randomized algorithm for every input product within a polylogarithmic factor of this time and with success probability at least $2/3$, for standard regular time bounds. Their theorem does the worst-case amplification; the conversion of a capped SAT execution to certified retained entries is the application here. [Hirahara and Shimizu, Theorem 1](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.ICALP.2025.97) · [Shinkar and Singh, including the erasure formulation](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/entities/document/10.4230/LIPIcs.APPROX-RANDOM.2025.29)

An observable erasure gives a stronger guarantee for these capped SAT races. A rank-one random walk recovers the whole product with zero error on every input, in expected time $O((T_{\rm mean}+d^2)\log(d+2)/\alpha)$, where $T_{\rm mean}$ is the partial procedure's mean actual work on uniform factors. With all entries returned in finite mean time, four masks suffice in expected $O(T_{\rm mean}+d^2)$. [Proof, mean clock accounting and relationship to the published rank-one walk](certified-matrix-entries-from-average-sat-clocks.md)

For $\alpha>3/4$ an elementary version gives **zero error** in expected $O_\alpha((d^2+U+d^2h)\log(d+2))$ time. Choose uniform independent matrices $R,S$. The sum of the four products of pairs in $\{A+R,R\}\times\{B+S,S\}$ is $AB$. Before each capped run independently apply random cyclic row and column shifts to its factors, undoing the shifts on its returned matrix. Each fixed target entry now corresponds to a uniform query index independent of the uniformly random factors in that run. The chance of any erasure at that target in the four terms is at most $4(1-\alpha)<1$. Save the recovered exact entries and repeat independently until every coordinate has been recovered. A union bound over the $d^2$ geometric tails gives the expected logarithmic round count. This special case needs no deterministic test of a candidate product. The distributional condition concerns the adversarially placed query on each factor pair. Randomly shuffled complete files in the preceding section have different clocks.
