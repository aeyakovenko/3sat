# How much can chance help in finding two formulas with the same verdict?

For every stage $j$ there is a family of explicit signed 3-CNFs indexed by $x\in\mathbb F_2^h$, where $h=\lceil\log_2(j+2)\rceil$ and $q=2^h$. Their SAT answers have the form $a_j\cdot x$ for a hidden word $a_j$. The family is fixed independently of any hypothesized solver. Two different labels $x,y$ have the same answer exactly when $a_j\cdot(x+y)=0$. Any nonzero predicted difference can be realized by the pair $(0,x+y)$. The certificate sought below may simply rule out **one proposed word** $u$ as the value of $a_j$. On that certificate's own terms there are $q-1$ other possibilities. It need not specify the verdict of any named formula.

For every fixed deterministic program $D$ that soundly rules out a word, every sufficiently late successful stage needs $\Omega_D(2^j)$ steps. The threshold may depend on $D$. Consequently for $k=1,2,3$, a sound ordinary nondeterministic verifier that rules out even one word within $2^{\lfloor j/k\rfloor}/(j+1)^B$ steps on infinitely many stages would exclude an unrestricted deterministic $O(n^k)$ SAT algorithm, for sufficiently large fixed $B$. There is a simple family of genuine, normally true exclusions: every specified nonzero word is excluded on every default stage, and the number of nondefault stages through $J$ is $O(\log\log(J+4))$. Their truth frequency by itself gives no sound signal on an individual stage.

## The native program and its formula inputs

Use the normalized priority interpreter, sparse RAM bank simulation and geometric trial clocks of the [signed priority construction](priority-pinned-sat.md). Enumerate deterministic reporters in computable program order. At stage $i$ activate its first $a(i)=\lfloor\log_2(2+\lfloor\log_2(i+4)\rfloor)\rfloor$ ranks. An active unretired rank $e$ gets at most $\lfloor2^i/2^{e+1}\rfloor$ universal operations on the zero input and the stage metadata. A report consists of an $h_i$-bit value, including zero, with a separate signal flag. At the first timely well-formed report, output its value and retire that rank. Otherwise output the zero vector. Replaying all stages through $j$ costs $O(2^j)$. Only earlier winning indices need persist between stages. A report is always produced by the reporter itself; no external claim is treated as true.

A reporter which signals only words different from $a_i$ can never win. After activation and the finitely many wins at lower indices, every timely report by it would win. The fixed interpreter gives every fixed program a positive constant fraction of the native clock for sufficiently large $i$. This proves the native bound, including for a partial program that signals on only some stages. Replay followed by flipping one bit of $a_j$ matches the bound. Each nonzero stage uses a different retired rank. For each fixed computable $h_j$-bit pattern available in $o(2^j)$ time, $a_j$ agrees with it infinitely often: eventual disagreement would make that pattern an inexpensive sound exclusion after a finite threshold.

To realize the promised physical formulas, run this deterministic program on a virtual input of $M=2^j$ bits with label $x$ in a fixed short header and zero elsewhere. The simulated reporters always get the **canonical zero input**, independently of $x$. The final machine accepts exactly when $a_j\cdot x=1$. Compile its clocked run using the ordinary packed 3-CNF compiler. Padding gives common encoded length $\Theta(M(j+1)^3)$ per member, with the same public layout. Individual formula words are accessible from $j,x$ and their address after polynomial-in-$j$ setup by the local compiler. When the formulas are queried through an indexed family, a fixed polynomial-in-$j$ overhead suffices for virtual accesses; the exclusion lower bound itself measures the reporter on stage metadata. Supplying all $q$ formulas concatenated changes the total physical length. [Compiler and word costs](packed-band-transfer.md)

Under an $O(n^k)$ SAT hypothesis, a fixed deterministic reporter can query whether there exist a word $u$ and an accepting computation of the proposed sound verifier within $R=\lfloor 2^{\lfloor j/k\rfloor}/(j+1)^B\rfloor$ steps. Recover the $h$ bits by prefix queries with the same cap. Encoding each bounded computation uses $(R+\operatorname{poly}(j))j^{O(1)}$ explicit bits, including address and word-width adaptation. The fixed polynomial number of queries costs $o(M)$ for sufficiently large fixed $B$. No unproved advice about which word to exclude enters this reporter. Applying the native argument proves the certificate criterion.

## Accurate forecasts of the word on its rarest recognizable values

There is a single deterministic $o(2^j)$-time forecast of the **entire** $a_j$ with asymptotically full precision and recall simultaneously for every fixed polynomial-time name $f(j)$ of an $h_j$-bit value. In particular this holds separately for each of the $h_j$-bit encodings of any fixed positive integer at sufficiently large stages. These genuine successes can coexist with the lower bound for certifying one exclusion.

Use the [truncated replay](parity-of-native-histories.md#a-sublinear-time-forecast-that-recovers-almost-every-real-rare-award) on this reporter scheduler. With nondecreasing cutoffs $e(j),d(j)$, fully replay through $j-d(j)$ and then skip ranks smaller than $e(j)$. When no skipped rank actually wins in the last $d(j)$ stages the whole reported word and the retirement set are exact. Thus at most $e(J)d(J)$ of the word forecasts through $J$ differ at all. All $q$ predicted SAT answers at an exact stage agree simultaneously.

To choose the cutoffs, effectively list all polynomial-clocked pattern programs with explicit clocks, clipping or padding malformed outputs to a public $h_i$-bit word. Every listed pattern is hit infinitely often by the diagonal argument. A single ordinary computable search can find, successively for $m=1,2,\dots$, a horizon $H_m$ by which each of the first $m$ patterns has been hit at least $m$ times. Use increasing horizons, scanning actual stages and running the clocked pattern programs. This search need not be fast. On input $J$ run its first $J$ elementary steps. Take the largest completed $m$ with $H_m\le J$, or zero if none. The result $K(J)$ is nondecreasing, unbounded and obtainable in $\operatorname{poly}(J)$ time. Each fixed pattern is therefore hit at least $K(J)$ times through $J$ eventually. Choose $e=d=\max(1,\lfloor K(J)^{1/3}\rfloor)$, with harmless early clamping. Both cutoffs diverge and the runtime is $o(2^j)$. For every fixed pattern, its false positive and false negative counts are each at most $O(1+K(J)^{2/3})$, negligible compared with its genuine count. This is a simultaneous guarantee even for many different rare words; it supplies no authentication at a chosen stage. For each fixed pattern both kinds of mistakes recur infinitely often. Eventual absence of false negatives would let the program soundly exclude $f(j)$ whenever the forecast differs from $f(j)$, which happens infinitely often by the result for the distinct pattern obtained by flipping its first bit. Eventual absence of false positives would let it exclude that flipped word whenever the forecast equals $f(j)$. Both reporters would violate the native bound after a finite hardwired threshold.

## The exact amount of information in an equal-pair forecast

For a distribution $\mu$ on nonzero differences $y$ write $p_\mu(a)=\Pr_{y\sim\mu}[a\cdot y=0]$. With no information about the hidden word the best guaranteed probability is
$$
 p_*=(q/2-1)/(q-1).
$$
Uniform nonzero differences attain it, because every nonzero word has $q/2-1$ orthogonal nonzero differences. Conversely averaging over the $q-1$ possible nonzero words gives exactly $p_*$ for **every** distribution. The zero word always succeeds.

If one specified nonzero word $b$ is excluded, draw $y$ uniformly from $b\cdot y=1$. For every nonzero $a\ne b$, the two linear forms are independent, giving success exactly $1/2$. This is optimal given only this exclusion for $h\ge2$. More generally let $E$ be the set of excluded nonzero words. If $E$ is nonempty but spans a proper subspace $U$, averaging $p_\mu(a)$ over $a\notin U$ gives at most $1/2$. For each fixed $y\ne0$, the fraction of orthogonal words outside $U$ is $1/2$ if $y\notin U^\perp$, and $(q/2-|U|)/(q-|U|)$ otherwise. Thus even many exclusions confined to a proper subspace cannot guarantee a strict majority.

If the excluded words contain a basis, a strict majority becomes possible, and the optimal guarantee when **just that basis** is excluded is exactly
$$
 \frac12+\frac{1}{2W},\qquad W=1+(h-2)2^{h-1},\qquad h\ge2.
$$
In coordinates for that basis, pair with dual coordinates on the difference side. Assign difference $y\ne0$ probability $(|y|-1)/W$, where $|y|$ counts nonzero coordinates. The nonnegative weights sum to one. For every allowed nonzero word $a$ (weight at least two), the full-cube signed sum of $(|y|-1)(-1)^{a\cdot y}$ is zero by orthogonality of the constant and coordinate functions to that character. Removing $y=0$ adds one. Consequently $2p_\mu(a)-1=1/W$. For the upper bound use the same weight distribution on hidden words of weight at least two. For every nonzero $y$ of weight at least two the same character calculation gives $1/W$, while for a unit $y$ the signed expectation is $(1-q/2)/W\le1/W$. Averaging over the hidden words proves the upper bound against every forecast. At $h=2$, excluding a basis leaves just one possible nonzero word; the guarantee is one. With any spanning $E$, the basis strategy still gives at least the displayed bound. Together these calculations characterize exactly when a strict majority can be guaranteed. They are statements about worst-case play given only the specified exclusions. They do not certify those exclusions for the actual $a_j$.

There is also an exact test for a *random* proposed exclusion, even if it sometimes fails. Let $B$ be a sampled nonzero word. After choosing it, sample $y$ uniformly subject to $B\cdot y=1$. At an actual nonzero $a_j$, the equal-pair success probability is
$$
 \frac{1-\Pr[B=a_j]}2.
$$
The contribution vanishes on a correct guess of the hidden value and equals one half on every other choice. Thus blind proposals, which already give a false candidate with probability $1-1/(q-1)$, buy exactly zero improvement over $p_*$. A sampler whose collision probability with the actual word is smaller than $1/(q-1)$ by a reliable inverse-polynomial gap would suffice in the randomized clock range below. This asks for a distributional improvement without requiring each proposed exclusion to be correct. On $a_j=0$ the second-stage sampler always succeeds.

## Converting a weak randomized prediction into an excluded word

Suppose a fixed clocked randomized algorithm produces a nonzero difference using only $j$, the virtual formulas and its random bits. Write $p_j(a)$ for its equality probability against a candidate word $a$. A guarantee $p_j(a_j)\ge p_*+\gamma_j$ at all stages where the algorithm is invoked gives a way to rule out one word. Some nonzero $a$ has $p_j(a)\le p_*$. Estimate each $p_j(a)$ to additive error less than $\gamma_j/3$ and minimize the estimates. The resulting word has $p_j(a)<p_j(a_j)$, so is an actual exclusion. If the success guarantee is at least $1/2+\gamma_j$, one can recover a whole basis of excluded words. At each step minimize an estimate over words outside the span already recovered. The proper-subspace average bound guarantees a candidate with true success at most $1/2$. All inputs and formula words used by the sampler are publicly generated even in these counterfactual score computations. Only the final comparison uses the sound mathematical guarantee about $a_j$.

In particular, if $T$ is a worst-case clock and $r$ is the number of random bits, exhaustive enumeration of the tapes and all $q=O(j)$ candidates gives a deterministic exclusion in $2^r(T+1)\operatorname{poly}(j)$ native steps. With the exact scores **any strict advantage** suffices. A known exceptional finite initial segment can be hardwired. Because almost all stages are default, mere success on an unspecified infinite set would be far weaker than the required guarantee. One may instead use a recognizably eligible infinite set so long as recognizing it fits the clock and the guarantee holds whenever the procedure reports.

Here is a deterministic bound under the $O(n^k)$ SAT hypothesis when the sampler uses many coins. Let the constructible clock $T\ge j$ have $\log(T+2)=O(j)$ and let $\gamma_j\ge(j+1)^{-d}$ for fixed $d$, with a known rational lower bound. A fixed word program consumes at most $O(Tj)$ random bits. For a proposed threshold $\tau$ distinguish $p_j(a)\le\tau$ from $p_j(a)\ge\tau+\eta$ where $\eta$ is a small fixed fraction of the known gap. Repeating independently $O(\eta^{-2}(\log(T+2)+\log(j+2)))$ times and testing the empirical mean against $\tau+\eta/2$ gives error $\epsilon$ at most $(T+j+2)^{-C}$ for an arbitrarily large fixed $C$. Call the new predicate high on acceptance, and let $R'$ be its coin length. Its time and coin length are $(T+1)\operatorname{poly}(j)$. Take $m=R'+2$ translates of the high set of random tapes. In the high case there exist $m$ shifts that cover every tape: the expected number uncovered by independent shifts is $2^{R'}\epsilon^m<1$. In the low case their union has measure at most $m\epsilon<1$, choosing $C$ large compared with the fixed amplification costs. Guess the shifts, universally choose one tape, and test all shifts deterministically. This two-block test has clock $(T+1)^2\operatorname{poly}(j)$ including data access. In the intermediate range either answer is safe. A polynomial-in-$j$ threshold grid with spacing $\eta$ gives estimates within $2\eta$, even when gap answers are inconsistent: take the largest threshold accepted (default zero), with endpoints clipped to $[0,1]$. Two bounded-run reductions and two applications of the hypothesized solver give total time $(T+1)^{2k^2}\operatorname{poly}(j)$ for all $q$ candidates. With a separately known bound $r$ on consumed random bits, the shifts take $(r+1)^2\operatorname{poly}(j)$ bits and all the deterministic checks take $(r+1)(T+1)\operatorname{poly}(j)$ work when $r=O(Tj)$. This gives the sharper total $((r+1)(T+1))^{k^2}\operatorname{poly}(j)$. This also gives a sound test for identifying a basis at a strict-majority guarantee. [Lautemann's covering argument](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0020019083900443)

For a sharper dependence on $T$, use the uniform three-block simulation in Theorem 3 of Viola's paper. Its first block supplies a short seed for a hitting generator of shifts, the second supplies a random-tape string, and the third chooses a shift index. The chosen shift is computable in quasilinear time in the amplified tape length. On that shifted tape the checker evaluates the amplified sampler. It can use independent logarithmically many repetitions for the theorem's final error reduction. In our application the sampler on a specified tape is the deterministic RAM itself. All these evaluations and the shift construction cost $(T+1)\operatorname{poly}(j)$ per branch after inverse-polynomial-gap amplification to constant error. All local overheads and the threshold grid are polynomial in $j$ at these clocks. The resulting alternative cost is $(T+1)^{k^3}\operatorname{poly}(j)$. The better of the two simulations has exponent $\min(2k^2,k^3)$. At $k=1$ this permits $T$ as large as $M/(j+1)^B$ with a sufficiently large fixed $B$. At $k=2,3$ the elementary two-block estimate gives exponents $8,18$. None of these simulations places a workspace restriction on the supposed ordinary SAT solver. [Viola, Theorem 3 and Lemma 14](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~viola/papers/BPvsE.pdf)

Thus even an inverse-polynomial-in-$j$ improvement over blind pair selection at a reliably certified sequence of stages gives a further route to the unrestricted question when its clock meets the applicable budget. One actual positive procedure is the uniform nonzero-difference forecast itself (sample by rejection or use a uniform index among the $q-1$ choices). Its success at every nondefault scale is exactly $p_*$. For fixed-bit practical implementations a distribution within $2^{-b}$ total variation can be produced with $O(h+b)$ coins by reduction modulo $q-1$ of a long uniform integer. The theorem leaves open an actual procedure gaining any certified advantage at the required speed, or a short sound proof excluding one proposed word. Finite-extension diagonalization has a substantial history. Priority for the combination of these explicit formula promises, forecast bounds and cost is unverified.
