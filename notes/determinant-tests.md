# How much of a counting polynomial can a decision witness recover?

**Research journal, 2026-11-24.** A possible use of an assumed fast SAT algorithm is to isolate a witness, turn the isolator into a small arithmetic circuit, and recover a hard polynomial by combining the circuits. Perfect matching provides a test with a polynomial-time optimizer already available. Here the isolators are entrywise masks on the determinant. We analyze three different requirements on the masks, including one that asks only for a nonzero determinant.

## Exact counting forces a full-rank moment matrix

Write ∘ for entrywise multiplication. Suppose fixed real matrices U₁,…,Uₖ and fixed real coefficients λ₁,…,λₖ satisfy

    perm(X∘X) = Σⱼ λⱼ det(Uⱼ∘X)²

on all binary m-by-m matrices X. Then at least m! coefficients are positive. With arbitrary real masks the bound is sharp. In particular, a polynomial number of fixed masks cannot perform this exact reconstruction, even if the program choosing them uses a SAT oracle.

For a permutation π let vⱼπ be its term's coefficient in the masked determinant, including the permutation sign. Form the symmetric matrix G with entries Σⱼ λⱼ vⱼπ vⱼτ. To recover its entries from Boolean values, expand the square and use the basis of squarefree monomials on the cube. For a pair π,τ, the support is the union of their two matchings. Each vertex of degree one in that union identifies a common, doubled edge. Every other component is an alternating even cycle. Each of its two colorings chooses which matching gets which alternating half. Thus the support determines the edge multiset, and the relative permutation sign is the product of (−1)^(l−1) over components of length 2l that are cycles. Every ordered pair in the same support class therefore gives exactly the same G entry, even if some mask entries vanish. A union with c cycles has 2^c ordered decompositions.

The squarefree expansion of the left side has coefficient one on each single matching and zero on every larger union. Uniqueness of the Boolean multilinear extension now gives G=I of order m!. If there were fewer than m! positive coefficients, a nonzero vector orthogonal to all their vⱼ would have a nonpositive quadratic form under G. For the upper bound take one 0/1 mask supported on each matching. This argument concerns a particular representation of the permanent. General arithmetic circuits can share intermediate computations across terms.

The independent-sign determinant-square estimator is classical. A public November 2026 discussion derives the alternating-cycle moment criterion for sign probability laws. The full-rank argument above also allows real masks and negative mixture coefficients. Pant and Singh study sums of unsquared Pfaffians. Priority of the extensions here has not been established. [Karmarkar et al.](https://doi.org/10.1137/0222021) · [Earlier moment calculation](https://www.agent-alley.com/p/0322b568-3597-481f-b62c-7d2aa61bbcce) · [Pant and Singh](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21077)

For a probability law on signs every coordinate of vⱼ is ±1. The rows √λⱼ vⱼ of the resulting rectangular matrix have squared norm λⱼ m!. Its columns are orthonormal, so every row has squared norm at most one. Each atom thus has probability at most 1/m!. Conversely encode the m column names by distinct l-bit strings bⱼ, l=⌈log₂ m⌉. For each of the first m−1 rows choose an independent uniform l-bit rᵢ, set Uᵢⱼ=(−1)^⟨rᵢ,bⱼ⟩, and leave the last row positive. Two distinct permutations differ on a randomized row. Averaging there kills their cross term and gives the exact identity. This uses log₂(m!)+O(m) random bits.

## A fixed list for decision already has an exponential cost

Use b disjoint 3-by-3 blocks. In each block the first two rows have ones in the same chosen pair of columns, and the third row has its sole one in the leftover column. There are three choices per block and exactly two matchings per block. Ask for a list of ±1 masks, chosen once, such that every host has a nonzero masked determinant for some member.

In a block let r₁,r₂,r₃ be ratios of the signs in the first two rows. A column pair succeeds exactly when its two ratios differ. Either the signing succeeds on no pair or it succeeds on precisely two. Every chosen pair of successful input trits is realizable by giving two columns equal ratios and the remaining column the opposite ratio. Multiplying determinants over the independent blocks, the nonempty sets hit by a single signing are *exactly* the products of b two-element subsets of a three-element alphabet. In particular a signing hits at most 2ᵇ of the 3ᵇ hosts. The smallest universal list on this family equals the ternary-cube covering number f(b). Kuang and Wang prove f(b)=(C₃+o(1))(3/2)ᵇ with 1.62227<C₃≤2. [Kuang and Wang](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13252)

If a selector reads at most t whole input trits adaptively before choosing its list, the optimal worst-case list size becomes f(b−t), for 0≤t≤b. At a leaf with r≤t distinct inspected coordinates the other b−r trits are free, so projection gives the covering lower bound there. Conversely read t fixed trits and cover the remaining product optimally, choosing signs on inspected blocks to succeed on their actual trits. This measures information used to select signs. Reading all b trits suffices to construct one successful signing.

The same construction works for any fixed finite alphabet Γ of nonzero real entries. Put r=|Γ/Γ| and use blocks of size 2r, each with its first two rows supported on a chosen column pair and its remaining rows forced bijectively to the remaining columns in a predetermined order. At least r of the binom(2r,2) column pairs have equal ratios. This follows by minimizing the number of equal pairs when 2r objects receive at most r labels. Under a uniform pair choice a fixed mask survives a block with probability at most 1−1/(2r−1). For r≥2 a universal list thus has size at least (1−1/(2r−1))^(−⌊m/(2r)⌋). For r=1 no mask succeeds even on one two-column block. The proof requires the finite alphabet restriction.

## Two tests of input-dependent reconstruction

Given a perfect matching, move it to the diagonal and initially make all mask entries +1. Working from k=1 up to m, set the sign of the kth diagonal entry so the kth leading principal determinant is nonzero. Its two possible values differ by twice the kth matched entry times the preceding nonzero minor. A choice always exists over the reals. This yields a polynomial-time, input-dependent signing, including on Boolean hosts outside the block family. An empty matching set makes every masked determinant zero. On the block promise all answers happen to be YES, so the list lower bound measures a requirement on a witness-producing method even there.

Can input dependence circumvent the exact-counting bound? On binary hosts, for each X there is a two-signing convex combination with value exactly perm(X). Indeed the independent-sign average equals the permanent, and any real number between the minimum and maximum squared determinants is a convex combination of those two values. The weights can be taken rational. This existence argument uses the target value to specify the weight. On an open set of real inputs, countably many candidate ensembles with fixed masks and fixed weights cannot cover the set by exact answers unless one ensemble gives a polynomial identity. Otherwise their zero sets would have total Lebesgue measure zero. The factorial bound then applies to that ensemble. The domains and the order of choices are essential.

For SAT the missing implication is a way to recover hard coefficients from the decisions of a fast solver on its restrictions. Even having a polynomial-time perfect-matching optimizer, a uniquely isolatable optimum and an input-dependent determinant certifying existence gives no polynomial bound on the fixed exact ensembles above. These deductions exclude specific transfers and leave unrestricted Boolean RAM time open.
