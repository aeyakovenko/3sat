# How many certified pairwise predictions would force the actual SAT bit?

A certificate about the XOR of two answers can be easier to find than a certificate for either one. For the [balanced ordinary checksum 3-CNFs](sat-on-random-checksums.md), a sound procedure that supplies these relations on a little over half of all pairs would suffice for the unrestricted SAT clock. The reduction uses the two lines of a low-degree polynomial through the actual output coordinate and checks only a polynomial number of the supplied relations. A second test treats a sampler that can make mistakes.

Let $M=2^j$, $K=\mathbb F_q$ with $q=2^s$, $q-1>d$, and let $P:K^d\to K$ interpolate the genuine binary gate table on the Boolean cube. It has total degree at most $d$. The required native bit is $P(o)$ at a public Boolean address $o$. Write $f(p,\lambda)=\mathrm{Tr}(\lambda P(p))$ for the response to the uniform public query $(p,\lambda)\in K^{d+1}$. Its actual physical SAT verdict is $[f(p,\lambda)=\tau]$ for an independent fair pin $\tau$. Known terminal pins can be added to or removed from any pair parity. All query lengths, field-operation costs and local physical-word costs are polynomial in $j$. For definiteness take the polynomial field size from the balanced checksum construction.

## A single nondeterministic computation from uniformly sound relations

Fix a bounded certificate verifier. It reads two query descriptions, a proposed parity, and a witness. At every stage, every accepted parity must equal the XOR of the true responses. One may also use the physical formula queries via their local word generator. Say a query pair is covered if it has any accepted witness within the cost limit. No algorithm to sample the covered pairs is required. Suppose that at infinitely many stages the covered fraction of independent uniform pairs exceeds
$$
                  \frac12\left(1+\left(\frac d{q-1}\right)^2\right).
$$
Then the native bit at every such stage has a sound nondeterministic certificate using at most $2s(d+1)$ accepted pair witnesses plus $\operatorname{poly}(j)$ extra bits for the algebra. The same certificate format is sound on **all** stages. The clock, including the writing and verification of all witnesses, is $T\operatorname{poly}(j)$ if each pair witness and its verification cost at most $T$.

Choose independent uniform directions $a,b\in K^d$. Query on the two lines $o+ta,o+ub$ at independent uniform $t,u\in K^*$, and use independent uniform masks $\lambda,\mu\in K$. For each fixed $t,u$ the two points are independent uniform field points. Thus some two directions preserve at least the assumed density when the two nonzero parameters and masks are varied. Both line restrictions of $P$ have degree at most $d$ and share the constant $P(o)$.

Describe two hypothetical line polynomials with the same constant by their field coefficients, interpreted as binary unknowns. Each certified answer imposes one binary linear equation
$$
     \mathrm{Tr}(\lambda R(t))+\mathrm{Tr}(\mu S(u))=\ell .
$$
Compare the true pair with any hypothetical pair having a different common constant. Each difference polynomial is nonzero, so vanishes on at most $d$ of the $q-1$ parameters. At any position where a difference value is nonzero, its trace against a uniform mask is a fair bit. Independence gives a pair-parity agreement fraction at most the displayed threshold, even if the hypothetical constant is a non-Boolean field element. Therefore the correct equations on these two lines force the common constant. Gaussian elimination selects at most $2s(d+1)$ independent rows with the same solution space.

A verifier guesses directions and such a list of equations with witnesses; it recomputes the endpoints and verifies every claimed parity. It checks consistency, forces the constant by ordinary binary linear algebra, and signals only the specified bit value when forced. The true two line polynomials always solve all accepted rows, including on stages with few witnesses. Guessing false directions, a deficient row list or an unproductive stage cannot make a false sign pass. This procedure uses neither global table verification nor advice about which stages work. If the fixed pair verifier has internal advice, it must be authenticated in a way that guarantees the stated uniform soundness; guessing untrusted verifier advice here would invalidate the argument.

Under a deterministic $O(n^k)$ RAM algorithm for ordinary explicit SAT, $k\in\{1,2,3\}$, the single existential computation costs $T^k\operatorname{poly}(j)$ deterministically via the packed bounded-acceptance encoding. Thus for a large enough fixed $B$, coverage above the displayed threshold with $T\le 2^{\lfloor j/k\rfloor}/(j+1)^B$ on infinitely many stages would exclude the corresponding clock, by the [native successful-detector theorem](priority-pinned-sat.md). The argument also applies to parities of $m$ independent answers for any fixed $m\ge1$, with $m$ lines through $o$, $ms(d+1)$ rows and exponent $m$ on $d/(q-1)$ in the density test. It is close in spirit to random self-reduction for low-degree polynomials. [Gemmell and Sudan](https://people.csail.mit.edu/madhu/papers/1992/gemmell.pdf)

Since the true constant is a **bit**, the verifier can simply XOR a subset of the certified answers. Regard the common constant as one binary unknown and the $2d$ positive-degree coefficients as $2ds$ further binary unknowns. Ruling out the other bit means the constant-coordinate functional lies in the row span: otherwise a vector in the kernel would change its constant coordinate. A basis therefore expresses it as the sum of at most $2ds+1$ certified rows. For the selected pairs $j'$ the directly checkable equations are
$$
 \sum_{j'}\lambda_{j'}t_{j'}^i=\sum_{j'}\mu_{j'}u_{j'}^i=0\quad(1\le i\le d),
 \qquad \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\sum_{j'}(\lambda_{j'}+\mu_{j'})\right)=1 .
$$
These are field equalities for the nonconstant coefficient functionals by trace nondegeneracy. The XOR of the accepted labels then equals $P(o)$, independently of every other true coefficient. Finding such witnessed moment identities even on very sparse sets of pairs would suffice: the density criterion is one way to guarantee their existence.

There is a lower limit on what certified relations alone can imply. Consider two hypothetical binary tables differing only at $o$. Both agree on the parity of a pair of queries on exactly a fraction $(1+\delta^2)/2$ of pairs, where $\delta=1-(1-1/q)^d$. Indeed their response difference is the trace of one Lagrange basis value times the mask. Its sign mean is precisely the probability that this value vanishes. For $m$ independent queries replace the square by $\delta^m$. A complete list of true relations on this agreement set cannot distinguish the two tables. These are candidate tables for the algebraic inference; the actual native clauses distinguish them. The small difference between this obstruction and the sufficient density is left open.

## A deterministic cost bound on a public pair of lines

Fix any uniformly polynomial-in-$j$ computable directions, and let the query distribution use just those two lines, all nonzero $t,u$, and independent masks and fair pin. One ordinary 3-CNF represents the parity query: keep the unique base computation once, use selector bits $\mathrm{Tr}(\lambda L_i(o+ta)+\mu L_i(o+ub))$ in the running XOR, and compare with the printed pin. All words are locally computable as before. For each fixed deterministic solver correct even just on this promise there is a fixed $B$ such that at every sufficiently late stage at least a fraction
$$
                   \tfrac12\left(1-(d/(q-1))^2\right)
$$
of the $(t,\lambda,u,\mu)$ draws take over $M/(j+1)^B$ steps on **both pins**. Indeed enumerate this fixed polynomial-in-$j$ grid and run the solver on both pins up to the cap. Completion on either one supplies the true parity, so their union is a uniformly sound set of equations. Above the density threshold it forces the actual constant. The deterministic search for a forced bit and all capped simulations together cost $o(M)$ for large enough $B$. The native sound-detector theorem excludes even infinitely many such successes. The solver may be told the parameters. Identical physical strings, if they occur, count with the multiplicities of the displayed distribution. This bound concerns exact or sound partial solvers. For unpromised heuristics there is a separate [unconditional Walsh construction](almost-everywhere-hard-sat-forecasts.md).

## Inverting a possibly incorrect sampler with two SAT calls

Let a stage sampler take $r$ uniform seed bits and return a pair of public queries $z=(x,y)$ and a proposed XOR. Its accuracy on its own draws is $1/2+\gamma$. Its pair marginal may be far from uniform. If $N=q^{2(d+1)}$ is the number of possible pairs, set
$$
                       \kappa=N\sum_z\Pr[z]^2.
$$
This is one for uniform pairs. Large multiplicities or concentration on few pairs can increase it. There is a predictor of the single response on a uniform query with advantage at least $\gamma^2/(2\kappa)$ given SAT access for two bounded existence tests, suitable stage advice, and $\gamma>0$. A pair sampler sourced from a walk may output the XOR of its recorded edge labels. Only the terminal pair and reliability of that XOR enter this statement.

For each $z$ let $g_z,b_z$ count the correct and incorrect preimage seeds. The predictor need not know which label is correct. Put $R=2^r$ and choose a power of two $H$ so that $\gamma/\kappa<\alpha=R/(NH)\le2\gamma/\kappa$. This is possible with $H\ge1$ since $\kappa\ge N/R$ and $\gamma\le1/2$. Choose a random pairwise independent hash from seeds to $H$ buckets. At a requested pair use two SAT tests to ask which output labels occur among preimages hashing to zero. Return the sole label if just one occurs; if both or neither occur, use a fair bit. Relative to the true label the signed bias is the probability that the correct label occurs minus the probability that the incorrect label occurs. By the first two inclusion–exclusion terms for the former and the union bound for the latter, its average over uniform $z$ and hashes is at least
$$
 \frac{\sum_z(g_z-b_z)}{NH}-\frac{\sum_z(g_z+b_z)^2}{2NH^2}
                  =2\alpha\gamma-\alpha^2\kappa/2.
$$
Half of this quantity is an advantage at least $\gamma^2/(2\kappa)$ for the chosen range. This derivation charges collisions among seeds reporting the same label as well, making the estimate conservative. If all answers of the sampler are correct, a simpler bound is $1/(2\kappa)$: at least $N/\kappa$ pairs occur by Cauchy–Schwarz, and every preimage of any such pair gives the right label. The existence tests can then use the unfiltered seed set.

Fix a hash, fallback bit, and one endpoint $y$ with at least the resulting average success, and store its one true bit as advice. The pair predictor now gives a uniform single-answer predictor by undoing that bit. All these choices are independent of the new query. Sampler advice may also be used: for **this** reduction a faulty guessed sampler never falsely certifies a native sign, because the [full balanced-checksum reconstruction](sat-on-random-checksums.md) ultimately verifies the alleged gate table. Even a sampler that has the stated advantage only on unrecognized stages fits this argument.

A binary Toeplitz matrix plus a uniform output offset implements the pairwise independent hash using $O(r+\log H)$ random bits. For any nonzero input difference, the first nonzero input position selects a fresh matrix diagonal in each output row, yielding a uniform difference word, and the offset gives a uniform first output. Computing the product is binary convolution. One may pack each input coefficient into $O(\log(r+\log H+2))$ bits, multiply integers and extract the low bit at each required coefficient to obtain near-linear bit time up to powers of a logarithm. The $H=1$ case requires no hash. This time bound uses only ordinary finite-word simulation of a uniform near-linear bit multiplication algorithm. [Harvey and van der Hoeven](https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2021/193-2/p04)

Let the sampler with its stage advice, seed and running time cost at most $U$ altogether. For $\gamma$ inverse-polynomial and $\log U=O(j)$ the guessed hash has $O(U+\operatorname{poly}(j))$ bits. Both bounded existence tests cost $(U+1)^k\operatorname{poly}(j)$ under $O(n^k)$ SAT, including the packed formula construction. Thus on the field chosen for $\epsilon=(j+1)^{-A}$, a sampler with $\gamma^2/(2\kappa)>\epsilon$ and $U\le 2^{\lfloor j/k^3\rfloor}/(j+1)^B$ infinitely often for sufficiently large $B$ would contradict the earlier checksum prediction consequence. This is the weaker clock needed when incorrect labels have to be tolerated. Hash-based isolation and distributional inversion have a long history. [Valiant and Vazirani](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(86)90135-0) · [Jerrum, Valiant and Vazirani](https://www2.stat.duke.edu/~scs/Courses/Stat376/Papers/ConvergeRates/RandomizedAlgs/JerrumValiantVaziraniTCS1986.pdf)

## Testing the candidate against the actual clauses

Simultaneous Frobenius powers give a zero-error pair sampler of uniform single-endpoint marginals at all stages, but only up to $s$ equivalent second endpoints can follow a fixed first one. Its collision factor is at least $q^{d+1}/s$. More generally in the power-of-two padded family, even concatenating arbitrarily many parity relations, each proved from fewer than $2^d/64$ chosen base clauses, cannot leave the Frobenius orbit of a typical selector. The covered fraction of independent pairs is at most the exceptional first-endpoint probability plus $s/q^{d+1}$. Because the exceptional first-endpoint probability is $O((d+1)/q+dq\,2^{-\lfloor d/2\rfloor})$, a correct generator confined to such paths cannot induce enough pair density for either transfer at the chosen field size: Cauchy–Schwarz gives $1/\kappa$ at most this covered fraction. The obstruction concerns justifications by retained native clauses. A verifier that reasons from the RAM program or a generator using a different representation could certify other relations. The missing object on this route is a cheap verifier covering enough genuine pairs, or a noisy sampler with sufficient accuracy and spread.
