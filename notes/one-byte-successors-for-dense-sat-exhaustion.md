# A one-byte successor for dense SAT cores, with an exact executed-step lower bound

Use the separate-operation RAM, a packed little-endian ASCII DIMACS file, and the [dense, sorted, duplicate-free proper 3-CNF convention](dense-sorted-cores-for-ram-exhaustion.md). Sort literals by unsigned name, putting positive before negative on ties. Let $L$ count **bytes** and $n=8L$ count the input bits used in the time target. Consecutive nonempty ASCII lengths are eight bits apart. In the formulas below the header ends with a newline and the body omits the final newline. Requiring a final newline throughout just adds a byte to each base size.

**Successor theorem.** There is a single explicit sequence of satisfiable centers $F_L$, one at every sufficiently large byte length. From $F_L$ to $F_{L+1}$ one deletes at most twelve clauses and inserts at most thirteen, with ordinary header updates. For standard width $w=16\lceil\log_2(n+2)/16\rceil$, each center has $m_L=\Theta(n/\log n)$ independent marked words. The sequence $m_L$ is nondecreasing. For $1\le k\le m_L$, the optimum worst-case native clock for deciding the promise of at most $k$ edits is
$$
 2m_L-\left\lfloor\frac{m_L}{k+1}\right\rfloor .
$$
Every correct program on that promise pays the lower bound on $F_L$ itself. Code size and writable workspace are unrestricted, and the program for the optimum may be chosen separately at each length. There is also **one globally correct SAT program** taking $O(n/\log n)$ steps on this entire sequence and its promised edits across all lengths. Thus this particular consecutive-length induction, including correctness against every stored program size, stops at the word scale.

## Exact-length padding for any dense core

Write $d(u)$ for the number of decimal digits of a positive integer $u$. Start with a nonempty dense core of $v\ge3$ occurring variables and $c$ clauses. Insert fresh variables $g_i=v+i$ and one clause per new variable with positive last literal $g_i$. Its two other literals have distinct smaller names. Setting the new variables true extends every old satisfying assignment; conversely deleting the added clauses preserves any solution of the enlarged file on the old variables.

With the other two literals always $1,2$, define the positive increment in byte length for $t\ge1$ by
$$
 P_t=d(v+t)-d(v)+d(c+t)-d(c)+\sum_{i=1}^t\big(d(v+i)+7\big).
$$
This counts all spaces, zero terminators, and the extra separating newline per clause. Since $P_{t+1}-P_t\le d(v+t+1)+9$, these lengths are strictly increasing and have logarithmic gaps. They are realized by different clause *sets* even when the input already contains all possible old clauses, since each new clause's largest name is its own fresh guard.

For a row with guard $g$, let $q=d(g)$. For $q\ge2$, all pairs of decimal lengths in $[1,q-1]$ can be used for its preceding names: take $2\cdot10^{a-1}$ and $3\cdot10^{b-1}$ and put them in sorted order. Using either sign independently gives every additional byte count from zero through $2q-2$ relative to the minimum. In all cases a gain of zero, one or two is possible with just the names 1 and 2. If $q=d(v+t)\ge12$, the final row alone bridges the entire gap to $P_{t+1}-1$, including decimal carries in both header fields. If $q<12$ and $t\ge12$, two optional signs in each of the last twelve rows provide 24 bytes of slack, enough for the gap. Earlier fresh names used as adjustable operands cause no new constraint, because the largest literal remains a positive fresh guard.

It follows that for every target increment at least $P_{12}$ an enlarged core of **exactly** that length exists. In particular $O(1+\log(v+2))$ bytes of initial slack suffice, independently of the input clause content. For $d(v+1)\ge12$ every increment starting at $P_1$ works. This last threshold is sharp for some satisfiable cores when all original clauses are retained. For each triple of names include the seven clauses true on the all-zero assignment. The resulting dense proper core has that assignment alone and already contains every proper clause it satisfies on those names. Any strictly larger satisfiable clause set retaining it must introduce a fresh name. Its first new clause costs at least $d(v+1)+7$ bytes, and the header grows by at least its contribution in $P_1$. Renaming the old core or replacing its clauses is a different operation.

For explicit construction, emit successive guards until the next minimum would overshoot, allocate the remaining slack, then sort or merge. This takes polynomial time in the requested length and uses no semantic SAT test. For the polynomial-clock [finite exhaustion over unbounded integer literals](finite-sat-exhaustion-with-unbounded-literals.md), it replaces arbitrary whitespace padding even on the restriction queries: normalize the pinned formula, then pad its core to the common test length. The unpadded restrictions all have $O(n)$ bits and $P_{12}=O(1+\log n)$ bytes. Increase the constant in their common length and handle trivial empty formulas directly. Consequently one bounded-slot candidate fitting all **dense sorted** cores at one length in every sufficiently large polynomial interval would again imply $\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}$. The logarithmic-advice consequence comes from the earlier verified witness search; the extra result is the canonical, exact-length realization. Classical polynomial padding is much less specific about the attainable lengths and syntax. [Berman and Hartmanis](https://doi.org/10.1137/0206023)

## What a true RAM separation would force on consecutive lengths

The padding also gives a simultaneous formulation of the unrestricted target. Allow each fixed program a sufficiently large fixed logarithmic width coefficient, as in the usual polynomial-address word-RAM class. Fix $d=1,2,$ or $3$. The following statements are equivalent.

* Ordinary proper DIMACS SAT has no globally correct deterministic $O(n^d)$ solver.
* For every finite nonempty list of globally correct solvers, every positive integer $h$, and every clock constant $C$, there exist arbitrarily large source lengths and one core at each such source length of either prescribed satisfiability verdict (chosen in advance) whose answer takes more than $C N^d$ steps for every listed solver on **every** one of $h$ consecutive canonical padded copies. Here $N$ is the actual encoded bit length of each copy.

Use the following precise convention. On a source file of $n=8b$ bits normalize its core as in the [same-width compiler](dense-sorted-cores-for-ram-exhaustion.md), then pad it to each byte length $2b+j$, $0\le j<h$. Any fixed offset for small lengths is inessential. Cores with no clauses can be decided directly and need not be hard sources. The padding is deterministic using the preceding construction. In particular neighboring copies can share all but at most twelve removed and thirteen inserted clauses: when the number of new guards increases, all but the final twelve old guards were already in their minimum spelling. A full set of copies preserves the same source core and every satisfying assignment on it extends to each copy.

For the nontrivial implication, suppose every sufficiently large source has at least one timely copy for at least one of the listed solvers. Compute the copies and try all the listed solvers at all those lengths with a timeout. Return the first actual verdict obtained. Total correctness of *each* solver ensures this answer is right, even though there is no prior information about which simulation is timely. For a prescribed verdict it suffices that all sufficiently large sources of that verdict have a timely copy: return the opposite verdict if all trials time out. If the successful copy can be different for each source, this uniform rule still costs $O(n^d)$, contrary to the first statement. Conversely one $O(n^d)$ solver and a large enough $C$ contradict the second statement already for $h=1$. This produces hard cores of either prescribed verdict. Requiring two opposite verdicts only one input bit apart is a stronger geometric condition; the [sibling search](one-bit-hard-pairs-at-consecutive-sat-lengths.md) gives it at a different deadline.

More quantitatively let $1\le H(n)\le b$ be a uniformly computable block size, and $t(n)$ a computable timeout. If each large source has a timely copy in its block for some solver in a fixed finite list, the same composition costs
$$
 O\big(H(n)\,[n/\log(n+2)+t(n)]\big)
$$
when the parameters can be computed within this budget. To charge construction at word scale, the normalized core has $O(n/\log n)$ clauses. The guard count $t'$ at target byte length $O(n)$ obeys $t'\log(t'+2)=O(n)$, since each successive new guard prints its own distinct name. Maintain digit lengths with counters until finding the correct $t'$, sort the resulting dense triples by radix, and emit their packed decimal spellings by the [packed builder](dense-sorted-cores-for-ram-exhaustion.md#a-restricted-file-no-longer-than-its-input). All of this costs $O(n/\log n)$ for each copy, including when the normalized source is very short. Simulate masked guest arithmetic with bounded host expansions, using a sufficiently large width coefficient to reserve the guest address bank. For successive trials, reset the copied input and only the cells actually written, keeping a chronological log of at most $O(t(n))$ writes. For polynomial clocks and blocks those scratch arrays have polynomial address range. Clock initialization, finite control and the number of solvers are included in the constants.

For example, if quadratic time really is impossible, then for any fixed list of correct solvers and any $C$ there must be arbitrarily large $b$ with one source whose $b$ distinct copies at **every byte length from $2b$ through $3b-1$** all take more than $C N$ steps on every listed solver. If cubic time is impossible the corresponding copies all take more than $C N^2$ steps. These implications are conditional on the requested separation. A faster result on a different source at each length would not suffice to contradict them. The portfolio does not in general fit at the same small width coefficient as a guest because it must retain its source and reset arbitrary writes.

## An infinite chain with a bounded number of clause changes

Keep variables 1 and 2 as free dummies. Pin every subsequent integer variable $i$ when it is introduced, by the four proper clauses on its desired signed literal and all choices of signs on 1,2. Choose true for $3,\ldots,14$ and for names consisting entirely of the decimal digit 5. Choose false otherwise. Start with all pins through 64. From that point onward introduce each next name in numerical order, inserting its four pinning clauses **one at a time**. Even the first clause for a new name preserves density.

For each $r\ge3$, after finishing the pins through $6\cdot10^{r-1}-1$, insert, one at a time, the $10^{r-1}$ clauses
$$
  (i\lor(i+1)\lor A_r),\qquad
  2\cdot10^{r-1}\le i<3\cdot10^{r-1},
$$
where $A_r$ is the all-5s name of length $r$. The first two names are pinned false and the third true. Every name with $r$ digits and first digit 5 is already fully pinned. Resume introducing consecutive variables after this batch. Every prefix is satisfiable by the selected values. All clauses are distinct.

Use twelve more clauses to choose a byte length between successive prefixes. One starts at each positive guard $g=3,\ldots,14$. In its shortest designated form it reads $(g\lor20\lor30)$. When the current largest name is $v$, choose the other two names independently as $2\cdot10^{a-1}$ and $3\cdot10^{b-1}$, with $2\le a,b\le\max(2,d(v)-1)$; both can be signed. They already occur, are larger than all twelve guards, and are different. At $v\ge64$ this supplies all byte gains from zero through $2(\max(2,d(v)-1)-2)+2$ per clause. Their first names separate them from each other, from the pins and from every testing clause. Their true guards preserve satisfaction even on a partially pinned prefix.

Let $B_j$ be the byte length of prefix $j$ with these twelve clauses at minimum, sorted together with all other clauses. Then $B_{j+1}>B_j$, since one clause is inserted and header counts do not shrink. The extra bytes are at most $3d(v)+13$, allowing three signs, separators, a newly longer name and both header carries. The twelve flexible clauses cover all gains up to $B_{j+1}-B_j-1$. For $d(v)\le3$ their capacity is 24; from $d(v)=4$ onwards it is at least $12(2d(v)-4)$. Thus every $L\ge B_0$ has a unique base $j$ with $B_j\le L<B_{j+1}$. Fill its flexible clauses greedily to attain $L$. A one-byte increase changes at most twelve such clauses and adds at most one ordinary clause. The lexical order of the changed records can move; the bound measures edits of clauses, not the number of different physical bytes. The [Lean proof](../lean/OneByteCoreSuccessors.lean) verifies the consecutive-size selection and capacity invariant for arbitrary base and slack sequences.

## The marked words and a fast full-program check

At each length with a complete testing batch, use the **most recently completed** one, of order $r$. Set $m_L=10^{r-1}$. If $v$ is the current largest name, then $6\cdot10^{r-1}-1\le v\le6\cdot10^r-1$. Numeric names and Boolean pins are explicit, so $n=\Theta(v\log v)$ throughout, including any partial pin or testing batch. Hence $m_L=\Theta(n/\log n)$ and $W/r\to(\log_2 10)/8<1/2$ for $W=w/8$. Each marked record's all-5s literal contains a full aligned word strictly past its first digit at every sufficiently large length. This argument permits changes of header size, word width and clause position.

At all selected words use the five settings of the [sparse-edit theorem](exact-executed-clocks-for-sparse-word-edits.md): replace the same positive number of most significant ASCII digits of the selected word by copies of 3, 4, 5, 6 or 7. The number of changed bytes is chosen using the odd part of $w$ so the numerical step $\Delta$ is a multiple of $w$ and $\Delta^2=0\bmod 2^w$. At center every word is all ASCII 5s. Every noncenter identifier keeps its first digit 5 but differs from $A_r$. It was pinned false before this batch began. The first two names of the clause are false as well. The first name $i$ fixes the position of its unique test clause, and the new third name is still bigger than $i+1$. All the formulas remain dense, sorted and duplicate-free with the same header and length. Satisfaction on the family is exactly the all-center test. The symbolic execution and forest lower bound and the equality-block upper program therefore give the stated exact clock at every such length.

The five-setting coordinates can be carried from $L$ to $L+1$. Within the same batch use the same logical clause and digit choice, selecting its new aligned word. At the transition to the next completed batch, send row $i$ to row $10i$, using centers on the other new rows. This preserves the number of edits and the verdict. For fixed $k$ the lower clock is nondecreasing along the chain. The transfer does not assert that the two runs of an arbitrary program execute the same instructions. The native lower bound is re-established at the new physical layout.

A uniform solver can exploit the same structure. Parse the proper formula and group clauses on a signed name and the four sign choices of variables 1,2. Every complete group soundly pins its named variable. Contradictory groups give UNSAT. If at most three occurring variables remain unpinned, try all of their assignments along with the proven pins, and evaluate every clause; otherwise run a general SAT decider. On every file here, including edits, only 1,2 and possibly the currently incomplete pin can remain. On these dense minimal spellings there are $O(n/\log n)$ tokens and all numerical fields fit a fixed number of words. The [packed numeric parser and counting sorts](scan-time-on-the-dense-list-projection.md#the-physical-cost-of-body-parsing) implement the check in $O(n/\log n)$ native time including initialization. Files outside the recognized syntax can fall back. Consequently the one-byte successor and the exact finite optima coexist with one fixed solver using sublinear time in encoded bits on the entire construction. The general SAT time goals remain open.

## The same clock takes radically different numbers of tests at different code budgets

Fix one of the sufficiently large centers with $m=m_L$ marks. At clock $2m-1$, the center and just **one single-word negative per mark** suffice to refute every program of at most $C$ stored instructions whenever
$$
  2m>(C+1)(2C+5).
$$
Here refuting means that a program is wrong on a test or takes at least $2m$ instructions on the center. For programs with at least $2m-1$ slots *allowed*, a sufficient suite drawn from the full edit promise requires a negative test on **every nonempty support**. This is a lower bound of $2^m-1$ negative tests at the very same center and deadline. Hence the exponential black-box suite lower bound for free code cannot be assigned to a fixed small program budget. [Support construction and stronger finite suite bounds](tests-needed-for-the-exact-sparse-edit-clock.md)

For the small-code bound, each distinguishing single edit forces a LOAD of its original memory word. The numerical address of that word must occur as a literal, register value or newly produced numerical word. The entire original input fits below address $J=\lceil n/w\rceil=o(2^w)$. Every full file word has a top byte at least 10 and thus a value above $J$. Consequently at most two values supplied by initial memory can be marked addresses, allowing zero and the one partially filled word. There are at most three other possible public register values and $2C$ numeric operand literals. Denote their total bound by $S=2C+5$. The many *different* high raw words of the dense input need no stock charge because none is a marked address. LOAD, STORE, and branches cannot first manufacture a previously unavailable numerical value. Therefore the center run has at least $U\ge 2m-S$ nonbranch instructions: $m$ mandatory LOADs and at least $m-S$ distinct numerical productions.

A branch-free segment in a halting run uses at most $C$ instructions, since repeating its program counter forces a fixed-successor cycle forever. If $B$ branches execute, $U\le C(B+1)$ and $CT\ge(C+1)U-C$ for $T=U+B$. Substitution of $T\le2m-1$ would give $2m\le (C+1)S$, as claimed. This is the [finite-control and address-stock argument](address-production-and-a-program-size-lift.md) with the initial stock counted among input addresses rather than across the whole raw alphabet.

For the large-code bound, if the suite omits all homogeneous edits on a particular nonempty support $S'$, hardwire its addresses. Require equality among the words in $S'$ and compare every other word to its own center literal. This uses precisely $m$ LOADs and $m-1$ branches, each stored once, and errs only on the four homogeneous edits on $S'$. This argument says that each support needs a representative. It does not say that choosing any representative on each support gives a sufficient suite.

In particular let $m(C)=\lfloor (C+1)(2C+5)/2\rfloor+1$. Freeze all but $m(C)$ sites at center at any length with $m_L\ge m(C)$. The $m(C)+1$-file suite certifies the $2m(C)$ lower bound for all $C$-slot programs. At $C+1$, activate exactly $m(C+1)-m(C)$ more sites on a sufficiently large successor core. The test now quantifies over every rewritten program at the new size. The required file length is $O(C^2\log(C+2))$ bits as $C$ grows because consecutive batch sizes differ by a factor of ten. This gives an explicit stored-size successor at a word-scale deadline. Increasing the deadline to $Cn$, in encoded bits, is still the unresolved operation.
