# How far must a successor move to preserve the old answers?

Suppose a proposed next SAT database has $b$ fresh raw bits, with at least one known filling $z_0$ that preserves every old query answer. Write a Boolean polynomial $p_j(z)$ for the discrepancy in the answer to old query $j$. Thus a preserving filling makes all discrepancies zero. The polynomials here are given explicitly in multilinear form over $\mathbb F_2$. Previously fixed raw data can be removed by substitution. The number of preserving edits inside any particular set of editable positions matters when applying the [finite RAM-program exhaustion](one-master-for-many-ram-programs.md).

## A sharp bound on a prescribed set of editable positions

Assume each $p_j$ has degree at most two. Row-reduce the polynomials as vectors of coefficients, eliminating quadratic monomials first. Let $r$ be the rank of their quadratic parts, and $h$ the number of independent affine equations left by elimination. A nonzero constant equation is impossible because $z_0$ is supplied. The preservation conditions are equivalent to $h$ independent affine equations and $r$ quadratic equations. There can be many more old queries than either parameter.

For **every** set of $t$ raw coordinates, hold all other bits at their values in $z_0$. The number of preserving fillings in that particular subcube is at least
$$
                         2^{\max(0,t-h-2r)}.
$$
Consequently any specified $h+2r+1$ coordinates contain a nonempty edit preserving all old replies, provided that many free coordinates exist. More generally the edit can escape any set of $E$ forbidden fillings if $2^{t-h-2r}>E$. A known filling provides the nonemptiness needed by this bound.

Indeed, the indicator of the common zeros is the product of $1+p$ over the reduced list. Its Boolean degree is at most $h+2r$, even after fixing any bits. It is one at the seed. A nonzero Boolean polynomial of degree at most $D$ on $t$ bits has weight at least $2^{\max(0,t-D)}$. Choose a monomial of maximal degree actually present. For each assignment outside its support the sum modulo two of the polynomial over the remaining bits is one. No larger monomial can contain the chosen one, so this parity survives every restriction outside it. There is at least one solution on each such slice. This is the binary Reed–Muller minimum-weight bound; the common-zero estimate is a form of Warning’s second theorem. [Clark, Forrow and Schmitt, *Warning’s Second Theorem with Restricted Variables*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.7793)

One can recompute the ranks after restricting the edit positions or solving the affine equations. Parameterize their solution flat by free coordinates, substitute, and row-reduce again. If extra affine rows appear, solve them too. Each new affine pivot removes a free coordinate. An affine test or a quadratic on the resulting flat can be lifted to the original coordinates by extracting those free coordinates, provided all earlier affine equations are retained. The final $h$ counts the total independent affine conditions and $r$ the remaining independent quadratic parts. This gives the same bound with the refined parameters. For a specified subcube use its $t$ coordinates as the starting variables.

Both the bit-edit radius and the loss of density are sharp. Set $H=h+2r+1$ and impose equality of consecutive bits in a string of length $H$. Use $h$ single equality constraints. Group the other $2r$ into consecutive pairs. Two conditions $L=M=0$ become one quadratic $1+(1+L)(1+M)=0$. For a pair on three successive bits $a,b,c$ its quadratic part is $ab+ac+bc$. Each pair owns its own adjacent edge monomials, so these $r$ parts are independent. The affine singles have rank $h$. Even on their solution flat the $r$ quadratic parts remain independent if the singles are placed first. The only two solutions are the constant strings. Starting at zero, a second solution changes all $H$ bits. Unconstrained additional bits give exactly a $2^{-(h+2r)}$ preserving fraction at every larger dimension.

## Exact sampling and the transition from degree two to three

After the affine substitutions, write $R_1,\ldots,R_r$ for the surviving equations on $u$ free bits. The precise number of solutions is
$$
             2^{-r}\sum_{\lambda\in\{0,1\}^r}
                    \sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^u}(-1)^{\sum_i\lambda_i R_i(x)}.
$$
Each inner quadratic sum takes polynomial time. Choose a quadratic monomial $xy$ and write its polynomial as $xy+xA+yB+C$, where $A,B$ are affine in the other variables. Summing over $x,y$ gives twice the sum for $C+AB$. An affine exponent has sum zero if nonconstant and has known sign otherwise. Repetition computes the count with $2^r$ times polynomial work in the explicit representation. Restricting a bit cannot increase the rank of the remaining quadratic parts. Exact conditional counts give a deterministic search or a uniform sampler by choosing a uniform index into the ordered solutions. If the forbidden fillings are listed, subtract the number of distinct forbidden *solutions* sharing each prefix before selecting the next bit. Earlier polynomial-time quadratic root counting is due to Ehrenfeucht and Karpinski. [*The Computational Complexity of (XOR, AND)-Counting Problems*](https://theory.cs.uni-bonn.de/Zope/English/csreports/report_1990/paper_8543/abstract.html)

If old queries $q$ come from any given finite list and the new **scalar reply** $H(z,q)$ has total Boolean degree at most two in data and query together, then $r\le1$. The coefficients of $z_i z_j$ are the same at every old query. Subtracting one query discrepancy from each of the others leaves affine equations, even when the prescribed old answer bits are arbitrary. On a consistent affine flat a nonempty preserving set therefore occupies at least a quarter of that flat. Uniform rejection sampling from the flat takes at most four attempts in expectation. The example $1+z_1z_2=0$ attains a quarter. When every Boolean $q$ is queried and the old scalar reply also has explicit degree at most two, it suffices to compare the coefficient polynomials in $q$; there is no need to enumerate the query cube.

At total degree **three**, testing whether there is even one other preserving filling besides a given seed is NP-complete. It suffices to use the one-hot queries $e_j$ and
$$
                         H(z,q)=\sum_j q_j p_j(z).
$$
Each $p_j$ can depend on at most three data bits, and each $q_j$ occurs in at most three monomials. For a given 3-CNF $F(x)$ build a deterministic fan-in-two circuit for $(\neg s\wedge[x=0])\vee(s\wedge F(x))$. Use AND and NOT gates, introducing one data bit for each gate value. For $w=u\wedge v$ require $w+uv=0$; for $w=\neg u$ require $1+w+u=0$. Append a pin setting the final output to one. Use these as the $p_j$ and prescribe all old answers to be zero. Gate values are forced in order by the inputs. The seed $s=0,x=0$ and its gate values are computable. There are exactly $1+\#\mathrm{SAT}(F)$ preserving fillings, and counting just the alternatives to the seed is parsimoniously #P-complete. The tests together have linear circuit size in the input formula, with ordinarily indexed gates. Membership in NP follows by checking a proposed alternative on all the given generator queries. Requiring equality for all Boolean $q$ in this particular construction gives exactly the same tests. The proof uses only named elementary gate equations. It belongs to the standard reduction of circuit satisfaction to Boolean quadratic systems.

## What this would have to preserve for SAT time

If the excluded masters on the chosen coordinate subcube number at most $E$ by an independent analysis of the **actual** programs and their deadline, the counting bound above gives an extension escaping all of them. For the general quadratic case, $2^r$ times polynomial work suffices to find an extension avoiding an explicit list. For the cubic construction the old verdicts are all known, a preserving seed is given, only that seed is forbidden, and the difficulty of a further extension already encodes SAT. The hardness can use actual opposite-verdict proper 3-CNFs on one physical database. Store each bit of $z$, including each proposed gate value, as a padded positive or negative pin and duplicate its packet. Give each $p_j$ a public constant-size Boolean circuit on its at most three participating bits, Tseitin constraints for that circuit, and distinct names for its bit and its complement. These circuits evaluate *violations* on the stored bits; a nonzero violation is allowed on the base formula. Replace either copy of any input pin packet by the signed pin for one output. The remaining copy fixes the original bit. The formula has a model exactly when the queried bit has the claimed value. Three false anchors and proper ternary padding suffice as in the [two-copy construction](one-master-for-many-ram-programs.md). The number of names and clauses is linear in the number of circuit wires and tests, with ordinary logarithmic-length identifiers. The algebraic degree above refers to logical data bits and one-hot query coordinates. The file supplies a query by its output name; its decimal digits need not have this polynomial degree. Changing one logical data bit changes both physical copies of its pin. The public layout can supply the test circuits in topological order with fixed record formats. A globally correct SAT solver can recognize the layout, check the pins and gates, and evaluate all tests in $O(N)$ steps on these files of $N$ encoded bits, using a complete fallback elsewhere. A supplied seed and list of zero answers can come from an old database of constant-zero tests with the old raw bits copied into separate unchanged slots on extension. The hardness concerns choosing the fresh filling of its new database. To derive an unrestricted RAM time lower bound one would additionally have to force a wrong verdict or an excessive elapsed clock for each candidate on the actual file. A fast algorithm can answer every individual query in the explicit gate family above. The complexity of finding a new preserving point does not lower-bound its clock.
