# Can a short challenge strategy replace a long computation proof?

**Research journal, 2026-11-24.** Consider using a fast SAT algorithm to remove interaction from a verifier for a time-hierarchy language. Two resources matter separately: how many challenges must be inspected if they are chosen before the prover speaks, and how hard it is to choose a challenge after seeing a reply. For sumcheck these resources can differ exponentially, even for a zero polynomial.

## A fixed challenge tree for sumcheck has an exact leaf count

Work over an odd-characteristic field. The verifier checks a claimed sum of a polynomial f over the k-dimensional Boolean cube. In round i the prover gives a univariate polynomial q of degree at most dᵢ, subject to q(0)+q(1) equaling the incoming claim. The verifier picks a field element z and carries q(z) forward. At a leaf it checks the claim against f at the successive challenges. The ordinary randomized sumcheck has perfect completeness. Consider instead testing a preselected tree of challenges and asking whether a consistent labeling by prover messages exists. Each choice of q is shared among all children at its node. Merge repeated challenge values with their subtrees at the same prefix. [Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan](https://doi.org/10.1145/146585.146605)

Write b for the number of rounds with dᵢ≥2. **Precisely 2ᵇ leaves are necessary and sufficient to force rejection of a false claim with such a tree.** The lower bound already holds for f=0 and claim one, when the verifier allows the stated degree bounds. It concerns trees fixed independently of the replies; their shape and challenge values can depend on the input and claim.

At a subtree, call an incoming error *possible* if the prover can satisfy every check below it. Errors are differences from the honest partial sums, and the zero error is always possible. The possible errors form a linear subspace of the field, hence are either all elements or just zero. Call the latter subtree forcing. A checked leaf is forcing. At a node the possible errors are precisely the values L(q)=q(0)+q(1) for error polynomials q that vanish at the challenges leading to forcing children. Other children accept every error, independently once q is fixed.

A forcing node needs at least one forcing child because L(1)=2. A node with dᵢ≥2 needs at least two. With only a single forcing child z, the polynomial X−z vanishes there and L(X−z)≠0 unless z=1/2. In that case (X−1/2)² vanishes there and its L-value is 1/2. Induction gives the lower bound. For its converse choose challenges 0 and 1 in every high-degree round; their pair detects any nonzero incoming error. In an affine or constant round choose just 1/2 since L(q)=2q(1/2). This tree works for every f and false sum with the specified degree bounds. For f=0 an adaptive verifier with access to q can instead pick a Boolean endpoint whose value is nonzero. This needs only one path and very little computation.

There is a related black-box lower bound even for adaptive evaluation of an unknown f. A deterministic exact evaluator on the all-zero transcript must query at least 2ᵇ field points. Otherwise the Boolean-sum functional must be a linear combination of fewer evaluation functionals: every polynomial vanishing on the queried points follows the zero transcript and must have zero sum. On products of multilinear monomials in the b high-degree variables, the functional's matrix is 2^(k−b) times the b-fold tensor power of [[2,1],[1,1]], of rank 2ᵇ. Each evaluation contributes a rank-one matrix, a contradiction. Midpoint evaluation and the Boolean choices above attain the bound with weights. This uses the classical moment-matrix method for cubature. A verifier given the circuit of f can exploit its representation. [Fialkow and Petrović](https://cs.newpaltz.edu/~fialkowl/files/cubature.pdf)

## Reply-dependent strategies and exact counting

Use an integer version on Boolean challenges. For an explicitly supplied k-variable CNF F and claimed total C between 0 and 2ᵏ, the prover splits a count into nonnegative child counts for its two next restrictions. Check their sum and their bounds by the number of remaining assignments. The challenger selects a child. At a full assignment the verifier checks the CNF bit. A winning challenger strategy for a false total forces rejection against *every* sequence of submitted counts. Any circuit implementing it can be checked with one SAT query for a prover sequence that escapes rejection. The verifier unrolls k rounds of the circuit and its elementary consistency tests.

Under SAT∈P, the assertion that all false totals for all CNFs have polynomial-size winning challenger circuits, with one common polynomial bound, is equivalent to every #P function being in FP. For the forward implication, false totals would have existential strategy certificates with a universal check. SAT∈P makes this a polynomial-time decision predicate. True totals always have a passing prover, hence no winning challenger. To compute the unique true total, ask whether any integer completing a proposed binary prefix satisfies the resulting true-total predicate. SAT∈P again gives polynomial-time search. Parsimonious Boolean verifier encodings recover any #P count. Conversely, exact counting in FP computes the true total for either child given its prefix. If a valid split has a false parent, choose a child with a wrong count. This algorithm gives polynomial-size strategy circuits when the formula and root claim are fixed. The equivalence uses a polynomial bound on an entire adaptive strategy, even though each individual reply can be just one bit.

## Multiplicative gaps permit short adaptive strategies under fast SAT

There is a positive construction for gross errors. Fix 0<ε≤1, with ε⁻¹ polynomially bounded in the formula size. For every F, under SAT∈P there are polynomial-size challenger circuits defeating an overclaim by a factor at least 1+ε or an underclaim by the same factor. A positive claim on a zero total and a zero claim on a positive total are included. First use nonnegative child counts and integer addition. A circular-distance test below treats field residues.

To see the cost, choose a rational η of inverse-polynomial size with (1+η)^(2k)<1+ε for k≥1, for example η=1/⌈8k/ε⌉. We construct one circuit, with F fixed, giving estimates H(w) for the number N(w) of satisfying completions of *every* Boolean prefix w, such that H≤N≤(1+η)²H. Use a SAT query for exact emptiness. For a positive N use geometric thresholds M=(1+η)ʲ, 0≤j≤⌈log_{1+η}(2ᵏ)⌉. A threshold test must reject when N≤M and accept when N≥(1+η)M.

Set B=⌈64/η²⌉. For M≥B choose an integer l≥0 with B≤M·2^(−l)<2B. Hash k-bit assignments into l bits with a uniformly random affine Boolean matrix and offset. Inclusion in the zero bucket has probability p=2^(−l) and the indicators for distinct assignments are pairwise independent. When M<B set l=0 and p=1. Ask whether at least ⌈(1+η/2)Mp⌉ distinct satisfying completions lie in the bucket. One explicit SAT instance can describe this many copies of F, prefix and hash checks, and require their assignments to be in strictly increasing lexicographic order. It uses O(B) copies, each of size Õ(|F|+k²) before fixed polynomial bookkeeping, where |F| counts encoded bits. For l>0 the bucket count has mean Np and variance at most Np. At N≤M its chance of crossing the threshold is at most 4/(η²Mp). At N≥(1+η)M the failure probability is at most 4(1+η)/(η²Mp), by maximizing μ/(μ−(1+η/2)Mp)² for μ≥(1+η)Mp. For l=0 the threshold query is exact.

Take the majority of O(k+log(k/η+2)) independent tests. A union bound over all prefixes and thresholds shows that a single hardwired list of hashes works on every separated comparison for the fixed F. One may truncate a k-output hash to its first l coordinates. Select the largest accepting threshold, defaulting to 1 for a nonempty prefix with none. This gives H≤N≤(1+η)²H, including N=1. All threshold literals can be prepared as rational constants with polynomial bit length in k and 1/η. The construction gives existence of small circuits; an arbitrary SAT search for good hash coins would need a separate correctness argument. A polynomial-time SAT algorithm supplies polynomial-size Boolean circuits for the bucket tests. This is an instance of Stockmeyer's approximate-counting method. [Stockmeyer](https://doi.org/10.1145/800061.808740)

For an overclaim with announced children yᵢ, pick a child maximizing yᵢ/Hᵢ among positive true children. A child with Hᵢ=0 and yᵢ>0 has priority and certifies a positive claim on an empty subtree; a child with both zero can be discarded. With no such positive-on-empty child, the ratio of parent claim to true count is a weighted average of the ratios yᵢ/Nᵢ. The selected child's true ratio is at least the parent's divided by (1+η)². For an underclaim, minimize yᵢ/Hᵢ over nonempty children; some such child's true ratio is at most the parent's, even if an empty child receives positive weight. The selected child's ratio is at most the parent's times (1+η)². The direction of the root error can be given as one bit of strategy advice. Over k steps the loss is smaller than 1+ε, leaving a false value at the checked leaf. Cross multiplication suffices for all comparisons. If the current true count is zero and the announced value is positive, follow any positive child. If the current true count is positive and the announced value is zero, follow a nonempty child. These cases need only ordinary decision SAT.

## Recover a gap test in the original finite-field game

The ordering argument above cannot compare field residues by their integer ratios. There is a way to use Boolean challenges in sumcheck over a prime field even when the prover may send *arbitrary* residues. On Boolean inputs choose signed integer lifts of f(a), computable by a small Boolean circuit with polynomially many output bits. For each prefix w let T(w) be the signed sum of the remaining lifts and M(w) their total absolute mass. Write distₚ(y,T)=min_{j∈ℤ}|y−T−jp| for the circular distance. Assume the verifier can evaluate f at its selected Boolean leaf. A root discrepancy distₚ(y,T)>0 on zero mass, or distₚ(y,T)≥εM with positive mass and inverse-polynomial ε, has a polynomial-size winning challenge strategy under SAT∈P.

Indeed the positive and negative masses in each subtree are ordinary unweighted counts after adding bits for an integer z with 0≤z<|f(a)| and testing the sign. Apply the preceding hash construction simultaneously to all prefixes for each of these two counts. Write their lower estimates as H⁺,H⁻, common accuracy factor λ=(1+η)², H=H⁺+H⁻ and S=H⁺−H⁻. Then H≤M≤λH and |S−T|≤(λ−1)H. Choose η so small that λᵏ<1+ε/2, for example η=1/⌈16k/ε⌉. This only costs polynomial size. Distances to rational estimates S can be compared by integer arithmetic on their polynomial-length encodings.

Suppose a valid split presents child residues y₀,y₁. If either child has zero mass and a nonzero claim, choose it and subsequently follow nonzero child claims until a rejecting leaf. Otherwise discard zero-mass children, whose claim and sum are both zero. With ρ=distₚ(y,T)/M positive, the triangle inequality on the circle gives

    Σᵢ distₚ(yᵢ,Sᵢ) ≥ distₚ(y,T) − Σᵢ |Tᵢ−Sᵢ|
                      ≥ (ρ−(λ−1)) Σᵢ Hᵢ.

Select a positive-mass child maximizing distₚ(yᵢ,Sᵢ)/Hᵢ. Its true normalized discrepancy is at least (ρ−2(λ−1))/λ whenever the numerator is positive. After j rounds the lower bound is (ρ_root+2)/λʲ−2, which stays positive through the leaf. Only the two endpoint values of each prover polynomial are used. For the CNF indicator, if the claimed total is zero and the true count lies in (0,p/2], then ρ=1. The strategy defeats inconsistent modular cancellations across children while inspecting a single path. If p>2^(k+1) and the announced root count is an integer y≤K≤2ᵏ, *every* incorrect such count has a gap: for N>0 and y≠N, |y−N|/N≥1/(K+1), and the modular distance equals this ordinary absolute error before normalization. Thus claims bounded by a polynomial admit exact rejection strategies even on formulas with arbitrarily many satisfying assignments. For unbounded y a wrong neighboring total can have ρ=1/N, so the arbitrary-exact-total obstruction still applies.

## Apply the construction to a hierarchy computation

Given a strategy circuit of size s for a k-round splitting game, a nondeterministic machine can guess it and call deterministic SAT on an explicit Õ(k(s+|F|+k)) bit formula for an escaping transcript, with elementary binary-count and encoding costs included. If a hierarchy decision reduced within budget to rejecting a multiplicatively false total for a sufficiently short explicit predicate, the gap construction would remove the requirement for exact counts on that predicate. The two-sided promise and the formula-production time must both be proved.

In the ordinary Cook–Levin encoding of a T-step accepting computation, a zero-versus-positive total already suffices. Its explicitly supplied formula has order T up to polylogarithmic factors in the usual tableau construction. Replacing counts by their signs leaves that input cost. Alternatively one can count, on only O(log T) random bits, violations of a purported succinct history circuit. This gives a small predicate when the history circuit is small. The argument above produces the challenger for that predicate and supplies no circuit describing an accepting history. In addition, a positive integer count may differ from a wrong neighboring count by a factor as small as 1+2^(−k), outside the efficient approximation guarantee. The outstanding step is a near-linear-clock hierarchy encoding whose transcript checks have the short predicate and the necessary gap without presupposing the witness's compression.
