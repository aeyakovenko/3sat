# How much of a random SAT checksum follows from known linear constraints?

The [binary checksum construction](sat-on-random-checksums.md) turns a bit of a dense linear checksum of a genuine gate table into the existence of an ordinary complete SAT assignment. Its predictor receives short field parameters or may read the generated physical formula. The conditional lower bound requires an advantage of a specified size at the chosen field size. A small inverse-polynomial advantage may already be possible from exact structural information.

Let $Q=2^s$ and interpolate a binary table on $\{0,1\}^d$ by its unique multiaffine polynomial $P$ over $K=\mathbb F_Q$. Suppose the only information about the table is membership in a fixed, known, nonempty binary affine subspace $h_0+U$ with $\dim_{\mathbb F_2} U=r$. Ask for the binary inner product of a uniform $s$-bit $\lambda$ with the field coordinates of $P(p)$ at uniform $p\in K^d$. An independent terminal pin may reverse the desired verdict and is public. Then the optimal randomized worst-table probability of getting the answer right is exactly
$$
 \frac{1+\mu}{2},\qquad
 \mu=\mathbb E_p 2^{-\dim_{\mathbb F_2}\{P_u(p):u\in U\}}
     =\mathbb E_{u\in U}\Pr_p[P_u(p)=0].
$$
For $\lambda$ restricted to nonzero masks, replace $\mu$ by $(Q\mu-1)/(Q-1)$. The formula concerns the information available; it does not charge the time required to calculate subspace images.

For a fixed query the answer is known if $\lambda$ annihilates the image, in which case use $P_{h_0}(p)$. Otherwise predict by a fair coin. Every candidate table has exactly the displayed success probability. Conversely a uniform table in the affine subspace gives a balanced verdict on every non-annihilating query, so no randomized strategy has a larger minimum success. Counting annihilating masks proves the first expression. Counting zeros by the kernel size of $u\mapsto P_u(p)$ gives the second, including $r=0$.

If $u\ne0$, its interpolant is nonzero. Its fraction of nonzero values is at least $(1-1/Q)^d$. Indeed write the polynomial in the last variable as $a t+b$. If $a$ is nonzero, induction gives the lower bound using at most one zero at each point with $a\ne0$. If $a$ vanishes identically apply induction to $b$. A product of one nonconstant linear factor per variable attains the bound. Consequently
$$
  \mu\le 2^{-r}+(1-2^{-r})\big[1-(1-1/Q)^d\big].
$$
Thus knowing even many exact affine equations among table bits gives limited worst-table prediction power while $r$ stays large. For a gap $\epsilon$ with $d/Q\le\epsilon$, obtaining a worst-table advantage greater than $\epsilon$ requires $2^{-r}>\epsilon$. This bound does not restrict an algorithm exploiting additional nonlinear properties of the actual native table. The distance calculation is the product-code distance; the minimax formula specializes it to these SAT verdicts. ## A dense checksum whose value is sometimes certified cheaply on the actual run

Take the actual unique table and add $2\ell$ binary index coordinates, in pairs. For copies whose index pairs all agree store the corresponding original bit, and force all the other copies to zero. Padding the original table by prescribed zeros to a power of two is allowed. Generate ordinary equality and constant constraints for the new slots using the public index rule. The new table and deterministic checksum gates still have unique completions when the pin is correct. Choosing $\ell=O(\log(j+2))$ enlarges the near-native encoded formula size only by a polynomial factor in $j$, since the factor in table width is $4^\ell$. Its interpolation, with extra field coordinates $(a_i,b_i)$, is
$$
   P_{\rm new}(p,a,b)=P(p)\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1+a_i+b_i).
$$
On the binary index cube $1+a_i+b_i$ is the equality indicator in characteristic two. The right hand side is multiaffine in distinct groups of variables and agrees on that cube. Public field arithmetic therefore certifies a zero checksum whenever any $a_i+b_i=1$. With uniform independent field coordinates this event has probability $1-(1-1/Q)^\ell$. On it use the zero digit and the public pin to answer the physical SAT query. Elsewhere a fair guess gives exactly half this event probability as an advantage at every stage of the true computation.

The same test works if $\lambda$ is nonzero and all field coordinates are independently uniform outside $\{0,1\}$, for $Q>2$. In this case the usual interpolation weight of **each array position** is nonzero, since every factor is either $p_i$ or $1-p_i$. The publicly decidable cancellation event has probability $1-(1-1/(Q-2))^\ell$, since $b_i=1+a_i$ is an allowed value for every allowed $a_i$. Nonzero field weights may still give zero bits after applying a binary inner product. On other queries the deterministic answer UNSAT is correct exactly half the time because the terminal pin is fair and independent. The public header in the physical formula exposes every field coordinate and the terminal pin in polynomially many reads in $j$. This example is stronger than merely using the readily recognized mask $\lambda=0$.

When the full original $2^d$-bit array is unknown, the displayed cancellation probability $\theta$ controls the minimax answer quite closely under nonzero masks. Its interpolants include $1,x_1,\ldots,x_d$. An annihilating mask must kill $1$, and then every independently sampled $p_i$. For uniform unrestricted coordinates each of the latter tests has probability $1/2$. If coordinates exclude zero and one, the probability for a mask killing $1$ is $(Q/2-2)/(Q-2)\le1/2$. On the event that the added product is nonzero, multiplication by that product permutes the nonzero masks. The advantage from the publicly known copying and zero constraints alone is therefore between $\theta/2$ and $\theta/2+(1-\theta)2^{-d-1}$. Extra relations specific to the actual gate table could improve prediction.

For comparison the conditional difficulty of the unpadded checksum persists on the complement of these cancellations. Fix any public $a,b$ with nonzero product. Multiplication by that product is an invertible binary-linear transformation of the $s$ coordinates of $P(p)$, so it can be absorbed into a bijective relabeling of $\lambda$. Any average advantage on the conditional product distribution for $a,b$ occurs at some fixed such $a,b$, at the cost of storing their polynomial-length descriptions and a polynomial-in-$j$ factor in the virtual-read simulation time. A predictor on the padded **formula** can likewise be simulated by locally generating its formula words from these descriptions and the new label. If the other parameters are restricted to non-Boolean $p_i$ and nonzero $\lambda$, the removed fraction of the unrestricted distribution is at most $(2d+1)/Q$. If a fraction $\eta$ of challenges was removed, filling in with a fair guess gives full-distribution advantage $(1-\eta)\delta$ from retained advantage $\delta$. A retained advantage strictly above $\epsilon/(1-\eta)$ meets the original strict threshold. For the stated field size and large $j$, a margin $2\epsilon$ suffices. Thus the explicit good set gives a genuine correct forecast for a fraction of the instances, while the remaining signed advantage needed in the lower-bound argument is unproved. The near-native formulas on this subset do not yield an unrestricted time exclusion.
