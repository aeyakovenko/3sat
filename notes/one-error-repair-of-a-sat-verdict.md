# What it costs to repair one violated constraint

**Research journal, 25 November 2026.** Consider an ordinary 3-SAT instance together with a public assignment that violates exactly one clause. Can an arbitrary change of the SAT verdict be confined to a few places in a supplied near-solution? There is a bit-linear reduction from explicit 3-SAT for which, on every satisfiable output, *every* sequence of small changes from the public assignment to *any* solution passes through a state violating a constant fraction of the clauses. The construction first works with the six-symbol, four-port-path problem. Its change consists of activating one specified equality row. The ordinary Boolean presentation can change one literal sign. In addition, any low-conflict port assignment outside the six locally recognizable copies of the starting pattern decodes to an approximately proper coloring of the input graph, in the precise sense below.

The statement charges a sequence of assignments. A deterministic RAM solving SAT may bypass that sequence, including on the native hard pair described below. The construction tests a proposed local repair step in a solver simulation. [Four-port problem and bit encodings](mst-forests-and-four-port-coloring.md)

## Put the input verdict in a different kind of coloring

Start with a simple graph $G$ of maximum degree four. Three-colorability on this class admits a bit-linear reduction from fixed-width 3-SAT, including the cost of printed names. Add a disjoint triangle and pad with isolated vertices to a square grid with $r=s^2$ sites, taking the least power-of-two side $s$ that fits. The size increases by at most a constant factor. Use the explicit expander on $(\mathbb Z/s\mathbb Z)^2$ with edges from $(x,y)$ to $(x+1,y),(x,y+1),(x+y,y),(x,y+x)$. Count edge occurrences, discard loops and keep parallel links as distinct. The maximum incident multiplicity is at most eight. There is an absolute $h>0$ such that every primary set $S$ has at least $h\min(|S|,r-|S|)$ crossing links. Replace each link by a path of length two through its own fresh midpoint. Call the graph together with the original edges $J$. These paths impose no restriction on the primary colors because the midpoint can avoid both its neighbors. Thus $J$ has the original coloring decision, is connected, contains an odd cycle, and has maximum degree at most twelve. [Explicit expander and proof](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.6296)

Form the tensor product of $J$ with the loopless three-clique. For each $v$ write its three colors as a fiber $t_v=(t_{v,0},t_{v,1},t_{v,2})$. A base edge $uv$ contributes the six inequalities $t_{u,i}\ne t_{v,j}$ for $i\ne j$. The coloring $t_{v,i}=i$ is public. Fix an ordinary base edge $ab$, for instance in the added triangle, and add the missing inequality $t_{a,0}\ne t_{b,0}$. This introduced edge is the only one the public coloring violates.

The type of a fiber is its permutation if its entries are all distinct, and a common seventh symbol otherwise. If the six inequalities on $uv$ hold and $t_u$ is a permutation, each $t_{v,j}$ has just one possible color, namely $t_{u,j}$. If neither fiber is a permutation, the colors repeated in the two fibers must be different. Indeed the two sets of repeated positions each have size at least two, so there are occurrences at different positions. Consequently compatible fibers have the same type. With the new edge active every proper coloring has nonpermutation fibers everywhere. If it had a permutation at any vertex, that permutation would propagate across the connected base and violate the new edge. Reading the repeated colors of a satisfying assignment therefore constructs a proper coloring of $J$. Conversely a proper coloring $c$ gives $t_{v,i}=c(v)$ and satisfies the new edge. The reduction also works on graphs for which computing an alternative coloring is hard even though the first coloring is provided. The uniqueness of the coordinate coloring of a product with a graph of chromatic number above three is classical. [Greenwell and Lovász](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01886093) · [Robust factorizations and colorings of tensor graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08913) · [Lean proof of the marked-edge equivalence](../lean/PlantedColorRepair.lean)

## Count errors in every intermediate assignment

Let $E$ count violated product inequalities **before** adding the marked edge. If the two primary endpoints of an expander link have different types then at least one of its two subdivided edges has incompatible fibers. Whichever fiber is assigned to the intervening midpoint, at least one physical product inequality fails. Different link occurrences have distinct midpoint edges, so every cut between types is charged at most once in $E$.

In particular let $B$ be the primaries with a nonpermutation fiber. Then
$$
             E\ \ge\ h\min(|B|,r-|B|).
$$
There is also a decoding statement. If $E<hr/2$ then some single type occupies more than half the primaries. Otherwise summing the boundaries of the seven type classes would count at least $hr$ incidences of bichromatic-type links, each at most twice. For the majority type, at most $E/h$ primaries differ from it. If it is a permutation they agree with **one and the same permutation** on all three coordinates wherever they match. If it is the seventh type, decode the repeated color at each matching primary and choose arbitrary colors at exceptional primaries. Among original edges of $G$ there are at most $E+4E/h$ conflicts: a conflict between two matching primaries already violates a product inequality, and at most four original edges meet each exceptional primary. This is a constructive alternative for every low-error assignment, including assignments that satisfy neither pin.

The public assignment starts with $|B|=0$ and every solution after activation has $|B|=r$. No bound on the number of moves or their computational cost enters the cut inequality. Once a physical representation ensures that each move changes at most a few primary fiber types, a path must encounter a cut of linear size.

## Keep the bound when the graph is printed as four-port paths

The active product graph has degree at most $D=25$, including the extra edge. Use the three-terminal equal-color splitters from the earlier reduction wherever the degree exceeds four. Give a separate terminal to each external incidence there. The splitters within one original vertex are disjoint from those of other vertices. Fix one representative for each original vertex. If all its internal edges are properly colored, every terminal agrees with its representative and every common color is attainable. For an arbitrary coloring, charge a vertex with inconsistent terminals to one bad internal edge. At most $D$ product edges meet any such vertex. Every decoded conflict whose terminals both agree with their representatives already violates its external edge. Thus at most $D$ times the total number of violated split-graph edges are violated in the decoded product. Adding a private leaf has no bearing on this estimate.

Realize the split graph by the balanced orientation and alternating port paths of the earlier note. At one endpoint of the marked edge its final degree before adding a leaf is three or four: originally the product vertex has odd degree at least three, and a splitter terminal for that incidence has degree three. Add a private leaf there if the degree is three. With final degree four and balanced orientation, the marked incidence can start the alternating path at that vertex. Write $\rho$ for its first equality row. Disabling just $\rho$ detaches the marked-edge port. This replaces the marked inequality in the quotient by an edge to a fresh leaf. All equality paths still have at most four ports. Lift the public product coloring through the splitters, color the optional leaf, and recolor the detached port differently from its partner on the marked edge. This supplies a legal six-valued assignment satisfying every row except $\rho$. Enabling $\rho$ restores exactly the active coloring problem.

For each path choose one port as representative, avoiding the detached port at the distinguished vertex. If a legal assignment violates $b$ rows counted with $\rho$ enabled, at most $b$ paths disagree internally. Every violated decoded inequality touches one such path, and each path has at most four ports. Thus
$$
                  h\min(|B|,r-|B|)\ \le\ E\ \le\ 4Db .
$$
A change of one six-valued variable changes only its two ports. Each can be the designated representative of at most one product coordinate and therefore affects at most one primary type. In any sequence of moves changing at most $r/6$ variables per move, some state has $r/3\le |B|\le2r/3$. It violates at least $hr/(12D)$ rows, even though the initial assignment violates just one. Without counting $\rho$ the lower bound decreases by at most one. Since the whole construction has $\Theta(r)$ records, both the permissible move size and the forced peak are linear in the number of records.

Swapping two colors on one connected component of their induced subgraph is a **Kempe change**. Even unlimited Kempe changes on proper colorings of the inactive quotient cannot prepare a solution of the active instance. In the product's coordinate coloring each two-color subgraph is the bipartite double cover of $J$. It is connected because $J$ is connected and a detour through its odd cycle allows either walk parity. For a split vertex, terminals carrying color $i$ stay connected by paths using $i$ and either chosen second color: one anchor of each splitter has that second color. Thus every bichromatic path in the product lifts to a bichromatic connection between the appropriate terminals in the inactive quotient. The pendant and detached leaves do not sever these connections. A Kempe change affecting a product terminal therefore acts on all product terminals with either of its two colors, merely permuting the coordinate coloring. Other changes affect only auxiliary vertices. Induction over any sequence preserves the planted type, up to a global permutation. This Kempe conclusion uses the stated splitter construction; the conflict counting needs only the decoding bound.

## Boolean encoding and work on genuine native inputs

Use three one-hot Boolean bits for each port. Within each six-valued variable, require both triples to be one-hot and forbid the same color at both ends. For each equality row use the three forward implications from one triple to the other. One-hotness makes them equivalent to equality. Guard the three implications for $\rho$ with $\neg z$ and assert the unit $z$ for the active problem or $\neg z$ for the inactive one. With $z=0$ the public assignment satisfies the inactive presentation and violates exactly one clause of the active presentation. The two formulas differ by a single sign. All clauses have width at most three.

Even temporary violations of the Boolean encoding do not bypass the cut bound. Decode each illegally encoded six-valued variable to a fixed legal pair. Every illegal variable costs at least one private local clause, and it meets at most four equality rows. On rows between legal variables a failed equality fails at least one implication, except possibly the single guarded row. Thus $b\le4b_{\rm CNF}+1$ for every Boolean assignment, where $b_{\rm CNF}$ counts violated clauses. Changing one Boolean variable affects at most one decoded six-valued variable. To require exactly three distinct literals, pad each binary clause with both signs of a private bit, and each ordinary unit with all four sign pairs on two private bits. A false original clause costs one false padded clause regardless of their values. To retain a **single changed literal** at the switch unit itself, make it one clause on $\pm z$ and two private reserve bits forced false by a constant exact-width gadget (seven clauses fixing a private triple to zero). These reserve bits occur only at this switch. Even cheating that switch can hide at most the one row already charged above. The one-error public start and the linear conflict bound both survive.

There is a quadratic quantity here with an explicit reason why it does not give quadratic running time. Sum the number of false clauses over *all* states of a repair path, including repetitions. If a move changes at most $a$ Boolean variables with $1\le a\le r/16$, each move changes $|B|$ by at most $2a$. Take the first state with $|B|\ge 3r/4$ and the last preceding state with $|B|\le r/4$. Strictly between them lie at least $r/(4a)-1$ states, each with $\Omega(r)$ false clauses for sufficiently large $r$. The sum is $\Omega(r^2/a)$ on every successful path. Given any satisfying assignment, writing the differing bits in batches of at most $a$ gives the matching $O(r^2/a)$ upper bound, since there are $O(r)$ variables and clauses. In particular for single-bit moves the optimum sum is $\Theta(r^2)$.

All variables have bounded occurrence in the resulting CNF, including the switch and the private padding bits. Given the two endpoint assignments one can maintain the exact set of false clauses on a single-bit path in $O(r)$ total RAM work: update only the clauses containing the changed variable, with a membership flag and list position for each clause. A clause can stay false for many states without any work being spent on it during those states. The quadratic quantity measures time-integrated error; the input does not supply the final solution, and finding it is the computational question.

Starting at fixed-width explicit 3-SAT with encoded length $N$, perform the earlier normalization of repeated clauses and name compression. All subsequent graphs, splitters, and CNFs have a constant number of records per vertex or edge of the bounded-degree output. They take $O(N)$ encoded bits and construction steps. A solution of the marked instance can be decoded to a coloring of the original graph, and then to a satisfying assignment of the original formula. This holds for every exact repair. If the emitted CNF has $n$ encoded bits, the examples with growing $r$ have $n=\Theta(r\log r)$. The linear conflict and move scales are measured in clauses or variables, hence are $\Theta(n/\log n)$ at this fixed width.

One can also apply the construction on the two **actual** native computations of clock $M=2^j$, giving the simulator random access to both the physical formula and the one-error public assignment. Start from the locally generated degree-four graph for each pin, with bounded slots in both directions. Use its padded identifier range as the primary grid, giving unused identifiers isolated vertices before overlay. The expander links and multiplicities have local arithmetic names; null loops can use isolated private dummy slots. All product endpoints and the constant-size degree-reduction pieces are addressable in $\operatorname{poly}(j)$ time. For this local implementation replace global Euler-tour orientation by the companion paper's constant 23-vertex fixture: exterior slots have predetermined directions, every profile has a balanced internal orientation, and a proper fixture imposes equal colors on its free terminals. Put the marked incidence at a free terminal, occupying the second slot there with a private pendant if needed. Order the resulting four ports to detach the marked one as above. Inconsistent fixtures can each be charged to a bad internal edge before decoding, costing one more constant degree factor in $E\le O(b)$. Only one representative is used per product coordinate. A table of honest fixture colorings for each terminal color supplies the public seed locally. Boundaries of reserved port, clause and padding slots are regular; inactive slots can instead encode isolated tautologically satisfiable checks with private variables. No computation of the native verdict enters the seed. [Local orientation, cost and native complementarity](locally-generated-four-port-refutations.md)

The two transformed active verdicts are opposite because the original native verdicts are opposite. Their separate activation switches merely expose the two public one-error starts. A fixed always-correct RAM solver at time $t_j$ on a given transformed pin can be run from the original stage and pin in $t_j\operatorname{poly}(j)+\operatorname{poly}(j)$ native work, answering each formula or seed read as requested. By the journal's almost-everywhere native lower bound this costs $\Omega_D(M)$ on both pins at every sufficiently large stage; the transformed solver needs $\Omega_D(M/\operatorname{poly}(j))$ even with the seed supplied. This quantifies the cost on genuine inputs without requiring them to be gap instances. Their physical lengths are $M\operatorname{poly}(j)$, leaving the bound below the linear milestone. A simpler Boolean construction can guard an input formula by selector bits constrained to agree on an expander. Flipping their common value already creates a conflict barrier under small changes. The graph product here lets the *observed colors themselves* identify which of seven types dominates and decodes a low-error nonpermutation assignment to the original graph. Gap hardness for reconfiguration between two given solutions and expander consistency checks have prior literature. Here the start has one error and the destination is any solution after a single physical pin. We have not established priority for the combined restricted theorem. An input whose original coloring is known can have exactly the same barrier: a RAM can directly write the coloring constant on each fiber or return a verdict without following a path. A lower bound on every unrestricted solver still requires an argument about its computation. [Hirahara and Ohsaka](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00474) · [Ohsaka](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14160)
