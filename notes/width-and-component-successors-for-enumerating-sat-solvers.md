# Larger SAT seeds after component and bounded-width preprocessing

There is a computable successor for a class of complete SAT solvers. Fix any satisfiable proper 3-CNF seed $B$ and a finite list of the solvers described below. The successor appends fresh proper clauses to $B$, producing a satisfiable file and a second file with one additional clause that makes it unsatisfiable. Every listed solver tries exponentially many complete assignments in the number of fresh variables on both files. A solver with a sufficiently large component cutoff can handle the old files in linear time. The next successor can include that solver in the list. The construction measures elapsed work through the number of complete assignments actually tested. The [five-variable census](../audits/single_support_seed_census.py) gives a smaller exact example for preprocessing confined to individual variable triples.

Each solver can decide and discard connected components of at most $R$ variables, derive clauses by resolution of width at most $w$, and use units and affine equations on at most $r$ variables certified by the derived clauses. To certify an equation it must exclude each violating tuple by its blocking clause or a subclause. Allow tautology removal, subsumption, pure-literal simplification and iteration of these operations. Here $R,r,w$ are fixed for the solver, with $w\ge3$. After preprocessing it tests complete assignments to the remaining variables in lexicographic order starting at all zeros or all ones. It may choose its variable order from the input. The enumeration tests candidates individually and stops at its first model or after the last candidate. The permitted component routine is exact. These are globally correct decision procedures on arbitrary proper files.

## Connected blocks with a unique model

Fix $K=4$. On $n$ vertices take $2K$ independent uniform permutations $f_j,g_j$. For every $i,j$ insert the clause

$$
     (\neg x_i\vee x_{f_j(i)}\vee x_{g_j(i)}).
$$

Add $Ln$ negative clauses on independently sampled uniform triples of distinct vertices. First choose a sufficiently small fixed $\epsilon>0$ depending on $K$, then a sufficiently large integer $L$ depending on $\epsilon$. For example, $\epsilon=1/16$ and $L=2^{19}$ suffice. All clauses admit zero. The following estimates give uniqueness even after deleting clauses whose vertex sets are pairwise disjoint.

Suppose the surviving formula admits a nonempty set $S$ of $j$ ones. At each head in $S$, at least $K-1$ original implication clauses must have a tail in $S$. Choose the exempt round and a successful tail in each remaining round. For one permutation, the probability that $a$ specified distinct heads all map into $S$ is $(j)_a/(n)_a\le(j/n)^a$, with falling factorial notation. Independence across permutations bounds the probability for fixed $S$ by $[K(2j/n)^{K-1}]^j$. Summing over $|S|=j$ gives $[eK2^{K-1}(j/n)^{K-2}]^j$. For $j\le\sqrt n$ the sum vanishes. For $\sqrt n<j\le\epsilon n$ choose $\epsilon$ so the bracket is at most $1/2$. For $j\ge\epsilon n$, each negative clause independently fails with probability at least $(\epsilon/2)^3$ for large $n$. Choose $L$ so that the mean number of failures is at least $16n$. A binomial lower-tail bound and a union bound over $2^n$ sets show that all these assignments violate more than $n$ clauses with probability tending to one. At most $n$ vertex-disjoint clauses can be deleted.

There is a fixed $\alpha>0$ such that, with probability tending to one, every $1\le m\le s=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor$ clauses span more than $7m/4$ distinct vertices. Put $q=\lfloor7m/4\rfloor$. Within a fixed $q$-set only $Kq$ head clauses are candidates. For any $a$ of these, the probability all tails lie inside is at most $(q/n)^{2a}$ by the permutation calculation. For every choice of $m-a$ independent negative clauses the corresponding bound is $(q/n)^{3(m-a)}$. Summing both choices, using $\binom{u}{v}\le u^v/v!$, bounds the probability of at least $m$ internal clauses by

$$
  \frac{[Kq(q/n)^2+Ln(q/n)^3]^m}{m!}
  \le \left[\frac{e(K+L)q^3}{mn^2}\right]^m.
$$

Multiplying by $\binom nq$ gives at most $[C(m/n)^{1/4}]^m$, since $m\le q\le7m/4$. One may use $C=128(K+L)$ and $\alpha=[512(K+L)]^{-4}$. Sum first to $\sqrt n$, then to $\alpha n$ with $C\alpha^{1/4}<1/2$. Both sums vanish.

In the incidence multigraph count two parallel incidences as a cycle, including when a tail repeats its head. With probability tending to one, the full vertex sets of the clauses in any two distinct cycles using at most 32 clauses each are disjoint. An overlapping pair would give a connected configuration of $e\le64$ clauses on $v\le2e-1$ vertices, since its cycle rank is $3e-(e+v)+1\ge2$. For each of finitely many marked types its expected count is $O(n^{v-2e})$. Indeed, choose the vertices in $O(n^v)$ ways. A head clause has a specified head and one of $K$ rounds. Its two tail images cost $O(n^{-2})$ per clause jointly across these boundedly many rows, by sampling without replacement in each permutation. Inconsistent prescriptions have probability zero. A negative clause has $O(n)$ index choices and costs $O(n^{-3})$ per prescribed triple. Delete one clause from each short cycle. All clauses are now proper and distinct supports meet in at most one variable. Every vertex retains both signs from its $K$ head occurrences and $2K$ tail occurrences, and the expansion bound persists.

Fix a successful outcome and take any connected component with $t$ variables. It has a unique zero model. It retains at least $(K-1)t$ clauses from its heads. If it had at most $s$ clauses this would contradict expansion. Taking any $s$ clauses gives $t>7s/4$, so $t=\Theta(n)$ and $s=\Theta(t)$. The entire original construction has only $(K+L)n$ clauses, hence the component has $O(t)$ clauses. All conditions on the final blocks are finite tests, so exhaustive search finds arbitrarily large examples.

## What resolution and affine tests can derive

Take four disjoint copies of the connected block. In two copies flip literal signs on any $\lfloor t/2\rfloor$ coordinates, and in the other two flip the complementary set. Each copy now has a nearly balanced unique model. Their unique joint assignment has exactly $2t$ zeros and $2t$ ones. For the negative file add a clause on one variable from each of three different copies, with all three literals false at that assignment. The incidence graph acquires no cycle from this bridge. Any $m\le s$ fresh clauses involve at least $13m/8$ variables. Without the bridge the stronger $7m/4$ bound applies. With the bridge and $m\ge14$ use $7(m-1)/4\ge13m/8$. For $1\le m<14$ the incidence subgraph is a forest and has at least $2m+1$ variables. At least $m/4$ variables occur exactly once in such a collection, by counting its $3m$ incidences.

For a non-tautological consequence $D$ of the fresh formula, let $\mu(D)$ be the smallest number of original clauses implying it. Use $\mu=0$ for tautologies. Every variable occurring once in a smallest witnessing collection must occur in $D$. Otherwise delete its clause, take an assignment satisfying the others and falsifying $D$, and adjust that variable to satisfy the deleted clause. Thus if $1\le\mu(D)\le s$, then $|D|\ge\mu(D)/4$. Resolution is subadditive in $\mu$, and weakening cannot increase it. If $8w\le s$, induction along a width-$w$ derivation gives $\mu(D)\le4w$ throughout. Two preceding witnesses together use at most $8w$ clauses, so the small-witness bound applies again. This is the boundary-variable argument used in classical resolution width lower bounds. [Ben-Sasson and Wigderson](https://people.inf.ethz.ch/emo/SatSem05/Papers/BensassonWidgerson01.pdf)

Every collection of at most $s$ fresh clauses has models spanning the entire affine space on its variables over $\mathbb F_2$. Each nonempty subcollection has a variable $x$ occurring only once. Remove its clause $E$ and apply induction to the other variables, including unused ones as free coordinates. Every remaining model extends by some choice of $x$. Some remaining model satisfies another literal of $E$, since a full affine span cannot fix that literal false. This model extends with both values of $x$. Projection and this pair together give full affine span. Consequently a width-bounded derivation as above cannot produce a unit or a contradiction. An affine equation on $a\le r$ variables requires blocking all $2^{a-1}$ violating tuples. Their derivation witnesses together have at most $2^{a-1}4w$ clauses. When this is at most $s$, full affine span contradicts the equation.

No new clause of width at most three can subsume an original clause. Such a derived clause has a witness of size at most 12 by the same boundary count, and its incidence subgraph is a forest. A forest witness with $m$ clauses has at least $m+2$ boundary variables. Thus $m=1$ for a non-tautological consequence of width three, and that consequence must be the original proper clause. All original polarities and large connected components therefore persist. The disjoint satisfiable old seed cannot improve a derivation on the fresh variables. Fix any model of the old part and restrict the derivation. Resolution under restriction gives a derivation of a subclause of the restricted conclusion within the same width, allowing weakening. The same restriction applies to an alleged mixed affine relation. Old-only simplifications leave the fresh components intact.

## Successive elapsed-time witnesses

Choose $t>R$ and $s$ above all the finite width and arity thresholds. Regardless of the final variable order, a model of the positive file has at least $2t$ bits opposite the starting polarity. The smallest binary rank of a word with $2t$ ones is $2^{2t}-1$. The positive scan therefore tests at least $2^{2t}$ assignments, and the negative scan exhausts its residual space. Even a variant that enumerates each large component separately with its own choice between the two starting polarities needs $2^{\lfloor t/2\rfloor}$ tests on a positive new component. On the negative input it must exhaust the unsatisfiable bridged component, since the other components are satisfiable. With the old seed fixed and fresh names dense above its largest identifier, both files have $N=\Theta_B(t\log t)$ encoded bits because the clause count is $O(t)$ and all $4t$ fresh decimal names occur. Choosing $t$ large enough beats any specified finite list of polynomial deadlines, including their constants. For the negative fresh formula itself, deriving the empty clause needs width $\Omega(t)$ by taking $w=\lfloor s/8\rfloor$. This gives resolution size $\exp(\Omega(t))$ by the cited width–size theorem.

Use the positive file as the next old seed. A new complete solver can set its component cutoff above the sizes of all components in the preceding pair. For fixed cutoff, intern variable names and partition the incidence graph in linear byte time, then use a finite decision table on the distinct clauses of each small component over local variable numbers. The constant may depend on the cutoff. This covers all clause orders, variable renamings and repetitions of the previous bounded-size components. The next larger blocks survive this routine. None of the rank bounds covers arbitrary branching, proofs outside the specified preprocessing, or a RAM that recognizes and uses the planted pattern. Alekhnovich, Hirsch and Itsykson established exponential lower bounds on satisfiable inputs for specified families of DPLL algorithms. Priority for the combined successor statement here is unestablished.
