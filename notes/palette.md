# How much asymmetry can one spare summary code introduce?

## 2026-11-23 · A stability question raised by the selector experiments

The short-summary decoder in [the selector experiment](local.md) requires that strict comparable inputs X⊂Y have h(X) ≰ h(Y). In particular every chain has distinct codes. At full utilization this forces h to depend on cardinality alone. With one spare code, an arbitrary partition of one middle layer is possible. I tested whether a quantitative form of the rigidity theorem survives between these extremes.

Let h be any labeling of subsets of [m] by at most m+1+q codes such that comparable distinct sets get different codes. The following results require only this last property. Hence they apply to all the summaries satisfying the decoder condition, including ones whose codes on different chains occur in different orders.

**Interval estimate.** Let P(A,B) be the set of codes used between A and B inclusive, for A⊆B. Define

    e(A,B) = |P(A,B)| − (|B∖A| + 1).

This is nonnegative because a maximal chain in the interval uses |B∖A|+1 distinct codes. For A⊆B⊆C,

    e(A,C) ≥ e(A,B) + e(B,C).

Indeed P(A,B) and P(B,C) intersect in exactly h(B). Any two points chosen from the respective intervals are comparable, so equal codes force them to be the same point, necessarily B. Taking the cardinality of their union proves the estimate.

**Consequence.** The integer p(X)=e(∅,X) is nondecreasing and takes values from 0 to q. If p(A)=p(B), then e(A,B)=0. Throughout such an interval h depends only on rank relative to A. To see this, every maximal chain uses the entire interval palette. Swap two neighboring insertions along a chain. The two chains agree at every position except the intervening rank, and at that position both must use the only remaining code. Single-element exchanges connect all sets at any fixed rank inside the interval.

In particular, if the two middle vertices of a diamond receive different codes, the top has strictly larger p than the bottom. For q=1 every such diamond straddles the *same* increasing set {X : p(X)=1}, which is determined by h. This permits defective diamonds at many different Hamming weights. Their positions across comparable intervals remain constrained.

**Exact charge along a chain.** Fix a permutation specifying a maximal chain. Call position i active when exchanging insertions i and i+1 changes the intermediate vertex's code. For each collection of disjoint active pairs, the corresponding two-step intervals have excess one, so the interval estimate bounds the size of the collection by q. Equivalently, make all those swaps simultaneously. Each replacement code was missing from the original chain, since it differs at its own slot and is comparable to every other slot. All replacements occur together on the new chain and must be distinct. The odd positions form a disjoint collection, as do the even positions. At most min(m−1,2q) positions on any chain are active. With one spare code, any two active positions must be consecutive.

**A sharp test and a failed inference.** Take m=2ᵇ−2, q=1, and designate one coordinate v. Supply as h(X) the b-bit unsigned binary expansion of

    m+1 − |X| − 1[v∈X].

It decreases strictly as integers along strict inclusions, so it meets the decoder condition. Every rank 1 through m−1 contains two distinct codes. The only active positions on a chain are the neighbors of the insertion of v. If that insertion is interior, there are exactly two. Thus both the chain bound for q=1 and the failure of global rank symmetry occur in the same O(m)-time summary. More generally, mark q coordinates and replace the indicator by their count. With isolated marked insertions away from the two ends, all 2q possible changes occur when m≥2q+1 and sufficiently many codes are available.

Moreover, for **any** monotone Boolean predicate g on the subsets, setting h(X)=m+1−|X|−g(X) when q=1 gives a legal summary in the same way. Knowing h and the cardinality recovers g. Consequently a one-boundary description by itself says very little about the computational cost of that boundary. A single distinguished coordinate already gives the extremal chain example cheaply. To use this rigidity for a RAM lower bound one would have to derive a particular expensive predicate from the SAT solver and charge its computation even when all intermediate results can be retained.

The interval estimate and sharpness arguments above are elementary manuscript results. `SwapCount.lean` checks the interval cardinality argument, the excess inequality with rank baselines, and the finite counting step for disjoint swaps. `palette_experiment.py` exhausts the marked-coordinate example on six bits and checks the interval estimate on it and on all middle-layer splits on four bits using six abstract codes. Priority of this formulation is undetermined.

**Final local result.** In every interval, a single vertex has at most 2q+1 differently labeled immediate extensions, where q bounds the interval excess. A greedy chain proves the bound below; the missing-type construction attains it for all q. The q=1 and q=2 arguments and the two weaker bounds record how this estimate was found.

## A sharp bound on the colors of immediate extensions when q = 1

Even when every defective diamond crosses the same increasing set, three different codes can occur at a single rank. Take b≥3 and m=2ᵇ−2. Choose a reverse linear extension of the Boolean order on the b-bit codes whose first element is the all-ones code and whose next three elements are distinct coatoms U,V,W (codes with exactly one zero). Use the top code on the empty set. Color the singletons with U,V,W, using all three. For each pair select a color among U,V,W absent from its two singleton subsets. Use the remaining m−2 codes, in extension order, one per rank 3 through m. There are exactly enough, since the whole palette has m+2 codes. Codes in the three-element block are mutually incomparable. Across blocks, an earlier code cannot be below a later code. This verifies the decoder condition on every strict inclusion. The three singleton colors preclude any fixed relabeling of a summary of the form rank plus a single Boolean predicate, which uses at most two values at any one rank. The increasing set obtained from interval excess still exists.

In fact **any one set A has immediate extensions of at most three different colors** when q=1. Suppose four distinct colors occurred on the sets formed by adjoining respectively i,j,k,l to A. Restrict to the four-dimensional interval from A to D=A∪{i,j,k,l}. It has at most six colors, since its excess is at most one by superadditivity inside the whole cube. Every lower two-step interval in this four-cube is defective: the two intermediate colors differ. Its excess therefore equals one. For any pair of chosen coordinates the interval from their two-element extension of A up to D must then have excess zero. Its two intermediate elements, both at relative rank three above A, have the same color. Any two three-element extensions in this four-cube share two chosen coordinates, so all four have a common color. It differs from each singleton-extension color because each singleton extension lies below some triple extension. We have now counted seven different codes: the code of A, four singleton-extension colors, the common triple-extension color and the code of D. This contradicts the six-color bound. The coatom construction attains three at A=∅. Order reversal gives the same cap on immediate predecessors.

The induction behind the four-dimensional argument gives a bound for every q. Write R(q)=(q+1)(q+2)/2. **At most R(q) distinct colors occur among the immediate extensions of any A.** An interval of excess zero has rank-dependent labels, giving R(0)=1. For the induction, choose r immediate extensions with distinct colors and restrict to their r-dimensional subcube above A. Its excess is at most q. Put p=R(q−1)+1. When r≤p the desired bound follows. Otherwise every bottom interval spanned by exactly p of the chosen coordinates has excess at least q, by the induction hypothesis applied to that interval. Superadditivity implies that its complementary upper interval up to the top of the r-cube has zero excess. For each relative rank j≥p+1, two j-sets differing in a single exchange share at least p coordinates. They lie in one of these zero-excess upper intervals at equal relative ranks, so their labels agree. Single exchanges connect that rank, giving one color across the entire rank. The r−p upper ranks use different colors along a chain, and each of their colors differs from every atom color since each atom extends to a vertex of that rank. Counting also A requires at least 1+r+(r−p) colors. At most r+1+q are available, giving r≤p+q=R(q). The same argument applies after reversing inclusions. The exact estimate below improves this inductive bound.

## Two spare colors and a general construction

For q=2 the immediate-extension cap improves from six to **five**. Suppose there are six distinctly colored extensions and restrict to the six-cube they span. The induction above, with p=4, forces relative ranks five and six to use one color each, disjoint from the six singleton colors and the bottom color. These nine colors exhaust the available palette. Every four-set at relative rank four must use a singleton color outside that set. Identify those six colors with coordinates. Orient each edge of the complete graph on the six coordinates towards the color chosen for the complementary four-set. The color of a three-set must lie in its complementary triple. All three edges in that triple correspond to comparable four-sets. A legal color for the three-set must therefore be a vertex that none of those three edges points towards. In particular the tournament has no directed triangle. The edge orientation is transitive: if a points to b and b to c, a must point to c. Order the vertices accordingly. Each four-set's color is the larger member of its complementary pair, and each three-set's color is the smallest member of its complementary triple. Finally fix a two-set with complementary coordinates a<b<c<d. Among its three-set extensions the colors include a and b, and among its four-set extensions they include b,c,d. Every available color outside the two-set is forbidden. This contradiction proves the improvement.

More generally, **2q+1 is attainable** for every q≥1 when sufficiently many bits are available. Choose b≥2q+1, m=2ᵇ−1−q and 2q+1 distinct coatoms immediately after the top in a reverse linear extension of the b-bit codes. Give the coordinates 2q+1 types, using all types, in some fixed total order on types. An empty input receives the top code. A singleton receives its type's coatom. At rank j from 2 through q+1, use the (j−1)-st type missing from the input in the chosen order. At least 2q+1−j≥j−1 types are missing. This color differs from every singleton color below it. Along an inclusion between two of these higher ranks, the set of missing types can only shrink, and the selected order statistic strictly increases in index. Hence the selected type strictly increases. The coatoms themselves are pairwise incomparable as bit vectors. Use one fresh code for each rank above q+1, in the remaining reverse linear-extension order. The total number used is 1+(2q+1)+(m−q−1)=m+q+1, and the summary satisfies the monotone-decoder criterion on every strict inclusion. It witnesses the five-color bound for q=2. This construction attains the greedy upper bound proved below.

## A linear upper bound by random chain counting

A second argument narrows the asymptotic gap. For q≥1 and r≥q+2 distinct singleton-extension colors, a necessary condition is

    1/q + 1/(q+1) + ... + 1/(r−2) ≤ 1.

In particular r≤eq+1, where e is Euler's number, since the sum bounds the integral of 1/x from q to r−1 from below. Select the r-cube witnessing the r different colors. For 2≤k<r let aₖ be the fraction of its k-sets carrying one of those r colors. At most q−1 other colors are available on these ranks, after reserving a distinct color apiece for bottom and top. A color class is an antichain. Its fractions across ranks sum to at most one, because this sum is the expected number of its members on a uniformly random maximal chain. Thus Σ aₖ≥(r−2)−(q−1)=r−q−1. This is the random-chain argument for the Lubell inequality. [Lubell (1966)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9800(66)80035-2)

For each distinguished singleton color i, all its occurrences at ranks k≥2 omit coordinate i. They form an antichain in the (r−1)-cube omitting i. The fraction of its k-sets of that color in the smaller cube is r/(r−k) times the fraction in the whole cube. Summing the r resulting random-chain bounds gives Σ aₖ/(r−k)≤1. Since 0≤aₖ≤1 and the weights 1/(r−k) increase with k, the least possible weighted sum with total mass at least r−q−1 is attained by filling ranks 2 through r−q first. This gives the claimed harmonic inequality. It recovers r≤3 for q=1 and r≤5 for q=2; for q=3 it gives r≤8, while the construction gives seven. These two counting estimates leave a gap of one at q=3.


## Exact upper bound from a greedy chain

Restrict to a subcube with r differently labeled atoms above a fixed A, one atom for each distinct color of an immediate extension. Its palette has at most r+1+q colors by interval superadditivity. Identify the r atom colors with their corresponding coordinates. The case r=0 is immediate. Begin a maximal chain at A and insert any first coordinate. At each subsequent step, if possible insert a coordinate that is still absent and whose atom color has never occurred on the chain so far. Stop this rule as soon as no such coordinate remains.

At the stopping vertex let S be the set of inserted coordinates and U the set of atom colors seen, under the identification above. The rule stopped because S∪U consists of all r coordinates. The first coordinate belongs to both sets. Every later inserted coordinate was unseen when chosen. Its own singleton vertex is strictly below the vertex at which it is inserted and every later vertex, so its color can never subsequently occur. Thus |S∩U|=1. Writing k=|S| and s=|U| gives r=k+s−1. Only k non-bottom vertices have been visited, so s≤k, whence s≤⌊(r+1)/2⌋.

Extend this chain arbitrarily to the top. Any atom color from a coordinate in S is forbidden at all subsequent vertices by strict comparability. Every atom color outside S is already in U. Thus the full chain still uses just s of the r atom colors. Its r+1 different labels omit at most q colors from the palette. In particular r−s≤q, yielding **r≤2q+1**. The reversed statement holds for immediate predecessors. Together with the construction above this gives the exact worst-case number for every q when enough coordinates and codes are available. For q=0 a rank labeling gives one.

This is an extremal theorem about proper colorings of comparable sets and a sharp example satisfying the stronger monotone-decoder condition. The examples compute their labels by counting element types and finding a missing type in sorted order. Deriving a lower bound for unrestricted RAM time from the color count would require an additional argument about the work of the SAT algorithm.

`SwapCount.lean` checks the interval palette inequality, the finite counting argument for independent swaps, the greedy overlap invariant under freshness and comparability exclusion, and the final arithmetic inference. The greedy chain construction, its extension to the top and the sharpness construction above are proved in prose.
