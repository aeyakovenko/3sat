# A quadratic check that stays hard after all outside coordinates are disclosed

Is the failure to add costs over time intervals caused by giving away the outside data? For linear code membership the conditional communication dimensions telescope. For a **single Boolean quadratic test** this explanation fails. At every node of every balanced binary hierarchy, some common actual input still requires communication proportional to all the bits on one side of the node. The sum of these requirements is a logarithmic factor larger than the data. The test itself takes linear word time on a RAM given its public arithmetic circuit. ## A linear-size circuit over a word-size field

Let $s$ be a power of two. Use a bounded-degree acyclic superconcentrator with $s$ sources, $s$ sinks and at most $D s$ edges and vertices combined, for a fixed $D$. For every two equally large sets of input and output ports there are that many vertex-disjoint directed paths between them, with no prescribed pairing. Valiant used linear-size superconcentrators to compute matrices with all minors nonsingular over the integers. Here both the bit width and the loss in submatrix rank matter. Use the field of $q=2^b$ elements with $b=\lceil 64\log_2((D+2)s)\rceil$; the field elements use $O(1)$ logarithmic words. Each nonsource node forms a linear combination of its incoming values. Write $A$ for the input-to-output matrix and $D_0$ for a separate diagonal matrix. Some choice of coefficients has the property that for **all disjoint** $I,J\subseteq[s]$ of size $k\ge1$,
$$
   \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb F_q} (AD_0+D_0 A^\top)_{I,J}\ \ge\ k/2 .
$$
The use of a binary field makes the sum in this expression the polar form of a single quadratic. The result is an existence statement at each scale. Good coefficients can be found by finite search and used as public data. No fast uniform search for them is assumed. [Valiant, *Graph-theoretic arguments in low-level complexity*, Theorem 4.2](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/spr08/cos598D/valiantrigidity.pdf) · [Jaggi et al. on path-frontier network coding](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/JaggiCJ03.pdf)

Choose every local coefficient and diagonal entry independently uniformly. Fix $I,J$ and route $k$ paths from sources $J$ to sinks $I$. Restrict the input to $J$. Track one row vector per path, initially the standard basis at the sources. Process the network in topological order. On a path replace the row of its previous vertex $v$ by the row of the new vertex, keeping the other $k-1$ tracked rows. Expose all incoming coefficients at the new vertex except the coefficient $\alpha$ on the chosen path edge, then expose $\alpha$. The new row is $\alpha v+u$ for an already determined vector $u$, writing $v$ also for its tracked row. If $v$ was dependent on the other rows, the rank cannot drop. Otherwise at most one value of $\alpha$ puts this row in their span: two distinct such values would put $v$ in the span. There are at most $Ds$ path updates, and each drops rank by at most one, with conditional probability at most $1/q$. The final rows constitute $A_{I,J}$. Other vertices can be evaluated in between without replacing a tracked row.

Now fix $A$, choose a basis of columns of $A_{I,J}$, and reveal the diagonal entries on $I$. Each basis column $v_j$ is replaced by $d_j v_j+u_j$, with $u_j$ the now fixed restricted column of $D_0A^\top$. The unrevealed $d_j$ are independent because $I$ and $J$ are disjoint. Replacing these basis columns one at a time has the same rank-drop bound, and the unselected columns can only increase the final rank. In the combined at most $(D+1)s$ trials, ending below $k/2$ requires at least $\lceil k/2\rceil$ drops. At any prescribed $a$ positions the probability of a drop at every one is at most $q^{-a}$ by conditioning, even though a trial depends on prior coefficients. Pad with zero-loss trials if necessary. The failure probability is at most $(((D+1)s)/q)^{\lceil k/2\rceil}$. There are at most $s^{2k}$ pairs to check for this $k$. The sum of these bounds over $k\ge1$ is less than one with the displayed $q$.

Fix such coefficients. Let $\lambda:\mathbb F_q\to\mathbb F_2$ return the low bit in a fixed polynomial basis. Define
$$
                 f(x)=\lambda(x^\top A D_0 x).
$$
A RAM given the topologically ordered circuit, weights, a degree-$b$ irreducible polynomial and $s$ raw field elements can compute $f$ in $O(s)$ operations. For ordinary integer-word instructions one can build tables for field products of pairs of short digits at each pair of digit positions. Use digits of at most $\max(1,\lfloor(\log_2 s)/4\rfloor)$ bits. There are constantly many positions since $b=O(\log s)$. Each table has $O(\sqrt s)$ entries. Construct each entry modulo the supplied polynomial in $\mathrm{polylog}(s)$ elementary steps, taking $o(s)$ total setup time for large $s$. A full field product then XORs constantly many table answers. Evaluating $AD_0 x$ and the final dot product uses $O(s)$ such operations. This runtime includes reading the coefficients but does not include discovering the good choice. The same evaluator works for every valid choice.

## The rectangle bound after conditioning

Fix disjoint $I,J$ as above, all outside values, and a proposed output $z$. If a nonempty rectangle $U\times V$ lies entirely in $f^{-1}(z)$, taking the four corners associated to any $u,u'\in U$ and $v,v'\in V$ gives
$$
             \lambda\big((u-u')^\top (AD_0+D_0A^\top)_{I,J}(v-v')\big)=0 .
$$
This holds because all internal and outside terms cancel. For a rank-$r$ field matrix the displayed bilinear pairing has binary rank $br$. Invertible row and column operations reduce the matrix to $r$ unit pivots. On one pivot, for every nonzero $a$, multiplication by $a$ is onto and $\lambda$ is nonzero, so the pairing $\lambda(ab')$ has no left kernel. Take the binary spans of differences from a basepoint of $U$ and of $V$. Orthogonality for a pairing of rank $br$ bounds the sum of the two dimensions by $2bk-br$. Consequently $|U||V|\le q^{2k-r}\le q^{2k-k/2}$ for either verdict, for *every* outside assignment.

In an ordinary deterministic communication protocol with a public output, each terminal transcript defines a monochromatic rectangle. The counting argument also applies to nondeterministic certificates with sound local verification and a publicly specified verdict, if all proof bits and communication bits are counted and the complete transcript determines a rectangle. Give the two parties the outside values and $z=f(x)$ for free, asking them to certify agreement with $z$ against all possible inputs. For each fixed protocol per pair (it can use the public outside data), the chance over uniform $x$ that there exists a successful transcript of fewer than $bk/8$ bits is at most $4q^{-3k/8}$. Condition first on the outside and take the union over transcripts and both possible $z$; the bound uses the *unconditioned* uniform distribution on the two private sides. Summing over at most $s^{2k}$ pairs for each $k$ shows that one $x$ defeats every such short transcript for every pair. The protocols must be specified before selecting $x$. A different common $x$ may be needed for a different solver.

## Consequence for an actual run on one explicit formula

Compile $f$ over the fixed parameters to a Boolean circuit. Each multiplication by a fixed field coefficient is a binary linear map on $b$ bits with $O(b^2)$ gates by direct expansion. The final bit of each product in the dot product is a binary bilinear form with $O(b^2)$ gates. The DAG has $O(sb^2)$ gates in all. Pin the $sb$ coordinate bits, impose the gate equivalences in width-three CNF, and assert output different from the public bit $z$. Only the input signs and output pin vary. Use fixed spacing so a physical input word depends on at most one field coordinate. This ordinary formula has bit length $O(sb^3)$ with binary identifiers. When $z=f(x)$ it is unsatisfiable. The gate values are forced by the pins.

For each correct deterministic SAT solver, for all sufficiently large $s$, there is **one** of these rejecting executions and a constant $c>0$ (depending only on the solver and layout) with the following property for *every* ordered pair of disjoint $k$-sets $I,J$ with $k\ge k_0$, for a solver-dependent constant $k_0$. Stop before the next instruction first observes a coordinate of $I$ after $\lfloor ck\rfloor$ distinct such coordinates have been observed, or at an earlier halt. On this run the cut is nonterminal. In the remaining suffix the number of distinct coordinates of $J$ read, plus the number of distinct work addresses read before a post-cut overwrite there, is at least $ck$. A read at a previously untouched zero address counts. All other input data, including the rest of the coordinates, are free in this count.

Indeed Bob knows the coordinates in $J$ and simulates the prefix, requesting a full $I$-coordinate and caching it on first use. After sending the fixed number of controller words he retains his private frozen RAM. Alice, who knows $I$, simulates the suffix, caches earlier values including zeros and supplies all post-cut writes locally. She asks Bob once per newly needed $J$-coordinate or old work address. This is an exact protocol for the claimed verdict on the entire input rectangle, including transcripts where the solver signals the opposite answer. It uses at most $K b(\lfloor ck\rfloor+1+u)$ bits, where $u$ counts the suffix objects. The additive controller and protocol framing can cost a full word, so first use the lower bound for $k\ge k_0$ with $k_0>32K$, choosing $c<1/(32K)$. The preceding common-input proof establishes the suffix assertion for **all $k\ge k_0$**. No assertion for smaller cuts is needed; a controller register alone can hold their information.

In particular, for every balanced binary hierarchy on the coordinates of that very input, apply the statement with the two children at each node of size at least $2k_0$. The resulting lower bounds on distinct objects *within their respective suffixes* sum to $\Omega(s\log s)$ incidences. There are only $s$ input words in field coordinates up to a constant factor. The same step or old word can satisfy requirements in different suffixes. On the raw word evaluator the same simulation and rectangle proof apply, and $O(s)$ total operations force some operations to receive $\Omega(\log s)$ charges. On the explicit formulas a general SAT solver can evaluate a recognized pinned acyclic gate layout in $O(sb^2)$ word steps; recognizing and checking such a layout is a separate cost from evaluating the raw arithmetic circuit. Neither count excludes linear time in encoded formula length.
