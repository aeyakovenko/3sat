# Which successor entries certify one SAT verdict?

The minimum-spanning-tree preprint counts maxima of fixed subsets under a common order. If one instead removes each reported maximum before asking for the next one, the same histogram is consistent with every permutation. How much of a real memory table has to be read to resolve even one bit of such an ordering? For a single cycle of successor pointers there is an exact answer. Three distinguished vertices already give one ordinary Horn SAT decision. Directed successor order is a classical problem studied by Etessami. Historical priority for the exact certificate counts here is unverified. [DLMST, Lemma 3.9 and Claim 3.12](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2026/339/download/) · [Adaptive batches and their histograms](few-writer-maxima.md) · [Etessami on ORD](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcss.1997.1485)

## All completions after an actual set of reads

There are $w$ labeled vertices. The entry at $i$ gives its successor $p(i)$, and $p$ is promised to be a single directed cycle. Fix $k\ge3$ distinct marked vertices with public names and ranks. A certificate on a specified cycle is a set of successor entries whose true values determine the requested answer among *all* single cycles. It may depend on the answer and the whole cycle. The cost counts distinct entries. The queried entries of a correct deterministic decision algorithm on any input form such a certificate, even with adaptive queries, arbitrary local computation, and unlimited workspace.

Read any proper subset of the entries. The known arcs form disjoint directed paths, including isolated vertices. Every completion amounts to putting these labeled paths in an oriented cyclic order and connecting the tail of each to the head of the next. Each cyclic order gives one completion. Write $r$ for the number of paths containing a mark. Exactly $(r-1)!$ cyclic orders of the marks are consistent with the entries. Each marked path contributes a nonempty ordered list of distinct labels. Any cyclic ordering of these lists can be extended by inserting the unmarked paths, and the resulting marked order determines the ordering of the lists. Moreover all the marked orders have equally many completions, by deleting the unmarked paths from a uniform cyclic permutation of all paths. With all entries known, take one block containing all marks.

Write $l_0,\ldots,l_{k-1}$ for the successive mark-to-mark arc lengths on the given cycle, so $l_i\ge1$ and $\sum l_i=w$. Cutting any nonempty collection of entries leaves as many marked blocks as there are distinct intermark arcs with at least one cut. Thus a certificate for the full cyclic order must include all entries of all but at most two arcs. Its exact minimum cost is $w$ minus the two largest lengths. Querying exactly the other arcs attains the bound.

## The parity of the marked order

Suppose $k=2t+1$. Reading the ranks in oriented cyclic order gives a well-defined permutation sign, since rotation by one place has even sign. The observations determine this **one bit** precisely when exactly one marked block has odd length in marks. Indeed exchanging two neighboring blocks of $a$ and $b$ marks changes the sign by $(-1)^{ab}$. If just one block is odd, every permutation of the blocks preserves the sign. If more than one is odd, choose two labeled odd blocks and interchange their positions. In any linearization, crossing the intervening marks contributes an even number of transpositions because the two sizes have even sum. The exchange flips the sign. It is a well-defined involution on cyclic block orders. The number of odd blocks is then at least three. Exactly half of the consistent cycles have each sign.

This also works after an adaptive algorithm has stopped. Fix its random coins. The full transcript fixes precisely the queried entries among possible promised inputs. Under a uniform random cycle the sign is still a fair bit on every transcript that leaves at least three odd blocks. Consequently an algorithm with success probability $1/2+\delta$ on this distribution must reach a deciding set of entries with probability at least $2\delta$.

**Exact cost.** Give the cycle on the $k$ marks edge weights $l_i$. The least certificate size for the sign is the minimum weight of a matching that leaves just one mark unmatched. In each even marked block pair consecutive marks; in the single odd block leave an endpoint unmatched and pair the rest. The arcs within these pairs are all fully known. This proves the lower bound, including the fully observed case by breaking the order at an arbitrary mark. Conversely, reading precisely the arcs in any such matching leaves blocks of two marks and one singleton. This certifies the sign.

The largest such minimum over cycles with $w$ entries and $k$ marks is exactly $\lfloor wt/k\rfloor$. There are $k$ matchings, one for each unmatched mark, and each edge occurs in $t$ of them. Their average weight is $wt/k$. For equality arrange the lengths in step-two order by taking $l_{2i\bmod k}=\lfloor(i+1)w/k\rfloor-\lfloor iw/k\rfloor$ for $0\le i<k$. The edge indices in any matching are $t$ consecutive indices in this cyclic step-two order. Its cost is therefore either the floor or ceiling of $wt/k$, also for a wrapping interval by periodic extension. All lengths are positive since $w\ge k$.

## Finding the certificate on a uniform input

Suppose a query gives one entire successor entry of the solver's choice. In any partial transcript with $c>1$ path fragments, each unexposed entry is a path tail. On a uniformly random single cycle its unknown successor is uniform over the starts of the other $c-1$ fragments. This follows from the uniform cyclic ordering of *all* fragments, including unmarked ones. Repeated queries provide no information.

For the sign let $o$ count the odd fragments. On querying an odd tail there is probability $(o-1)/(c-1)$ that the merged fragment count of odd parity drops by two. On any other outcome, including all queries from an even tail, $o$ is unchanged. All fresh queries decrease $c$ by one. These transitions give an exact optimum over arbitrary zero-error adaptive strategies. The largest attainable expected number of fragments remaining at decision is $c A_o$, where
$$
          A_1=1,\qquad A_o=\prod_{j=3,5,\ldots,o}(j-1)/j .
$$
At $o=1$ one can stop. Induct on $c$. The expected continuation from an odd tail equals $(c-1)A_o+(o-1)(A_{o-2}-A_o)=cA_o$. From an even tail it equals $(c-1)A_o$, strictly smaller. Initially all $w$ vertices are separate and $o=k$, so the optimal mean number of distinct queries is $w(1-A_k)$. Since $A_{2t+1}=4^t/((2t+1)\binom{2t}{t})=\Theta(1/\sqrt{k})$, this mean approaches the full table as the odd number of marks increases. The maximum certificate cost proved above approaches only half of the table.

For full cyclic order the terminal count is two marked fragments. Querying a marked tail merges two marked fragments with probability $(r-1)/(c-1)$. With $r\ge2$ the identical induction gives optimum expected remaining count $2c/r$ because $(r-1)(2/(r-1)-2/r)=2/r$ for $r>2$. A merge changes $r$ by at most one, so the stopping boundary is $r=2$. The initial optimal mean is $w(1-2/k)$ queries.

The exact deterministic worst-case query count is $w-2$ for either task. With at most two fragments both answers are settled. Against a solver, if an even fragment exists, answer any odd-tail query by joining it to an even fragment. A query from an even tail always preserves $o$. If all $c$ fragments are odd with $c>3$, a forced merge leaves $c-2\ge3$ odd fragments because $c$ is odd. This keeps the sign ambiguous up to three fragments. For full order the same adversary absorbs an unmarked fragment whenever one exists, and otherwise all fragments are marked. It can leave three marked fragments at $c=3$. Each adversarial reply has a single-cycle completion.

For three marks the exact optimal success probability of a possibly randomized algorithm using at most $q$ distinct queries on a uniformly random cycle is
$$
  1-\frac 12\,\frac{\binom{w-1-q}{2}}{\binom{w-1}{2}}
                      \quad(0\le q\le w-2).
$$
Before resolution there are three odd fragments. At $c$ fragments a query has resolution probability at most $2/(c-1)$, attained from an odd tail. Multiplying the successive survival factors gives the binomial ratio. Guessing the unresolved bit succeeds with probability exactly one half.

## One ordinary formula, with three marks

Take distinct marks $a,b,c$. Use variables $X_i$, two flags $G_+,G_-$ pinned true and false respectively, and pins $X_a,\neg X_b$. For each row $i$ add
$$
       \neg X_i\lor\neg G(i)\lor X_{p(i)},\qquad
       G(i)=G_-\ \text{if }i=c,\quad G(i)=G_+\ \text{otherwise}.
$$
These are Horn clauses of width at most three. Starting at $a$, follow the pointers until reaching $b$ or $c$. If $b$ comes first, the implications force $X_b$ and the formula is unsatisfiable. If $c$ comes first, take as true exactly the vertices on the walk through $c$ and satisfy the formula. Thus the two possible cyclic orders have opposite SAT verdicts. Keeping the disabled row at $c$ exposes its successor in the same format as every other pointer. The field is accessible even to a solver exploiting the full cycle promise.

For this single decision the exact promise certificate size at every input is $\min(l_0,l_1,l_2)$. Equally spaced marks require $\lfloor w/3\rfloor$ distinct successor entries, including on a rejecting input. With binary variable identifiers the explicit length is $L=\Theta(w\log w)$. Each row has a single data field of $\Theta(\log w)$ bits and a machine word touches at most constantly many rows. If a RAM never examines any word touching a differing successor, its deterministic execution is unchanged. The lower bound therefore gives $\Omega(w)$ native steps on the balanced inputs, including for solvers that are correct on arbitrary CNFs. Certificates here expose whole successor entries. The reduction charges probes to physical words with only a constant-factor loss.

The worst-case discovery bound also gives a rejecting cycle and an accepting cycle on which at least $w-2$ distinct successor entries must be investigated in the one-entry probe model. Just before the adversary's final forced query both verdicts remain possible. One physical read intersecting several fields may count for several entries in a packed serialization. Constant public spacing can ensure each physical word intersects at most one varying field.

There is a matching $O(w)$-step algorithm on this layout. Check the signs, pins, flags, variable names and row shapes in order, allowing any in-range successor. Starting at $a$, follow at most $w$ links, testing for $b$ and $c$. Exhausting the clock without hitting either implies a repeated vertex, which makes $b$ unreachable and permits the reachable vertices to be the true set. This handles malformed cycles that nevertheless have the prescribed clause format, and a general solver handles other formulas. The scan uses constant writable words. Even exact information about the unresolved SAT bit after every possible partial observation only reaches scan time on this family. The unrestricted superlinear-time milestone is open.

## Encoding the growing number of marks in one SAT bit

For $w$ a power of two, use $d=\log_2 w$ bits $D_i$ at every vertex. Pin $D_a=0$ at one marked vertex and require $D_{p(i)}=D_i+1\pmod w$ for every row. Each row computes the increment from its own $D_i$ bits with $O(d)$ bounded-fan-in gates and equates the outputs to named bits of $D_{p(i)}$. On a single cycle the unique solution is the forward distance from $a$. For each pair of marked vertices in increasing *public rank* order, compare their distances and XOR all inversion bits. Require either selected sign. This is one SAT verdict since the gate values and distances are uniquely forced on the promise. Rotation invariance permits any starting mark.

Acyclic increment, comparison and XOR gadgets and the row equalities translate to $O((w+k^2)d)$ clauses of width at most three, hence $O((w+k^2)\log^2 w)$ encoded bits. For example $k$ may be any growing odd integer at most $\sqrt w$. Only the target variable names in row $i$ depend on $p(i)$; public spacing between rows ensures each input word depends on at most one successor. A probe of any such word is simulated by one query for the corresponding whole successor, cached thereafter. Thus every deterministic correct SAT solver needs to touch an average of at least $w(1-A_k)$ distinct successor rows on this distribution for either choice of output pin fixed publicly. In fact the same lower bound holds **conditional on rejection alone**, and conditional on acceptance alone. Let $T_*$ be the first number of distinct queried successors at which the observed whole entries determine the sign. For each $q$, the event $T_*>q$ is a disjoint union of undecided transcripts of the query strategy. On each transcript the two signs are equally frequent under the uniform cycle. At the empty transcript they are equally frequent too. Thus $T_*$ has the same distribution conditional on either sign as it does unconditionally. The algorithm cannot halt before that time. This argument uses the earliest decisive whole-entry transcript even when the physical solver has only inspected part of those entries or continues working after the sign has become determined. No hypothesis on the solver's workspace appears. Even growing $k$ does not make that count exceed $w$.

There is a direct evaluator on this second family too. Validate the clause template, the alignment of all target names in a row, and all public gates in time linear in the number of clause records. The modular increments force every directed cycle of the total successor function to have length divisible by $w$. With $w$ vertices this demands one cycle through them all. Verify that promise by following the pointers from $a$ and checking the first return time, using a $w$-step limit. When the check passes, the traversal supplies the distances, and the public pair tests supply the sign in $O(k^2)$ additional steps. A general SAT solver handles inputs outside the template.
