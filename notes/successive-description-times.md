# Many successive improvements in the shortest description of one word

**Research journal, 24 November 2026.** Suppose explicit SAT has a deterministic linear-time algorithm on the specified logarithmic-word RAM with unrestricted workspace. At **every sufficiently large length** $m$ there is then an $m$-bit word $y$ whose shortest available description gets strictly shorter at least $c m/\log m$ times between $2^{3m}$ and $2^{4m}$ steps. The word has a description of length at most $3\lceil\log_2(m+2)\rceil$ by the final deadline. One fixed algorithm computes such a word from $m$. The changes occur on the same word in disjoint, prescribed time intervals. There is also a version in which the measured description lengths recover an arbitrary binary pattern from a set of density tending to one.

Write $K_s(y)$ for the shortest exact program producing $y$ by time $s$ on any fixed efficient literal prefix-universal stream machine $V$ of the journal, with infinity if none qualifies. For a finite clock interval let $J_V(m,y)$ count the strict decreases of $K_s(y)$ as the integer time $s$ passes from $2^{3m}$ to $2^{4m}$. A first decrease from infinity counts once. The linear-SAT hypothesis implies

$$
\liminf_{m\to\infty}\ \frac{\log_2 m}{m}\max_{\substack{|y|=m\\K_{2^{4m}}(y)\le 3\lceil\log_2(m+2)\rceil}} J_V(m,y)>0.
$$

Thus a zero lower limit on even one such machine would exclude linear SAT. Vereshchagin and Shen relate decompression-time profiles at Busy Beaver deadlines to stochasticity profiles. Here the deadlines are explicit, vary by only a polynomial factor per measurement, and lie inside one specified exponential-time interval. I have no proof of a violating upper bound. [Vereshchagin and Shen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.04950) · [Native model](time-by-length.md)

## Counting histories without recursion on the number of clocks

Put $b=\lceil\log_2(m+2)\rceil$, $W=12b$, $S_j=2^{3m+Ajb}$ and $k=\lfloor m/(Bb)\rfloor$. There are fixed integer constants $A$ sufficiently large and $B\ge\max(200,2A)$, so that $S_{k+1}\le2^{4m}$ for all sufficiently large $m$. Our tests at stage $j$ look at a word's prefix and a list of conditions of the form $K_{S_i}(y)<u_i$ or $K_{S_i}(y)\ge l_i$ for $i<j$. There are at most $O(m)$ tests and all thresholds are at most $m+1$. To count such words in a given prefix, ask for at least $v$ of them using a sorted, distinct, existential list of at most $2^m$ words. A universal selector chooses a row. For that row supply transcripts of timely programs for all required upper tests. Universally rule out every valid counterexample transcript to any of the lower tests by selecting a test index and proposed transcript. Invalid selector indices create no obligation. The Boolean matrix checks the actual RAM operations, memory reads and publications as in the bounded native verifier. It connects the selected word to the table and checks its prefix. The existential upper transcripts can all be given together. This uses a **fixed** number of quantifier blocks independent of $j$. There is no nested call to the previous stage's word selector.

The list occupies $O(m2^m)$ bits, and the transcript blocks and checks together have size at most $S_{j-1}\,\mathrm{poly}(m)$ since $\log S_{j-1}=O(m)$. Polynomially many threshold queries do binary search for a count or unrank a word by extending its prefix. Under linear SAT the fixed-block elimination and native simulation therefore take $(2^m+S_{j-1})m^{O(1)}$ time, uniformly over $j\le k+1$. One fixed algorithm handles $A,B$ as numeric parameters; the exponents for constructing the matrices and checking a fixed number of blocks are independent of their fixed values. A sufficiently large $A$ pays for compilation and all these steps before $S_j$. The base $S_0$ needs no such computation. ## A simple word at every length

In this argument take $L_j=m-3jW$ for $1\le j\le k$. Let the initial pool consist of all $m$-bit words with $K_{S_0}(y)\ge m-W$. Fewer than or equal to $2^{m-W}$ words fail that test. Given a stage index $j$ and a rank, a single decoder selects that rank in the pool consisting of the initial condition and all earlier conditions

$$L_i-W\le K_{S_i}(y)<L_i\qquad(1\le i<j).$$

It reads a self-delimiting binary code for $m$ of length at most $2b+1$, a $b$-bit stage index, and exactly $L_j-5b$ rank bits. Including the fixed interpreter header its name is strictly shorter than $L_j$ eventually. All field sizes are computed before reading the field, and all pools are ordered lexicographically. Negative field lengths or out-of-range ranks can diverge. The parameters ensure $L_k>m/2$ for sufficiently large $m$.

Put $N_j=2^{L_j-5b}$. If the pool has at least $N_j$ words, the decoder gives $N_j$ distinct members a program shorter than $L_j$ by $S_j$. Among them at most $2^{L_j-W}$ can have $K_{S_j}<L_j-W$. At least $N_j/2$ enter the next pool. Since $N_{j+1}\le N_j/2$ and the initial pool covers $N_1$, induction carries all $k$ conditions on a common nonempty set. The pruning refers to the indicated **past** clock only. A surviving word can still get a shorter description later. The intervals of allowed description lengths at successive sample times are disjoint and decreasing, including the initial lower cutoff.

At $S_{k+1}$ a fixed decoder with only the self-delimiting parameter $m$ selects the first word meeting all $k$ conditions. Its complete length is eventually at most $3b$ and its output has every prescribed decrease. This proves the lower-limit assertion. If a direct exhaustive simulation replaces fixed-block elimination, one can enumerate all names of length at most $m$ through $S_{j-1}$, store the required publication records and compute all the counts in $2^m S_{j-1}\,\mathrm{poly}(m)$ time when $\log S_{j-1}=O(m^2)$. Taking $S_j=2^{3m+2jm}$ therefore gives the same number of decreases unconditionally, with a final time of order $2^{O(m^2/\log m)}$. That computation does not fit the narrow grid above.

## Encoding a pattern in the measured lengths

For $P\in\{0,1\}^k$ restrict outputs to the prefix $P$ and put $L_{j,P}=m-(3j+P_j)W$ with indices $j=1,\ldots,k$. The two possible length bands at a given stage are disjoint, so the sampled lengths of a successful word determine $P$ without reading its output bits. Include $P$ in each stage name, using exactly $k$ bits after the parameter fields. Take $N_{j,P}=2^{L_{j,P}-k-5b}$ rank choices. Distinct prefixes allocate disjoint sets of output words. The base pool in each prefix has size $2^{m-k}$ before the initial cutoff. The fraction of prefixes losing more than half their initial pool is at most $2^{1-W}$.

For a fixed $j$ and a fixed value of $P_j$, the thresholds $L_{j,P}=L$ are the same. The total number of words across *all* these prefixes with $K_{S_j}(y)<L-W$ is at most $2^{L-W}$. Consequently at most $2^{k+5b+1-W}$ prefixes in that group can lose more than half their stage quota to this lower cutoff, regardless of their earlier history. There are two groups at each stage. Outside a fraction at most $2^{1-W}+2k\,2^{5b+1-W}$ of all patterns, none of these losses exceeds half. For all such $P$ the preceding induction works because $L_{j+1,P}\le L_{j,P}-2W$. A final selector with parameters $m,P$ prints the first survivor by $S_{k+1}$ on a name of length $k+O(\log m)$.

Removing the patterns with unbounded prefix complexity $K(P)<k-5b$ loses at most a further $2^{-5b}$ fraction. From the output length one recovers $k$, hence from the word one recovers its first $k$ bits. For the remaining fraction $1-O(m^{-5})$, each selected word has unbounded complexity at least $k-O(\log m)$ and timely final complexity at most $k+O(\log m)$. At each sufficiently large $m$ this gives at least $(1-O(m^{-5}))2^k$ distinct measured profiles on words whose final descriptions have that length bound. A family of single late releases, or separate words at all stages, does not establish this joint profile assertion.

## An unconditional upper construction at the same order

One can make $\limsup_m (\log_2 m/m)\max_{|y|=m}J_V(m,y)$ arbitrarily small across a family of efficient literal universal machines. Fix an integer $g\ge1$. On the new machine allow publication only at global epochs. Put $e_0=4$ and $e_{u+1}=e_u+g\lceil\log_2(e_u+2)\rceil$. One epoch ends when $2^{e_u}$ virtual simulation ticks have elapsed since startup. If the underlying interpreter has already halted, keep its result and consume no further input until that epoch ends. The new machine preserves its exact domain and output map.

Here epochs can be synchronized in *physical* time, even for runs that have already finished. To make this explicit, execute one tick as a fixed-length simulation of one instruction of a chosen native reference interpreter, with an idle path of the same length. Simulated values have the stipulated width at their virtual tick. Cache the width and mask, updating them at the predetermined ticks when the width changes. The physical width at tick $s$ accommodates the virtual width at $s$ and a constant number of extra address bits after a fixed startup and a constant slowdown. For example, disjoint address prefixes or an even/odd address map separate virtual memory and wrapper state. The specified native instructions on one masked word have constant-size physical simulations when the widths and mask are supplied. Guard a bounded shift against the virtual width and mask its result. One-way bit reads and buffered output writes occur only on the active instruction path. Equalize the finite branch lengths with idle instructions. At a width change or epoch boundary the wrapper may use additional deterministic maintenance time depending only on the virtual tick, polynomial in its logarithm. Upcoming epoch counters and thresholds require $e_{u+1}+1$ bits at tick $2^{e_u}$, which fit in the native width $4\lceil\log_2(s+2)\rceil$ eventually for each fixed $g$. Small initial cases can be hardwired. Final publication is the common halt operation on the already prepared output buffer.

The physical epoch times are thus $t_u=C2^{e_u}+\mathrm{poly}(e_u)$, where the cumulative maintenance at the $O(e_u)$ width changes and epochs up to this point is absorbed by the polynomial. The constants may depend on the fixed wrapper. In particular a native computation taking $s$ ticks finishes within $O_g(s(\log(s+2))^{g})$ physical time after adjusting the fixed constants: the next virtual epoch is at most $2^g(e_u+2)^g$ times the preceding one. Composing with the reference interpreter's fixed compiler preserves the required polylogarithmic overhead for literal universality.

Between physical times $2^{3m}$ and $2^{4m}$, consecutive interior epoch exponents differ by at least $g\log_2 m-O_g(1)$ on the physical log scale. Thus **every** word of length $m$ has at most $1+(m+O_g(1))/(g\log_2 m-O_g(1))$ decreases there. Taking $g$ large makes the upper coefficient arbitrarily small, while $g$ stays fixed on each machine. This matches the order in the conditional assertion and rules out any uniform positive coefficient independent of $V$.

Making a common publication schedule much sparser also has an unconditional cost. A fixed native machine reads a unary delay field $1^L0$ before publishing. It can first read a self-delimiting $m$ and publish $0^m$ after this field. For $m\le L$, its native time is $O(L)$ and its exact program consumes at least $L$ bits. Its literal compiler on any efficient universal stream machine must therefore publish between $L$ and $CL(\log L)^q$ for fixed $C,q$ and all large $L$. Otherwise it either did not consume the specified name or exceeded its compiler time bound. For $2^{3m}\le L\le2^{4m}$ this implies a log gap of at most $O(\log m)$ between successive possible publication times in the interior, even just for the word $0^m$. For instance choose starting times geometrically separated by a factor exceeding $2C(4m)^q$ and retain the resulting disjoint allowed intervals inside this range. This gives $\Omega(m/\log m)$ different publications. Those delay-field names are extremely long and yield no corresponding count of shortest-description improvements. A separating upper bound would have to control improvements on actual words more selectively than global clock sparsity.

## An unconditional lower bound on the very same statistic

Direct enumeration can stay inside $[2^{3m},2^{4m}]$ if only the relevant program lengths are enumerated. It gives, at **every sufficiently large $m$ on every efficient native machine**,

$$
\max_{\substack{|y|=m\\K_{2^{4m}}(y)\le 3b}} J_V(m,y)
       =\Omega\!\left(\sqrt{m/\log m}\right).
$$

Set $L=\lfloor\sqrt{mb}\rfloor$, keep $W=12b$, take $k'=\lfloor L/(8W)\rfloor$, and use $L'_j=L-3jW$. Restrict the candidate words to $0^{m-L}z$ with $|z|=L$. Take $R_j=2^{3m+j(L+Ab)}$, where $A$ pays a fixed simulation overhead. At $R_0$ remove the words with $K_{R_0}<L-W$. For each later selection all relevant tests now ask only about names of length at most $L$. Enumerate all $2^L$ length-$L$ input streams to the preceding clock, stopping any run that would read beyond these bits. A timely halt on a shorter prefix is recognized by its consumed length. Record its output and the earliest completion at each relevant length cutoff. Sort or index these records and scan the $2^L$ candidate words. Each step of the simulation uses at most $\mathrm{poly}(m)$ physical work, since the virtual clock has $O(m)$ bits in this range. Costs for tracking the output length, retaining at most $m$ output bits per trial and doing the stage tests are absorbed in this polynomial. This computes the ranks in $R_{j-1}2^L\mathrm{poly}(m)$ time, which fits before $R_j$. Equivalently one can simulate each finite name separately, using fewer than $2^{L+1}$ trials.

With quotas $2^{L'_j-5b}$ the same pruning proof gives a word surviving all $k'$ strict decreases. At $R_{k'+1}$ select the first survivor using only $m$ as a parameter. This requires at most $3b$ program bits eventually. The clock calculation is $(k'+1)(L+Ab)\le m$ for all sufficiently large $m$, since $L^2/(8W)\le m/96$ and $b=o(L)$. Hence these are genuine successive improvements in the original time interval. This also rules out an upper-bound strategy that allows only a bounded or polylogarithmic number of improvements for such simple words. The unconditional count still falls short of $m/\log m$.

Even a global bound on the union of the short-word improvement times would discard important information. For a simpler example, take clocks $2^{3m+Ajb}$ with $A$ fixed large and $j$ ranging up to $\lfloor m/(2Ab)\rfloor$. A decoder with parameters $m,j$ simulates all programs of length at most $3b$ through the previous clock, then prints the first $m$-bit word they missed. Fewer than $2^{3b+1}$ names need simulation, and a missing word can be found among the first $2^{3b+1}+1$ strings in lexicographic order. Encode $m$ by first giving the binary length of $m$ self-delimited and then its binary digits, at a cost of $b+O(\log b)$; give $j$ in $b$ bits. The resulting name has length less than $3b$ and finishes by the next clock. Each interval therefore has a distinct newly short word, unconditionally, on every sufficiently large length. The stronger conditional count asks how many changes belong to one word.

The square root also identifies the cost of this particular exhaustive construction. If a proposed $k$-stage version reserves $W$ bits between successive program cutoffs, the sum of the positive cutoffs it enumerates is at least $W k(k-1)/2$. Giving each stage time for a separate run through the preceding clock on every such name spends this many bits in the logarithm of the combined clock ratio. The available logarithmic ratio is $m$. With $W$ of order $\log m$, this budget allows at most $O(\sqrt{m/\log m})$ stages. This calculation is specific to full enumeration at each stage; the fixed-block algorithm under the linear-SAT hypothesis pays only a polynomial factor per stage.

A [subsequent adaptive construction](adaptive-description-times.md) improves the unconditional bound at these same clocks to $\Omega(\sqrt m)$ by using the actual new program arrivals to encode the next stage.
