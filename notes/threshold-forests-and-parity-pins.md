# What can a minimum spanning forest tell us about a pinned parity system?

Khanna, Putterman and Song use a minimum spanning forest to preserve connected components at every weight threshold. To test this idea for SAT, take Boolean variables on edges and impose even parity at every vertex. A query fixes some edge bits and asks whether they extend to a solution. Even supplying the unique forest, the entire history of components, the degree of every labeled vertex at every threshold, and the dimension of the parity solution space at every threshold can leave one independent answer bit per seven vertices. Each distinguishing query fixes just **two edges that are present with the same endpoints in every graph**. ## A seven-vertex example

Start with the path on vertices 1 through 7. Give its six edges weight zero. There are three additional edges of weight one, recorded in three ordered slots.

| Block | First edge | Middle edge | Last edge |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 13 | 35 | 57 |
| B | 15 | 35 | 37 |

Here 35 denotes the edge between vertices 3 and 5. In A the three triangles are edge-disjoint. In B there are four internally vertex-disjoint paths from 3 to 5, namely the direct edge, the path through 4, the path through 2 and 1, and the path through 7 and 6. Both blocks have nine edges and seven vertices. Their degrees agree at each labeled vertex, including at 3 and 5. They have the same unique minimum spanning tree, and the space of even edge sets over the two-element field has dimension three.

For either graph the tree-edge values in every solution take the form $(a,a,b,b,c,c)$ in path order. For A the chord values are $(a,b,c)$. For B they are $(a,a+b+c,c)$, with addition modulo two. These formulas follow from degree-two parity along each path and the remaining parity equation at vertex 3. Every triple $(a,b,c)$ gives a solution. Consequently the set of possible observations on the **entire tree** is identical in the two graphs. So is the set of possible observations on **all chords**, viewed in slot order. Even the two separate probability distributions under a uniformly random solution agree. Even the joint projection retaining every tree edge and the two outer chords agrees, since the outer chord bits are always $a$ and $c$. The middle chord carries the distinction.

Pin the common edge 34 to one and the common chord 35 to zero. In A both belong to the middle triangle and must be equal. In B choose $(a,b,c)=(1,1,0)$ and the pins extend. No query fixing a single edge can distinguish the two blocks at the final threshold. Every edge belongs to some cycle, and the all-zero solution is available. Incidence lists of the discarded edges would of course identify the block.

## Exact cost of storing the missing information

For $g$ blocks choose A or B independently and join successive blocks by a weight-zero bridge from 7 to the next 1. The unique minimum spanning tree is one labeled path. Give the three chords of block $i$ the common positive weight $i$ to expose the blocks one at a time. Every block string has identical components, per-vertex degrees and parity-space dimension at each weight threshold. After $h$ whole blocks have been exposed the dimension is $3h$. Under any common slot order that puts the tree first, every intermediate prefix also has the same components and dimension across strings. If the middle chord 35 is inserted first within each block, the separate tree and chord projections even agree at every intermediate prefix. Indeed absent chords have value zero; once the middle chord is present it can absorb the change from $b$ to $a+b+c$ whenever either outer chord is inserted. Degrees inside a partially exposed group of tied chords can differ.

The two-edge query in block $i$ at the final threshold reads its type bit independently of every other block. Bridges have value zero in every even edge set, since summing vertex parities on one side of a bridge leaves only that edge. Thus a fixed-length auxiliary summary for all such queries, given the common data but without access to the discarded endpoint lists, needs at least $g$ bits. Storing the block string attains the bound and answers any pattern of pins. Allowing error $\eta<1/2$ for each chosen query still needs $g(1-H_2(\eta))$ bits, where $H_2$ is binary entropy. For uniform independent block bits each bit has conditional entropy at most $H_2(\eta)$ given the message and any shared independent coins, by binary Fano. Sum these conditional entropies and use that a fixed-length message carries at most its length in bits. For the one query in which the block is specified in advance, a single bit suffices.

## How many 2-CNF cases the same forest can hide

Consider the complete edge relation of each graph, so that an encoding must work for every subsequent pin. Suppose $s$ of the $g$ blocks are B. The exact number of projected 2-CNF solution sets needed to cover this relation is $2^s$. The minimum number of binary branch variables whose assignments always leave a syntactic 2-CNF, optimized over all existential 3-CNF encodings, is $s$. A single adaptive branching tree whose leaves project from 2-CNF also needs depth $s$.

For a direct proof, 2-CNF solutions are closed under coordinatewise majority. Taking witnesses for three terminal assignments shows this remains true after existential projection. Within B, choosing the four path bits gives precisely the even-parity relation on four bits. A majority-closed subset of this relation has at most four words. To see this, translate one of its words to zero. The translated set is closed under coordinatewise product, since the product of two words is their majority with zero. Its linear span is still closed under product and lies in the even code. Any nonzero vector with minimal support in this span splits off a one-dimensional direct summand on that support: intersecting it with any other support either gives all of it or gives the empty set. Induction produces a basis on disjoint nonempty even supports. There are at most two such supports on four bits, giving the claimed bound. A allows at most all eight words. The projection of one global piece to each block is majority-closed, so its size is at most $8^{g-s}4^s$. The full relation has $8^g$ words. This gives the lower bounds on cover size and on binary branch depth, even when the pieces correlate different blocks.

For the upper bounds A needs only equalities, and all bridges are pinned to zero. In each B block introduce one selector bit for the parity of the first two path bits, requiring the parity of the other two to equal the same selector. Encode each of these two ternary parity equations by its four width-three clauses. Fixing the selector reduces both relations to binary clauses. Equality propagates each path bit along its path. The resulting single encoding realizes the $2^s$ cases and the depth $s$. This is the graphic special case of the general affine-code calculation. At each completed threshold the logarithm of the exact cover size equals the number of B blocks exposed so far. The sequence reveals all $g$ block bits. Just its final value has only $g+1$ possibilities. [Affine-code theorem, references and finite checks](affine-codes-and-two-sat-backdoors.md)

A RAM handed the graph and arbitrary pins can process each constant-size block directly, checking the bridges and testing its eight words. This takes linear time in the number of blocks plus the number of pins. The example quantifies information lost in discarding endpoints and the cost of a reusable 2-CNF representation. Neither cost is a lower bound on general SAT decision time. A larger example shows agreement up to one third of the number of edges, excluding the endpoint, for pins on physically shared edges, and multiple forests give a positive certificate for bounded pin counts.
