# Finding a failed row versus certifying a transition by parity

**Research journal, 24 November 2026.** There is a one-step memory invariant with all three properties below, for arbitrarily large numbers $v$ of independent variables, with $3\mid v$.

* Every deterministic procedure that identifies a failed predecessor row on every bad outgoing state needs $\Omega(v)$ worst-case probes to the mutable memory. This holds with unlimited preprocessing of the public instance.
* A zero-error randomized procedure finds such a row in at most twelve expected memory probes on each bad state.
* A deterministic certificate that the transition preserves the invariant uses constant-probe universal checks on the public row data and zero probes to the mutable memory. It follows from one parity identity. The rule applies simultaneously to all memories.

Here each of the $v$ independent bits can occupy its own RAM word. The step is the identity. The theorem concerns the specified invariant presentation. It gives a test of the proposed strategy of extracting locally checkable proofs from rejecting SAT computations. [Adaptive invariant selectors](adaptive-invariant-selectors.md)

## Balanced triples with an inconsistent sum

Take 48 independent uniform permutations of $v$ variables. Read each permutation as a layer of disjoint triples, giving $m=16v$ rows. Each row requires the XOR of its three bits to equal a charge. In successive pairs of rows choose the charges independently by pair. Use $(z,z)$ for every pair except the last, which uses $(z,1-z)$, with each $z$ uniform. Thus the sum of all equations over the binary field is $0=1$. Each variable occurs 48 times and the charges have odd sum.

For a fixed assignment the failed-row counts from different pairs are independent, lie in $[0,2]$, and sum to expectation $m/2$. Hoeffding's inequality bounds the probability of fewer than $m/4$ failures by $\exp(-m/16)$. Since $m=16v$, a union bound over all assignments tends to zero. This works for every choice of the layers. Sampling a uniform row and reading its three bits therefore finds a violation in at most four expected trials on the resulting high-gap instances. One may replace each row by the four width-three clauses forbidding its wrong triples. A failed equation then identifies a failed clause after inspecting the same three bits.

For some absolute $\alpha>0$, with probability tending to one every nonempty set of $d\le r=\lfloor\alpha v\rfloor$ rows touches more than $7d/4$ variables. For $j=\lfloor7d/4\rfloor$ a union bound for a bad set of size $d$ is
$$
 {16v\choose d}{v\choose j}(j/v)^{3d}
 \ \le\ [C(d/v)^{1/4}]^d.
$$
Within one layer the selected slots sample without replacement, so their chance of landing in a fixed $j$-set is at most the corresponding with-replacement probability. The layers are independent. The displayed estimate follows from the binomial bound and $d\le j\le7d/4$. Sum first over $d\le\sqrt v$ by a geometric bound tending to zero. For the remaining terms choose $\alpha$ so that $C\alpha^{1/4}<1/2$. At least twice the neighborhood size minus $3d$ variables occur exactly once in such a set. The XOR of its rows therefore has Hamming weight greater than $d/2$. Fix layers and charges satisfying both high-probability properties.

## An adversary for adaptive probes

Write $H$ for the row matrix and $b$ for its charges. For a set $S$ of at most $r/8$ queried variables, retain the consequences obtained by adding at most $r$ rows whose sum has support inside $S$. Each retained coefficient vector has a witness of at most $2|S|$ rows by expansion. Two witnesses for the same vector have symmetric difference of size at most $4|S|\le r$. Independence of every such small row set forces them to agree. In particular their prescribed parities agree. Two retained vectors add to another retained vector by combining their witnesses. The retained equations thus prescribe one linear functional on a subspace of the coordinate space on $S$.

The adversary answers so that this functional agrees with the partial assignment. When one fresh variable is added, the new subspace intersected with the old coordinate space is exactly the old subspace because the row budget $r$ in the definition did not change. If a retained equation uses the new variable, it determines its bit, and subtracting any two such equations proves their requirements agree. Otherwise either bit extends. Repeated queries receive the old reply. After the procedure announces an index, expose its three variables too. Provided that the procedure has used at most $r/8-3$ probes, the announced equation holds, as its single-row witness is retained. Extend the replies arbitrarily to a total assignment. The procedure announces the same index and fails. Probes outside the designated words can be answered by zeros.

This argument adapts classical expansion and local consistency techniques. Hardness of deterministic clause search in highly unsatisfiable formulas already appears in the literature. The additional conditions here make the global parity proof locally checkable on the same instances. ## The identity step and what a checker sees

Let the controller contain a bit $e$. Old row $i$ says that if $e=1$, equation $i$ holds of the mutable bits. The outgoing row says $e=0$. Every memory with $e=0$ satisfies the old invariant. With $e=1$ the outgoing row fails, and an [adaptive predecessor selector](adaptive-invariant-selectors.md) must encounter a failed old row. If it queries row truth as a subroutine, expand the calls into their three word probes and record the first failure. The adversary applies after this expansion. Even complete public tables and unrestricted computation on them do not reveal the mutable assignment.

A symbolic checker can verify the premises of the all-rows parity identity without reading any assignment. For each layer and two distinct slots it requires that their variable indices differ, and checks their ranges. Each layer of $v$ slots therefore lists all $v$ variables once. For each charge pair it checks the specified equality or inequality. Each individual universal challenge inspects at most two table entries. These checks imply the identity for every memory. Pairing adjacent layers by variable and pairing the charges also gives a certificate using the general [affine cancellation rule](affine-row-cancellation.md). In that version an inverse position table per layer may be stored with the public data and checked by local lookups.

One can also change the invariant using auxiliary memory. Store the failed-row bits at the leaves of a balanced binary tree and their XORs at internal nodes. The root of the truthful tree is one for every assignment. On $e=1$ require all original rows, correct leaf and internal equations, and root one. These augmented requirements project to exactly the same old invariant. For every memory, even with corrupted auxiliary data, a deterministic selector checks the root and descends from a node marked one. Either a parent disagrees with its two children or one child is one. At a one leaf it finds an incorrect leaf or a failed original equation. This uses $O(\log v)$ probes. One update of an original bit affects at most 48 leaves and $O(\log v)$ auxiliary words in the truthful representation, given the inverse tables. Initialization of the tree takes $O(v)$ work. This gives a concrete way auxiliary variables can repair failed-row search. Their size, initialization and update costs would have to be charged in a simulation of an actual solver.

## Implication for the SAT search

Already on an identity step, replacing a semantic proof by a uniformly fast procedure for locating a failed predecessor row can lose a linear factor in the number of independent memory words. A constant-gap guarantee does not fix this loss. Algebraic cancellation and auxiliary parity trees do fix it on these examples. The remaining question is whether a rejection proof with sufficiently low total cost exists on the actual diagonal runs of an arbitrary fast solver. The balanced-triple examples provide neither that upper bound nor a runtime lower bound for ordinary SAT.
