# A threshold forest can already contain a full 3-SAT instance

**Research journal, 25 November 2026.** The minimum-spanning-tree paper suggests discarding constraints that are already supported at their own weight. Here is a SAT-equivalent problem for which this rule is exact simultaneously at every threshold. Hardness persists even when all its equality graphs are disjoint paths on at most **four** vertices. If every final component has at most **three** vertices, one can compute the first unsatisfiable threshold in linear time. The distinction concerns what must be decided after the forest has been found. [Khanna, Putterman and Song, Fact 2.5 and Corollary 4.2](https://github.com/Louiealbp/DLMST/blob/0072335716b145032b418760883daed2a89809db/final.pdf) · [Previous tests of threshold certificates for CNF](prefix-certificates-for-cnf.md)

## One relation and its exact threshold certificate

A variable takes one of the six ordered pairs of distinct colors from $\{0,1,2\}$. Call its first coordinate its tail and its second coordinate its head. A row $R(x,y)$ requires the head of $x$ to equal the tail of $y$. Rows carry thresholds. Include every row whose weight is at most the query threshold. A *port* is one coordinate of one variable. Form the undirected graph on the ports by inserting the equality edge for each row. The requirement that the two ports of one variable differ is part of its domain throughout.

A minimum spanning forest of this graph gives a subsequence of at most $2v-1$ original rows for $v\ge1$ variables. At every threshold its components are precisely those of all active rows. Every deleted equality follows along an active forest path. Thus all assignments at all thresholds, including after any additional common restrictions, are preserved. For fixed domain, building the graph is linear in the number of variables and rows; the cited MST theorem gives deterministic linear comparison-based time to find the forest with general comparable weights.

For a chosen threshold contract each equality component. Every original variable now gives one undirected **inequality** edge joining the components of its ports. This is the inverse arc-graph construction of Foniok and Tardif applied to the bidirected three-clique. A coloring of this quotient by three colors gives a legal pair at every variable, and every satisfying assignment gives such a coloring. A loop makes the system unsatisfiable. Parallel nonloop edges impose the same inequality. The MSF theorem bounds the first step. Deciding the resulting inequalities is exactly graph three-colorability. [Foniok and Tardif, Proposition 1](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/458/pdf) · [Lean proof of the equivalence and threshold-preservation implication](../lean/ThresholdPortColoring.lean)

## Three ports suffice for a linear threshold algorithm

Suppose each component of the final equality graph has at most three ports. At any earlier threshold, a quotient vertex has at most three incident edge ends, since each port belongs to one variable. After rejecting loops and identifying parallel edges, Brooks' theorem implies the only connected obstruction to 3-coloring is a copy of $K_4$. Odd cycles use three colors. [Baetz and Wood, statement and algorithmic proof of Brooks' theorem](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.8023)

There is also a linear-time algorithm for the **first** threshold where satisfiability fails, even with arbitrarily ordered and tied row weights. In each final equality component compute all internal pairwise connection times, taking the minimum weight across parallel rows and then all-pairs min-max path closure. Each component has size at most three, so this costs $O(v+m)$ operations in all for $m$ rows. For a variable whose two ports eventually meet, their connection time is its loop birth time.

For each isolated $K_4$ in the final quotient, its four vertices each use three ports and have three distinct neighbors. Its birth time is the maximum of the times needed to connect the ports in each of these four groups. Conversely, if a $K_4$ occurs at any earlier threshold, its vertices already use the full three ports each. No later row can merge those groups with a different group under the final size promise. No other inequality edge can touch them. Consequently every earlier $K_4$ is among the final candidates just listed. The minimum over loop and clique birth times is the answer. An empty candidate set means every threshold is satisfiable. Isolated $K_4$ components can be identified by constant work per quotient vertex after the components are computed.

## Four ports suffice for full 3-SAT

Start with any loopless graph of maximum degree four. Connect one common dummy vertex to each odd-degree vertex and orient Euler tours of the even graph, separately by component. Delete the dummy and its incident edges. Each original vertex now has numbers of heads and tails differing by at most one. Make one six-valued variable per oriented edge. At a vertex, arrange its incident ports alternating between heads and tails, starting on the more numerous side if needed. Join consecutive ports by equality rows, placing the head argument first regardless of the order along the path. This creates exactly one path per non-isolated original vertex, on at most four ports. Contracting recovers the original graph up to isolated vertices, and isolated vertices always admit a color. Construction takes linear graph time. It works at a single threshold with every weight equal.

Three-colorability of maximum-degree-four graphs is classical. The following direct construction also controls the size in *encoded bits* for the journal's time question. [Garey, Johnson and Stockmeyer](https://www.math.ucdavis.edu/~deloera/MISC/LA-BIBLIO/trunk/JohnsonDavid2.pdf)

1. Use an anchor triangle with colors true, false and base. A Boolean variable and its complement are adjacent to each other and to base. For three Boolean literals, introduce a fresh triangle with each vertex adjacent to the corresponding literal. Its colors extend exactly when the three literal values are not all equal. A clause $(a\lor b\lor c)$ is equivalent to the existence of a fresh Boolean $z$ satisfying $\operatorname{NAE}(a,b,z)$ and $\operatorname{NAE}(\neg z,c,\mathrm{false})$. Thus a 3-CNF gives a linear graph in its variable and clause counts.
2. Split a vertex of degree $d>4$ into $d$ equal-colored terminals. A single splitter uses two adjacent fresh anchors, each adjacent to all three of its terminals. These terminals necessarily receive the third color and either choice of the other two colors for the ordered anchors extends it. Arrange $d-2$ splitters in a chain, identifying one terminal between consecutive splitters, using distinct terminals for each identification. Exactly $d$ unshared terminals remain. Attach one old incident edge to each. The anchors have degree four, shared terminals degree four and unshared terminals degree three. Applying this to all original high-degree vertices increases the graph size by a constant factor.
3. Apply the Euler orientation and path construction above. In reverse, a fixed-size Boolean encoding per six-valued variable restricts it to valid symbols; each row forbids a fixed list of invalid symbol pairs. Exact fan-in-two gate clauses with fresh auxiliary bits yield ordinary 3-CNF. With dense identifiers and all variables occurring, the sizes in either direction are linear in the corresponding fixed-width encoded lengths.

For the forward bit count take a fixed-width binary 3-CNF with $m$ clauses and literal names of $w$ bits fitting a RAM word, and use only occurring names. One can normalize the triples, discard duplicates, and renumber occurring variables by binary radix passes over the input in $O(m(w+1))$ steps. If $m'$ triples remain, then $m'\le(2^{w+1})^3$ and at most $3m'$ variables occur. The gadgets use $O(m'+1)$ vertices, edges and rows, with $O(w+1)$-bit names. The output and construction therefore fit $O(N)$ bits and steps, respectively, for input bit length $N$, including when many input clauses are repetitions. For the reverse direction delete unused declared variables and use dense occurring names. There are only a constant number of output clauses per row and variable. Deduplicating identical rows first likewise prevents a blowup in fresh identifier width when the original constraint list contains very many repetitions. All constant-size exceptional inputs can be answered directly.

In particular a deterministic $O(N^k)$ algorithm on the promised four-port paths gives one with the same exponent on ordinary dense fixed-width 3-SAT, for $k=1,2,3$. Conversely a bound for 3-SAT gives that bound on these path instances. This is an equivalence of time questions. The path construction does not decide either of them.

## Even the inequality graph may be triangle-free

Maffray and Preissmann proved that 3-colorability remains NP-complete on triangle-free graphs of maximum degree four. Here is explicit linear-size accounting for an additional step in the reduction above. Replace each vertex of the simple degree-four graph by a fixed twelve-vertex graph with terminals 3 and 9. Its 21 edges, listed by upper neighbors, are

| Vertex | Higher neighbors |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1, 4, 6, 9 |
| 1 | 2, 5, 7 |
| 2 | 6, 8 |
| 3 | 4, 7 |
| 4 | 5, 8 |
| 5 | 10, 11 |
| 6 | 10, 11 |
| 7 | 8, 11 |
| 8 | 10 |
| 9 | 11 |

It is triangle-free, has maximum degree four and gives each terminal degree two. A finite proof of its terminal relation enumerates the assignments in $\{0,1,2\}^{12}$, keeping a coloring if its values differ on the two ends of every listed edge. The counts for terminal colors are ten on each diagonal pair and zero everywhere else. The code below checks these exact counts. [Maffray and Preissmann](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0012365X97892679) · [Exhaustive gadget calculation](../audits/threshold_port_coloring_audit.py)

Use a balanced orientation of the original graph; put outgoing edges on terminal 3 and incoming edges on terminal 9. Each terminal receives at most two additional edges. Every interblock edge joins a 3 to a 9, so interblock edges by themselves contain no triangle. A triangle using two blocks would require two original edges between the same vertices. The decorated graph is therefore triangle-free. Each coloring of the original graph lifts in exactly $10^{|V|}$ ways, and every coloring of the decorated graph restricts to one of these. The decorated graph has exactly $12|V|$ vertices and $21|V|+|E|$ edges. Apply the port-path construction to this graph. Thus forbidding every triangle in the inequality quotient still permits a linear-size reduction of 3-SAT on four-port paths.

## Sometimes every path row is necessary even for the final verdict

Chain $L\ge1$ diamonds, each a $K_4$ with the edge between its two terminals missing. Identify consecutive terminals along the chain and join the two end terminals by an extra edge. Each diamond forces its terminals equal, so the graph is not 3-colorable. Every single edge deletion admits a coloring. If the closing edge is removed, use equal terminal colors throughout. If an edge inside a diamond is removed, give that diamond's two terminals different colors and carry them along the chains on the two sides. For a missing central edge both internal vertices can use the third color. For a missing terminal-to-center edge give that center the color of its now nonadjacent terminal and the other center the third color.

Under the four-port construction, deleting a single equality row splits one quotient vertex of degree $d\le4$ into two nonempty groups of incidences. If one group has just one edge $e$, start with a coloring after deleting $e$ and give the detached port a color different from its neighbor. The remaining case has two groups of size two. Color the graph with that vertex removed, which is possible by edge minimality, then color each new vertex to avoid its two neighbors. Thus every equality row of the original unsatisfiable instance is essential to the single unpinned verdict. There are $5L+1$ inequality edges, $3L+1$ quotient vertices, and exactly $2(5L+1)-(3L+1)=7L+1$ equality rows. This rules out saving even one row from these instances if the certificate must itself be an equivalent subinstance with the original variables and domains. A solver can still recognize the diamond chain and prove its contradiction directly.

Arbitrarily large examples with every row necessary also exist with triangle-free quotients. Decorate the capped chain by the twelve-vertex blocks above. Deleting any original interblock edge gives a 3-colorable graph, since a coloring after deleting the corresponding chain edge lifts through the blocks. Choose an edge-minimal non-3-colorable subgraph. It must retain all $5L+1$ interblock edges. The subgraph stays triangle-free and has maximum degree four. After removing isolated vertices it has minimum degree at least three, since any coloring without a vertex of degree at most two extends to that vertex. The port-path construction now gives at least $2|E|-2|E|/3$ equality rows, and the same vertex-splitting proof makes every one essential. This is an existence proof about subinstances. Choosing a minimal subgraph is unnecessary in the linear-time SAT reduction.

These row-minimal instances also give a direct input-read bound for ordinary 3-SAT. Give each port three one-hot color bits, require exactly one, forbid matching colors at the two ports of a variable, and encode each row as a fixed block of implications equating its two color triples. These clauses have width at most three. If three distinct literals are required, use shared forced-false reserve bits for padding. Replacing any single row block by a copy of a different one changes the formula from unsatisfiable to satisfiable without changing its header or any bit outside that block. On the original formula any correct deterministic solver must read at least one word overlapping each block: otherwise its full run is identical on the corresponding modified input. The fixed-width layout has $\Theta(\log L)$ bits per block on the capped chain, so one RAM word overlaps only $O(1)$ blocks. This proves $\Omega(L)=\Omega(N/\log N)$ steps on those actual unsatisfiable inputs of encoded length $N$. The calculation stops one logarithm below linear merely by counting input words.
