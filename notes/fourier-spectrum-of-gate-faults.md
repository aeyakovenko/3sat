# Finding the Fourier coefficients of a genuine gate computation from its local wiring

Take any of the locally indexed closed Boolean circuits computing the native priority bit $b_j$ with gate fan-in at most two. A gate name and its predecessors have $O(j)$ bits. Each query to the gate description takes $\operatorname{poly}(j)$ time. Give every gate, including constant gates, its own independent binary residual: flip that gate's computed answer exactly when its residual is one. Define $f(r)=(-1)^{\text{output}(r)}$, so $f(0)=(-1)^{b_j}$. Unlike the one-or-two-hole table, $f$ is defined on every possible fault pattern. The results below apply to every such representation of the actual bit; other circuits computing unrelated easy bits need not satisfy the lower bounds. [True bit and native time theorem](priority-pinned-sat.md) · [Noisy diagonal evaluation](noisy-evaluation-of-a-native-run.md)

For a set $S$ of gates write $\widehat f(S)=\mathbb E_r f(r)(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}r_i}$ with uniform independent residuals. The following properties hold.

* One coefficient at an explicitly listed $S$ can be computed exactly in $\operatorname{poly}(|S|,j)$ time, even if $S$ includes the output of an exponentially long computation. It is zero or $\pm2^{-r_S/2}$ for an even number $r_S$ at most twice the number of nonlinear gates in $S$.
* All nonzero coefficients of order at most $d$ and the exact sum of their squares can be found in $4^d\operatorname{poly}(d,j)$ time, even with unbounded fanout. This procedure can certify whether it has found the entire Fourier expansion.
* Consequently for a sufficiently large fixed $B$, put $d_j=\lfloor(j-B\log_2(j+2))/2\rfloor$, ignoring negative early values or using an integer-rounded logarithm. On **every sufficiently late stage**, at least $4^{-d}$ of the squared Fourier mass is on sets of more than $d$ gates for every $0\le d\le d_j$. In particular the real Fourier degree of the actual output as a function of gate faults exceeds $j/2-O(\log j)$. No SAT hypothesis enters this conclusion.

## Why an individual coefficient is a small quadratic sum

Let $o$ be the output gate, $y_i$ the table bit at gate $i$, and $g_i$ its Boolean function of predecessor bits. Sending all $y$ to $r_i=y_i+g_i(y_{\mathrm{parents}(i)})$ is a bijection of binary cubes by forward substitution, with all sums in this formula over $\mathbb F_2$. Uniform residuals therefore permit averaging instead over independent uniform $y_i$. The exponent in the Fourier character becomes
$$
       Q_S(y)=y_o+\sum_{i\in S}\big(y_i+g_i(y_{\mathrm{parents}(i)})\big).
$$
This quadratic polynomial uses at most $3|S|+1$ distinct table positions. A Boolean function on at most two bits has a squarefree polynomial of degree at most two; repeated predecessors are identified. All its coefficients can be computed from the local truth tables. No true gate value is required.

For clarity, when $Q$ has a nonzero mixed term on two variables $a,b$, write $Q=ab+aL+bK+R$ with $L,K$ affine in the remaining variables. Averaging $(-1)^Q$ over $a,b$ gives $\tfrac12(-1)^{R+LK}$. Repeatedly eliminate mixed pairs and reduce all squares by $z^2=z$. A nonconstant affine remainder averages to zero, while a constant contributes its sign. Each step is Gaussian elimination on the alternating polar form, decreasing its rank by two: the substitutions $a'=a+K$, $b'=b+L$ separate off $a'b'$ and leave $R+LK$. The initial rank is at most twice the number of nonlinear gates in $S$, by subadditivity of rank and the two-variable form of each mixed term. All operations, including the exact dyadic result, need only polynomial time in the named portion of the circuit. This is the classical quadratic Walsh-sum calculation applied in residual coordinates. [Carlet, Boolean Functions for Cryptography and Coding Theory](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108606806)

## Which sets can survive

Draw the edges of the dependency DAG from predecessor to consuming gate. The vertex $o$ has to belong to a support with nonzero coefficient if it is a sink: outside $S$, $y_o$ otherwise occurs alone. Without loss restrict to the transitive ancestors of $o$ and $o$ itself; all other *residuals* are independent of $f$ so a support containing any of them has zero coefficient. If a vertex $i\in S$ does not reach $o$ along vertices of $S$, choose a terminal vertex among the non-reaching ones in the induced graph. It is different from $o$ and has no outgoing edge into $S$. Its bit occurs in $Q_S$ exactly as a lone linear term, making the average zero. Thus each surviving set can be explored backwards from the output using only its own vertices.

To enumerate, try every ordered binary rooted subtree shape of at most $d$ vertices. Starting at $o$, follow its chosen left and right input ports. Discard invalid ports and repeated labels. At each successful shape, reconstruct the canonical discovery tree for the resulting set by depth-first search in a fixed port order, visiting each named vertex at most once. Process it only when that tree agrees with the shape. This eliminates duplicates that reconvergent branches could otherwise cause. The reachability property ensures that at least one shape occurs for every nonzero support. There are $\binom{2s}{s}/(s+1)$ shapes of size $s$, hence $O(4^d)$ to enumerate in total, each in polynomial local time. Include the empty support separately.

Since $f^2=1$, Parseval gives total squared mass one. Every enumerated squared coefficient is a multiple of $4^{-d}$. If their sum is less than one, the missing mass is at least $4^{-d}$. If their sum equals one, Fourier inversion at zero gives $f(0)$ by adding the enumerated coefficients with their signs. This check never guesses the correct degree. For $d=d_j$ its time is $o(2^j)$ by choosing $B$ larger than the fixed polynomial overhead. The bound applies on all sufficiently late stages: if the mass were exhausted infinitely often, at least one of the two sound sign detectors would succeed infinitely often under the native clock. The mass conclusion for each smaller $d$ follows from the same non-exhaustion and its own denominator. The inequalities estimate spectral weight, not time to decide the SAT formulas generated from this computation.

## A nondeterministic certificate with arbitrarily high-degree terms

A verifier may also be given any explicit list of distinct supports together with their gate names. Compute their signed coefficients with the quadratic algorithm, test that the squared sum is exactly one, and report the sign of their ordinary sum. Every successful list is sound at its own stage: all omitted terms have coefficient zero. Computations use a common dyadic denominator of bit length bounded by twice the maximum list entry size plus the number of bits needed for the sum. No search through absent gate names or unlisted supports is part of verification. Under an $O(n^k)$ RAM algorithm for ordinary encoded SAT ($k=1,2,3$), a fixed nondeterministic checker carrying this out in $2^{\lfloor j/k\rfloor}/(j+1)^C$ native steps for sufficiently large $C$ can succeed only finitely often, by the packed bounded-acceptance reduction and the native detector theorem. In particular finding such full-mass certificates infinitely often would exclude the corresponding SAT clock. This measures the cost of computing the listed coefficients as well as writing the list. [Native bounded-acceptance transfer](packed-band-transfer.md)

As a check on the proposed approach, append $L$ identity gates to the output in a path. On fault patterns the new sign equals the old sign times the parity character on all $L$ new residuals. Every nonzero support consequently contains those $L$ gates and its coefficient on the old part is unchanged. All coefficients below order $L$ vanish, even if the appended wiring is completely known. Known identity paths can be contracted analytically. For a general native computation we have no guarantee that its surviving coefficients can be specified and summed at low cost after such simplifications. The local enumeration and its exact stopping test give access to some genuine coefficients, without a proof for the unrestricted SAT clocks.
