# How far a SAT solver moves between memory accesses

Fix a correct deterministic SAT program on the usual word RAM, with a fixed finite number of registers, word width $w=\Theta(\log N)$ and arbitrary writable workspace. There are distributions of proper $N$-bit ASCII 3-CNFs on which, with probability $1-2^{-\Omega(N)}$, its actual execution has
$$
\sum_j |\min(a_{j+1},M)-\min(a_j,M)|=\Omega_P\big((N/w)^2\big).
$$
Here $a_j$ are the addresses of consecutive data reads and writes and $M$ is the last input word address, placing the input at address zero. On these distributions $M=\Theta(N/w)$. In particular, if the program runs in $O(N)$ steps on the support, almost every run has $\Omega_P(N/w)$ consecutive access pairs separated by $\Omega_P(N/w^2)$ words. The SAT verdict throughout the support is UNSAT. Correctness on satisfiable files is essential to the proof.

## The simulation charges a change of owner

Take the [bit-once matrix encoding](compact-bit-projection-of-matrix-mismatch.md) with $d$ a power of two and $D=d^2=\Theta(N)$. Choose independent uniform binary matrices $A,B$ and put $C=AB$. For communication purposes allow every triple $(A,B,C)$, so that the UNSAT verdict is exactly product equality. Choose any public assignment of **all** physical word addresses to Alice and Bob, giving each side between $D$ and $2D$ source bits in the input. Each matrix bit changes just one bit of one word. Thus each party can initialize its entire portion of memory from its own source bits and public constants; this includes any initialized input length or fixed workspace.

One party runs the machine's finite control and fixed register bank up to the next operation on a word owned by the other. At that point it sends the program counter, register bank and finite execution phase to its partner, who executes the pending access. Halt and the binary verdict have their own message tags. A fixed instruction with several memory actions can be expanded into a constant number of these operations using a constant number of temporaries. Word arithmetic and register-only steps are local. Mutable words stay with their owner throughout. This deterministic protocol uses at most $c_P w(1+k)$ bits on an actual run with $k$ changes of ownership between consecutive memory accesses, including the allowance for startup and halt. In particular the protocol works with data-dependent addresses and unboundedly many distinct accessed words within the address space.

A completed transcript for UNSAT describes a rectangle in the two parties' private input strings. Once the transcript is fixed, each party checks its own moves from its own input. Combining any two such consistent inputs preserves the transcript and hence the verdict. Every such rectangle lies in the graph $AB=C$. The [balanced rectangle bound](product-rectangles-at-adaptive-cuts.md) gives it mass at most $2^{-\gamma D}$ under the **same distribution** for every allowed partition. There are fewer than $2^{b+1}$ binary transcripts of length at most $b$. Taking $b=\lfloor\gamma D/2\rfloor$ proves that at a fixed balanced ownership partition the probability of fewer than $\alpha_P D/w$ ownership changes is exponentially small, for a sufficiently small constant $\alpha_P>0$.

This statement holds simultaneously for up to $2^{\gamma D/4}$ public ownership partitions by a union bound. A program may choose a partition from this family *after* the input and trace are revealed. For example, every balanced ownership partition with a description of at most $\gamma D/5$ bits relative to a fixed decoder and public dimension qualifies for large $d$. Decoding time plays no role. This assertion covers any assignment of owners to the workspace represented in the family.

## From cuts to address distances

Give Alice the words through address $c$ and Bob the words above it. The cumulative number of source bits moves from zero to $3D$ in increments at most $w$. At least $D/w-O(1)$ distinct input word cuts leave between $D$ and $2D$ source bits on the left. There are $O(N/w)$ input thresholds, so the protocol bound holds at all the balanced ones simultaneously with probability $1-2^{-\Omega(N)}$.

A move from word $a$ to word $b$ crosses exactly $|\min(a,M)-\min(b,M)|$ among the cuts $0,\ldots,M-1$. Sum in the other order over consecutive accesses. The balanced cuts already contribute $\Omega_P((D/w)^2)$ to this sum. If there are at most $KN$ accesses, allow $h=\lfloor\epsilon N/w^2\rfloor$ units of travel per hop, with $\epsilon$ sufficiently small given $K$ and the lower-bound constant. This accounts for at most half the required travel. Each hop of remaining length greater than $h$ contributes at most $M$, so at least $\Omega_P(N/w)$ such hops occur. The constants here may depend on the linear clock as well as the program. The finite coarea identity, its selected-cut inequality and the long-hop estimate are [checked in Lean](../lean/WordCutTravel.lean). The RAM simulation and distributional estimate are manuscript arguments.

For a per-access radius bound $r\ge1$ the same inequality gives $\Omega_P(N^2/(w^2 r))$ steps on these files. On a single finite-alphabet, single-head tape containing both input and workspace, represent the consecutively laid-out input in bits or bytes. A head crossing transfers only the finite control. Each cut needs $\Omega(N)$ crossings, there are $\Omega(N)$ such cuts and each head move crosses at most one. This gives an $\Omega(N^2)$ time bound on almost every correct-product file for each such solver. The one-head crossing argument is classical. Multiple concurrently active heads require a different simulation. [Hennie, *One-Tape, Off-Line Turing Machine Computations*](https://www.eecs.tufts.edu/~aloupis/comp260/lectures/hennie-1965.pdf)

## A bound with several tape heads

On an ordinary $k$-tape machine for fixed $k$, align the left ends of the tapes and write $p_j(t)$ for head $j$'s position at step $t$. Put the finite-alphabet file on tape one. On the same distribution, with the same high-probability quantifier, a correct run of duration $T$ satisfies
$$
 T+\sum_{t<T}\big(\max_j p_j(t)-\min_j p_j(t)\big)=\Omega_P(N^2).
$$
To see this, split **every tape** at the same public threshold $c$. Each party keeps track of the positions and contents of the heads and cells on its side. When all heads are on its side, it simulates locally up to a boundary event or halt. At a mixed configuration the parties exchange finite control, scanned symbols and flags identifying any crossing heads, costing $O_k(1)$ bits. A step from an unmixed configuration that moves one or more heads across the cut also requires only a constant-sized message, including the identity of the crossing heads, any finite control and the new ownership pattern. Every incoming head starts exactly at a boundary cell, whose position is known. Elapsed local time does not need to be transmitted. Thus the communication is at most a constant times one plus the number of mixed time steps plus the number of head crossings. Between messages local computations and their boundary events are deterministic, so completed transcripts still define rectangles. Each of $\Omega(N)$ balanced thresholds needs $\Omega(N)$ bits simultaneously. At any tick the number of mixed thresholds is at most the head span, and total individual crossings over all thresholds cost at most $kT$. Summing proves the inequality.

In particular, if all head spans are bounded by $R$, the clock is $\Omega_P(N^2/(R+1))$; under a bound $R=O(T^\alpha)$ it is $\Omega_P(N^{2/(1+\alpha)})$ for fixed $0\le\alpha\le1$. Ordinary unrestricted tape heads can remain far apart for many ticks.

There is also a concrete obstruction to converting arbitrary linear-time multitape computations into subexponential DNNFs. Use two ordered lists of vertex records. Each record has a vertex bit and four incidence flags; a left record also has a proposed XOR bit per color. For each color pair the marked left and right records in order. Accept exactly when their counts agree and each paired edge carries the XOR of its endpoints. A uniform multitape machine copies the lists to two tapes, scans them forward in lockstep over the marked entries for one color, rewinds and repeats. With four colors this takes linear time.

On an infinite sequence of lengths fix the flags of the first three colors to a three-monotone bounded-degree bipartite expander with $m$ vertices per side, and take the fourth color to be the identity perfect matching. Fix unused proposed bits to zero. In the resulting graph, any vertex cut whose smaller side contains at least $\delta m$ vertices has $\Omega_\delta(m)$ boundary edges for fixed $\delta>0$. To verify this, choose whichever of the cut and its complement contains at most $m/2$ left vertices, and let $a,b$ denote the numbers of left and right vertices there. If $|a-b|\ge c m$, use the identity matching. Otherwise $a\ge(\delta-c)m/2$ and the left expansion contributes at least $(1+\eta)a-b\ge\eta a-cm$ boundary edges, positive and linear for small enough $c$ relative to $\delta,\eta$. Now apply the [mixed-edge argument](linear-sat-control-for-word-travel.md) to all unfixed vertex and edge bits. At a balanced bit partition, either both vertex sides have at least $\delta m$ vertices or a small vertex side has linearly many edge bits with both endpoints on the opposite side, for sufficiently small $\delta$. A matching of mixed parity checks makes each positive rectangle exponentially small. Thus the uniform linear-time tape language requires exponential DNNF size at these lengths. The flags themselves are ordinary input to the tape program; only the lower-bound restriction fixes them to expanders. [Dujmović, Sidiropoulos and Wood, *Layouts of Expander Graphs*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.05020)

On a standard RAM every jump in this sum can take a single instruction. The [linear-time expander consistency family](linear-sat-control-for-word-travel.md) realizes the same cut, travel and long-hop conclusions on actual DIMACS files where one globally correct solver takes only $\Theta(N/\log N)$ worst-case steps on the tiled layout. To derive a superlinear time bound from the matrix inputs would require an additional property that charges operations for the values computed or the addresses selected. The present estimate charges distance.
