# One extra bit of precision turns sparse cycle tests into global cycle parity

**Research journal, 2026-11-24.** Can the algebraic lower bound apply to polynomial-size *bit inputs* and a numerical accuracy requirement compatible with a fast decision algorithm? There is a promise on integer matrices where the Boolean decision takes linear time in the dense encoding, yet even relative approximation by integer arithmetic circuits requires superpolynomial size. More precisely, the number of low output bits preserved by a proposed arithmetization controls which global consequence follows.

For m≥2 write H(X) for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial. The test inputs are X=2b where b is an m-by-m nonnegative integer matrix and the sum of all its entries is at most m. H(X) is zero except when b is the adjacency matrix of one Hamiltonian cycle, in which case H(X)=2ᵐ. In the dense encoding with O(log m) bits per entry, a RAM can check row and column sums and follow the unique successor from one vertex within O(m²) word operations. Outputting the binary numeral also fits this clock. If desired, the checker has a standard Boolean circuit and an explicit, polynomial-length SAT encoding. The lower bound below takes the matrix entries as integer arithmetic inputs.

## First transfer, from sparse Boolean tests to unrestricted Boolean inputs

Let P be the polynomial computed by a division-free arithmetic circuit with integer constants, additions, subtractions and multiplications. There is no formal degree bound. Suppose that on each Boolean matrix b with at most m ones,

    P(2b) ≡ 2ᵐ H(b)  (mod 2^(m+1)).

Then the same congruence holds on **every** Boolean matrix. Work in the ring of integers modulo 2^(m+1). Every monomial of P(2X) of total degree greater than m vanishes there. Replace each positive exponent by one on Boolean inputs. The resulting squarefree polynomial has degree at most m, as does 2ᵐH(X). Values on the Hamming ball of radius m determine all coefficients through that degree over any ring. Indeed, evaluating at the indicator of S sums the coefficients indexed by subsets of S, so subtraction recovers the coefficient at S. Higher coefficients are zero on both sides. This proves the extension. Modulo 2ᵐ the promise output is already identically zero.

Consequently a size-s such circuit would evaluate the parity of the number of Hamiltonian cycles in an arbitrary directed graph using O((s+1)m²) Boolean gates. Hardwire the low m+1 bits of each integer constant, implement gate arithmetic modulo the power of two by binary schoolbook arithmetic, and take output bit m. The parity problem is ⊕P-complete. This is a Boolean consequence conditional on preserving the residue, even with no guarantee at higher precision. [Björklund and Husfeldt](https://thorehusfeldt.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/08/pdhc.pdf)

## Moderate additional precision forces the hard formal polynomial

Put L=m+ν₂(m!)+1, where ν₂ is the power of two dividing a nonzero integer, expressed as an exponent. Suppose now P(X) agrees with H(X) modulo 2ᴸ on every integer test input above. Then the formal degree-m homogeneous component of P, reduced modulo two, **equals H over the two-element field**.

For a multi-index α of total degree m write α!=∏ᵢ αᵢ!. Take its mixed forward difference Dα, based at zero with step 2 in each coordinate. This is an integer linear combination of the values at 2b for 0≤b≤α, all of which are test inputs. On a monomial X^β the difference equals

    2^|β| α! ∏ᵢ S(βᵢ,αᵢ),

where S(r,j) is a Stirling number of the second kind, with S(0,0)=1. The univariate signed binomial sum counts surjections from r labeled elements to j labels by inclusion-exclusion, giving j! S(r,j). The multivariate formula follows by taking the product. It vanishes unless β≥α and gives 2ᵐα! for β=α. Therefore Dα(P−H)/(2ᵐα!) is an integer whose parity equals the coefficient of X^α in P−H. Higher terms each contribute an even integer after the division. Since Dα(P−H) is divisible by 2ᴸ and α! divides m!, this quotient is even. This holds for every α. [Stirling numbers and differences](https://dlmf.nist.gov/26.8)

The precision threshold for determining an arbitrary degree-m coefficient is sharp. The monic univariate polynomial ∏_{j=0}^{m−1}(X−2j) takes values divisible by 2ᵐm! at every nonnegative even input. Adding it preserves all residues at precision L−1 while flipping the parity of the coefficient of Xᵐ. This sharpness statement concerns unrestricted coefficient recovery. Hardness of a particular polynomial could hold at lower precision.

Reduce the original circuit modulo two and compute homogeneous components up to m at every gate by truncated convolution. The resulting circuit for H has O((s+1)(m+1)²) gates. The published lower bound at the **fixed characteristic two** therefore yields s≥m^{Ω((log m)^(1/3))} on all sufficiently large orders. Constants of arbitrarily large integer magnitude are allowed. For exact agreement the analogous forward-difference argument works with step p and coefficients in the rationals with denominators coprime to a fixed prime p: the normalized difference equals its target coefficient modulo p. [Arithmetic lower bound, Statement.lean lines 26–41](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/blob/11b6c7e3c927df2658f513b6b68ea7f8929c62c5/vp-vnp/Statement.lean#L26-L41)

Even a real relative error strictly below one, required as |P−H|<H on positive tests and P=0 on zero tests, implies exactness on all tests for integer P. On a positive test, the full set of m live edges supports exactly one cycle. All proper subfaces have value zero. The alternating sum over these subfaces, including the full face, thus equals P at the positive input. Every surviving monomial in this sum uses all m live edges and is divisible there by 2ᵐ. The only multiple of 2ᵐ strictly between zero and 2^(m+1) is 2ᵐ. This argument uses integer coefficients and prevents small *relative* error from hiding a bad residue. Functional lower bounds for arithmetic circuits have been studied in several other models. Priority of the sparse even-input transfer has not been established. [Forbes, Kumar and Saptharishi](https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.04207)

## Attempt to obtain the required output bits from a fast SAT algorithm

A single exact division after a small integer straight-line circuit suffices on these same test inputs. Let Rᵢ(X) and Cⱼ(X) be row and column sums. Let Tᵣ(X) be the polynomial of arborescences toward a fixed root r, computed by a directed matrix-tree determinant. Consider

    Q(X) = (∏ᵢ Rᵢ(X)) (∏ⱼ Cⱼ(X)) Tᵣ(X).

Q has homogeneous degree 3m−1 and polynomial circuit size, including a division-free determinant circuit. At b with total mass ≤m, the two products can both be nonzero only on a permutation matrix, where they equal one. The forced outgoing arcs from the nonroot vertices form a rooted tree exactly when the permutation is a single cycle. Thus on all test inputs Q(2b) = 2^(2m−1) H(2b). One final exact division by 2^(2m−1) recovers the desired integer. A final zero test alone recovers the decision bit. These operations explain precisely how the standard RAM escapes this lower bound. The entries b themselves also admit Q(b) as a division-free indicator, so the scaling of the *arithmetic inputs* is essential. [Matrix-tree construction and determinant sources](cycle-representatives.md)

There is a direct use of the hypothesized O(nᵈ) SAT algorithm in testing a candidate circuit. An existential Boolean formula guesses b, checks its mass bound, evaluates the candidate at 2b modulo 2ᴸ and compares with the easy expected residue. Supplying the constants reduced to L bits, schoolbook binary sums and products give an explicit formula of size Õ((s+m²)m²) bits, including gate indices. Hence one call detects a discrepancy within Õ(((s+m²)m²)ᵈ) time, plus reading the original representation if constants arrive in a longer encoding. At precision m+1 the same test certifies correctness as a global parity evaluator. At precision L it must reject the universal correctness claim of any candidate below the arithmetic bound. Finding an actual bad matrix uses ordinary self-reduction on its input bits. A fixed list of q test inputs admits a simple response: sum the cycle monomials of just those positive hosts appearing in the list, using O(1+qm) operation gates (add the m² inputs if these are all charged). This circuit also vanishes on every zero test. A short test list therefore supplies no such universal certificate for arbitrary polynomial-size candidates.

The unproved implication for the SAT problem is that a quadratic or cubic Boolean solver could provide a small arithmetic circuit preserving the stated bits on these integer inputs. Even a known fast RAM algorithm for this promise does not do so. [EvenJets.lean](../lean/EvenJets.lean) checks Möbius recovery on a Hamming ball under the cancellation law used by the residue ring. [even_jets_experiment.py](../audits/even_jets_experiment.py) checks 336 normalized differences and the exact-division identity on all 235 small hosts tested. The arithmetic lower bound transfer and its running-time consequences are manuscript deductions.
