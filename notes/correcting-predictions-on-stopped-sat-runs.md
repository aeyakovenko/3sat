# Correcting a predicted range answer using stopped SAT runs

A correct SAT decider can verify a guessed numerical answer on one stopped computation. Only a mistaken guess needs further queries to recover the answer. On the weighted range transcript, this gives an exact online procedure using at most $q+e\lambda$ stopped computations on a truthful stream with $q$ queries, $\lambda$ bits per answer and $e$ wrong predictions. If $e=O(q/\lambda)$ it uses only $O(q)$. More generally, a forecast distribution gives an exact procedure whose number of stopped computations depends on the information content of the true words under that forecast.

## The cost being measured

Use the public distribution of $n$ weighted dominance operations from Weinstein and Yu with $n/\log^2 n$ updates and $q=n-n/\log^2 n$ queries at the prescribed times. The aligned DIMACS transcript has bit length $L=\Theta(n\log n)$. Its ordinary annotations assert exact answers. At an individual pending annotation, a replacement word can instead ask for a bit or compare the true sum to a supplied numerical threshold. Blank all remaining unassigned slots with their position-specific dummies. All previous claims are truthful, so the SAT verdict gives the current test result with its known polarity. All these immutable completions have identical native histories up to issue of the pending load. [Ordinary transcript](weighted-range-transcript-encoding.md) · [Pending-word test encodings](parity-challenges-at-the-pending-sat-word.md) · [Weinstein and Yu, Theorem 1 and Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03030)

Fix a correct deterministic SAT program and the online order for exposing its input words at first discovery. At each pending claim allow the same fixed observer as in the truthful-prediction theorem. It sees the last $b$ query intervals of the genuine native trace, the public stage and current operands. It may probe $d$ additional old words adaptively and has no free persistent memory. The resulting view $V_j$ is formed once before any candidate is tested. Both the observer and its predictor are fixed independently of the sampled stream. Let $T$ be the number of master steps needed to serve the original operations. The bit width $\lambda=\lceil\log_2(n^2+1)\rceil$ covers every answer. [Exact view, costs and hard distribution](predicting-annotated-answers-on-a-truthful-sat-run.md)

Count a stopped tail from service of the pending load through halt, including that service. Let $h$ bound the native steps of every tail used by the proposed recovery procedure on the stream in question. The bound can be imposed on all candidate completions at the cuts as a simpler sufficient hypothesis. The online simulation logs only the master, paying $O((b+1)(T+n)+qd+n\log n)$ probes for its work and observations. During an operation it can simulate each tail from the frozen master using a private dictionary of fork writes, with no writes back to the master. A first inherited load reads the frozen work or tagged packet map. The pending word and unseen dummies have their prescribed values. Initialization costs $O(1)$ per fork, and one native step needs $O(1)$ cell probes. When done, the simulator emits the recovered true value and feeds the corresponding *ordinary* annotation to the master. The persistent costs, including the controller, are charged across callbacks. A cutoff with an abort can enforce a fixed polynomial limit on storage and time for the simulation outside its claimed success event.

Write $H=(\log n/\log\log n)^2$ and $B_n=n/\log^{C} n$ for a sufficiently large absolute $C$. The distributional proof gives absolute constants such that a fixed online procedure has probability at most $2^{-B_n}$ of both answering every query correctly and using fewer than $c n H$ probes, for small enough $c>0$ at sufficiently large source sizes. In particular the proof of its Claim 5 supplies a positive constant probe threshold for the fixed update schedule. For a fixed clock factor $A$, the prior choices $b=\lfloor\log n/(\log\log n)^3\rfloor$ and $d=\lfloor(\log n)^2/(\log\log n)^3\rfloor$ give base cost $o(nH)$ whenever $T\le A L$. Thus, except on unconditional probability $2^{-B_n}$, a stream with such a master prefix requires $h$ times the number of recovery tests to be $\Omega(nH)$. This conclusion is about the tested tails of ordinary complete files.

## One test per correct guess

For a prediction $g_j(V_j)$, run the equality completion first. On equality, emit the tested word. Otherwise ask the $\lambda$ separate coordinate tests, all from the same pending master state. This always recovers the true sum, so the probe upper bound on this stream is

$$
 O\!\left((b+1)(T+n)+qd+n\log n+(q+e\lambda)h\right).
$$

Consequently a uniform tail cap $h=o(H)$ forces $\Omega(nH/(h\lambda))$ actual wrong numerical forecasts on a fast master outside the exceptional mass. Conversely, if at most $O(q/\lambda)$ guesses are wrong on a fast master outside that mass, at least one of the tested completed files has an $\Omega(H)$ tail. The ordinary true-annotation continuation is used to advance the master after each recovery. A long tail in this assertion may belong to a different stopped completion.

For a list of $\ell\ge1$ possible answers based on the view, sort the distinct list values. Successive threshold tests locate the insertion position of the true value among them in $\lceil\log_2(\ell+1)\rceil$ tests. One equality test suffices to check the candidate if there is one. Use the coordinate tests only after a list miss. Thus the corresponding count is at most $q(\lceil\log_2(\ell+1)\rceil+1)+e_{\rm miss}\lambda$. Local enumeration of proposals is free in the cell-probe reduction. Every threshold and equality test still uses a genuine stopped tail.

## Forecasts, bit errors and a per-query cutoff

Fix a distribution $p_j$ on the legal answers given $j,V_j$. They inherit their ordinary increasing numerical order. Mix $p_j$ in equal proportions with the uniform distribution to get strictly positive masses $s_y$. Give answer $y$ the first $\lceil-\log_2 s_y\rceil+1$ binary digits of the midpoint of its cumulative interval of length $s_y$, taking the terminating expansion at dyadic points. The corresponding half-open dyadic interval lies within the cumulative interval, up to shared endpoints. These intervals give a prefix-free code in numerical order. At each branching node a threshold on $Y_j$ implements the split, since each child is a consecutive set in this order. Suppress nodes having a single child. This is the classical alphabetic Shannon–Fano–Elias construction. It applies to each of the finitely many views by fixing the comparison tree before the stream arrives. [Gilbert and Moore, *Variable-Length Binary Encodings*](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1959.tb01583.x)

On the actual stream the number of stopped tests at query $j$ is at most $3+\min\{\lambda,-\log_2 p_j(Y_j)\}$, also allowing zero forecast mass. Thus when $h=o(H)$ the clock bound forces the cumulative forecast loss $\sum_j-\log_2 p_j(Y_j)$ to be $\Omega(nH/h)$ outside the same exceptional fast mass, by exact online recovery. This uses an additional bound on the costs of the tested completions. The prediction-only entropy theorem uses no such hypothesis.

For a concrete bit-error form, let a fixed rule predict successive bits, receiving earlier true bits of the current answer and the already formed view. For each possible word, the set of positions where it differs from this sequential rule determines the word uniquely by replay. At most $\sum_{i=0}^r\binom{\lambda}{i}$ legal words incur at most $r$ such mistakes. First test the zero-error candidate if legal. Try the sorted lists for radii $1,2,4,\ldots$, capping at $\lambda$, until a hit. At each radius use only the comparison-and-equality search described above. The list at radius $\lambda$ includes every legal word. For an actual $k\ge1$ mistakes this takes $O(1+k\log_2(1+\lambda/k))$ tests, and $O(1)$ when $k=0$. Indeed the logarithm of the ball size is $O(r\log(1+\lambda/r))$ by the binomial bound, also for $r>\lambda/2$ by the trivial $2^\lambda$ bound. Summing over the doubling radii gives the same order as the final term, by summing the geometric series and its first moment. This bounds the work by the positions that actually need correction, even if the rule's later predictions changed after an earlier bit error.

Finally, impose a per-query cap $a$ on paid comparisons in any one of these fixed binary decision trees. After $a$ paid answers, choose each remaining branch by a fresh fair coin until a leaf is reached. On a fixed stream let $\ell_j$ be its depth in the exact recovery tree at the truthful view, and $R=\sum_j(\ell_j-a)_+$. Conditional on the preceding truthful guesses, the correct remaining path has probability $2^{-(\ell_j-a)_+}$. Hence the probability of a completely correct online sequence on this stream is $2^{-R}$. For any fixed $0\le g<B_n$, the mass of fast streams with $R\le g$ and $qah$ below a sufficiently small constant times $nH$ is at most $2^{g-B_n}$. This is where the small-success exponent permits a few unresolved branch choices after the hard deadline.

There is an information limit on a recovery argument that uses binary verdicts alone. Give such a procedure $q$ independent uniform $\lambda$-bit hidden answers and allow any binary question about them at each paid call. Conditional on its random tape, a tree with cutoff $s$ has at most $2^s$ terminal answer vectors. Its cheap-and-correct probability is at most $2^{s-q\lambda}$, so even a success threshold $2^{-B_n}$ saves at most $B_n$ questions on that example. The actual range answers have correlations. Given all query coordinates, their entire vector depends on only $O((n/\log^2 n)\log n)$ update bits. Exploiting these correlations cheaply on an online machine would require accounting for how the relevant earlier state is accessed.

An unrestricted SAT solver can defer checking prior claims and can perform different work on each stopped completion. The correction estimates bound that collection of work and conditional prediction quality. They do not add its instructions to the time of the truthful master.
