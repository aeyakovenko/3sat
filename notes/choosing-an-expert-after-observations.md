# Choosing the lower-error expert after observing the bin

The [two fixed naming rules](adaptive-naming-of-winner-pairs.md) can have identical error counts on every observed date up to the **last qualifying normal opportunity**. On actual matching bins, a fast deterministic predictor given this entire prefix and the exact remaining length still chooses the uniquely worse rule. With $r$ future two-date blocks left, the worst possible additional loss from its choice is exactly $r$ when the two past counts are tied. Correcting the choice after every observation also has a sharp cost. Relative to the better fixed rule, the optimal worst-case loss on a prefix of $t$ online choices is $\lceil t/2\rceil$. These statements concern an additional bit about genuine SAT-derived histories; the resulting bounds on deterministic prediction do not separate ordinary SAT time classes.

Each rule proposes excluding one ordered pair of different earlier live pool members, both of whom really will win with zero. Its mistake means that its first named rank actually wins now and its second actually wins on the next date. The two rules share a second name $s_j$, the least pool member still live at date $j$. They choose different first names, avoiding both $s_j$ and the previous date's $s_{j-1}$ when defined. They make proposals only when there are at least four live pool members and the number $N_j$ of **all** live lower ranks is at least $\ell=\lceil e/2\rceil$, for endpoint rank $e$. A mistake by either rule prevents a mistake by either rule at the immediately following start. Distinct mistaken starts therefore consume two different lower ranks each, with no reuse across starts. The counts here, as in the original construction, concern the phase through the genuine positive tag win.

## What the prefix lets a predictor certify

At the start of date $j$ every error on a start through $j-2$ has been settled by the genuine past. At most one rule can still have a pending claim from $j-1$ whose first name was the actual winner on that date. Take $u=1$ if there is such a pending claim and $u=0$ otherwise. Known earlier errors can be counted in time polynomial in the current stage. Let $d$ be the difference between the larger and smaller settled counts. An upper bound on the combined number of additional errors, including the pending one, is

$$R=u+\begin{cases}0 & N_j<\ell,\\ \lfloor (N_j-\ell)/2\rfloor+1 & N_j\ge\ell.\end{cases}$$

Indeed if $v>0$ future starts at or after $j$ yield errors, $2(v-1)$ distinct ranks live at $j$ have retired before the last of them, and at least $\ell$ lower ranks must still be live on that last start. This bound applies even if the phase ends early or several dates are cooldowns. Choose the rule with the smaller settled count, breaking ties deterministically. Its final total exceeds the better final total by at most $\max(0,R-d)$. To see this, all $R$ additional errors might go to the chosen rule, but its starting advantage was $d$. When $d\ge R$ this certifies a genuinely optimal rule from past data alone. An empty or tied settled record on its own supplies no such certificate while errors remain possible.

## Forcing the predicted rule to be worse after a long common record

Use the sparse ordinary program slots of the [contiguous-error construction](adaptive-naming-of-winner-pairs.md#a-contiguous-run-of-mistakes-on-actual-dates). For every fixed deterministic verifier descriptor, asymptotically all its matching bins have no lower raw retirement during the phase. In such a clean bin, all controls retire before a pool member wins. Let $K$ be the public first normal date after control clearance. At $K$ the whole pool is live and $N=N_K\ge e-O(\log^*(e+1))$. If the common search on a normal date selects a pair, its two ranks actually win in succession and the next date after those two is normal. An empty normal search gives the positive tag its win immediately. All searches complete on sufficiently late matching bins at the clocks below. Other bins and descriptor matches with raw preemptions need no prediction guarantee for the lower bound.

Define $m=\lfloor(N-\ell)/2\rfloor+1$. If the verifier always offers one pair on each normal opportunity with $N_j\ge\ell$ and none after that, there are exactly $m$ two-date blocks on a clean match. Their normal starts are $K+2a$ for $0\le a<m$, followed by the tag win on $K+2m$. Every qualifying opportunity here has at least $\ell-O(\log^*(e+1))$ live pool members, enough to provide at least **three distinct candidate first names** after avoiding $s_j$ and $s_{j-1}$. Here “qualifying opportunity” means a public guard date with $N_j\ge\ell$. The separate dense-bin condition on the actual positive win counts honest future-zero winners. No promise about the current tag winning is given to the predictor.

Fix any efficiently computable choice $t=t(m)$ with $0\le t<m$. Choices such as $t=m-1$ or $t=\lfloor 3m/4\rfloor$ (when $m$ is large) require only the public value $m$. Let $B$ be a fixed deterministic predictor at a checkpoint $j$, taking $2^{(1-\epsilon)j}$ work for some fixed $\epsilon>0$. It gets the genuine history through $j-1$, $e,K,m,t$, and the proposed checkpoint. Construct a deterministic verifier for the named pool event as follows, accepting exactly its chosen pair on a guarded normal date. During the first $t$ normal opportunities after control clearance, use the **third** eligible first name, different from both experts' choices, and their common second. At the next normal opportunity $j$ obtain the forecast bit from $B$ and use that expert for the rest of the phase whenever the public guard holds. The verifier can recover the checkpoint and earlier normal dates from rank roles and the genuine history. On subsequent dates it replays $B$ on the frozen checkpoint input with its original word width. Off the specified clean behavior, implement the same guarded count of normal opportunities, idling on incomplete data. Use a uniform bounded simulation, with a fixed fallback bit on timeout. Any genuinely timely answer of $B$ is used. Polynomial setup and enumeration overhead fit $2^{(1-\epsilon')i}$ at current date $i\ge j$ for a fixed $0<\epsilon'<\epsilon$. The deterministic descriptor search includes such a verifier with a sufficient cap; it does not test its own soundness.

On every sufficiently late clean bin matching this verifier, the first $t$ actual pairs have the third first name. They cost neither expert an error. An intervening response start cannot cost an expert either: its actual first winner equals the preceding common second, which both experts now avoid as first. Starts before control clearance have no pool first winner. Thus at $j=K+2t$ both expert counts are zero, and the previous date has no unresolved expert error. During the remaining $r=m-t$ normal blocks the chosen expert is wrong each time, the other never. The final expert counts are $r$ for the prediction and zero for the other, without any later hidden errors. This gives the sharp additive loss $r$ on tied prefixes, even when the predictor is told exactly how many blocks remain. The public bound above also gives $R=r$ here. Taking $r=1$ forces a wrong exact retrospective bit one block before the end. Taking $r=o(e)$ yields only an $o(e)$ excess in this delayed choice. Using the chosen rule on the unobserved earlier dates would require access to that choice before it was made.

There is a version for a predictor that issues a bit only on a nondeterministic accepting branch. Assume deterministic $O(n^k)$ SAT and branch time at most $2^{(1/k-\epsilon)j}$ for fixed $0<\epsilon<1/k$. On the genuine checkpoint test for each of the two possible outputs by bounded computations compiled to ordinary SAT and run a fixed correct solver. Use the first output with a successful test, or the fallback if both fail. The deterministic cost is $(2^{(1/k-\epsilon)j}+\operatorname{poly}(j))^k\operatorname{poly}(j)=2^{(1-\Omega(1))j}$. The preceding deterministic bin construction applies. At any such clean matching checkpoint with an output, at least one accepting branch names the uniquely worse expert. A predictor required to be correct on **every** accepting branch must abstain on this subsequence. Simply permitting a good branch alongside a bad one would let a predictor always issue both bits. ## Switching rules after each observed mistake

Let a fixed adaptive learner choose one of the two experts at each of its normal opportunities, using the entire genuine prefix, possibly including the previous observed loss. Have its matching deterministic checker offer that expert's pair. On a clean match each proposed pair actually wins in succession. On any prefix of $t$ such blocks, the learner incurs exactly $t$ errors. If it chose the first expert on $a$ blocks and the second on $t-a$, the experts incur exactly $a$ and $t-a$ errors, respectively. Thus the learner exceeds the better expert's loss by at least $t-\lfloor t/2\rfloor=\lceil t/2\rceil$ on **every** prefix. If it always selects an expert through the public live guard it has all $m$ blocks. If it may abstain, the first empty normal search is the genuine tag win. A learner required to make some claim at every sufficiently late qualifying positive win cannot abstain while at least $\ell$ live pool members remain, so on sufficiently late clean matches it still has $\Omega(e)$ blocks. The statement for each prefix holds without this additional coverage hypothesis. Nondeterministic adaptive proposals at the preceding SAT clock can again be canonicalized; a verifier issuing both may have a correct alternative but it also issues the actually false exclusion.

The prefix regret bound is sharp. Alternating the two experts disagrees with either fixed expert on at most $\lceil t/2\rceil$ of its first $t$ choices. On each disagreement the excess loss compared with that expert is at most one; on an agreement it is zero. This upper bound holds for arbitrary genuine outcomes on the $t$ scored choices. On its own clean matching bins it attains exactly $\lceil t/2\rceil$ regret for every $t$. The adversarial response here is built into the physical deterministic phase: it selects a realizable pair after the program has specified its choice. A randomly chosen rule using private coins that the common search never sees describes a different prediction experiment.
