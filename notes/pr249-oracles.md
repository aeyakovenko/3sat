# Boolean oracles on the complex permanent graph

**Research journal, 2026-11-24.** At fixed matrix order m let P(X) be the permanent and let Γ be the complex hypersurface Y=P(X). Consider a bounded deterministic machine over ℂ with arithmetic and equality tests. It may also have discrete work tapes and query a fixed language A of finite bitstrings. Its cost bounds every execution, and the bounds below concern maximum path cost in a nonuniform algebraic computation tree. A can be ordinary Boolean SAT or any other language, even an undecidable one. PR 249 gives a graph decision-tree bound of 2^(b·(log₂ m)^(4/3)) over exact complex inputs, for a fixed positive constant b and all sufficiently large m. We use the operation and division conventions of its pinned proof. [PR 249 at commit 50e44b5](https://github.com/1337isnot1337/solved-conjectures/blob/50e44b55f7f10adf6c8aa8f167b31dd5b23c2171/research/bss-permanent-graph/PROOF.md)

## Compiling explicit Boolean queries

Fix the dimension, cost bound and discrete input, if present. Unroll the machine to its finite computation tree. Along a path the outcomes of the field equality tests are fixed. If field information reaches the discrete work tape only through those outcomes, every explicit query bit is then fixed at its oracle node. The associated answer 1_A(w) is a constant of this unrolled tree. Replace the node by its actual continuation. Induction down the finite tree preserves the answer on **every** complex input. The new tree needs at most the old number of arithmetic operations and field tests along each path. Its description may be exponentially large, and the transformation need not be effective for arbitrary A. Algebraic decision-tree depth permits this nonuniformity. [Lean model of explicit queries](../lean/DiscreteOracle.lean)

A slightly different interface supplies query bits in field registers whose contents are promised to be 0 or 1. Test each against 0 to read it, and hardwire the reply at each resulting leaf. An occurrence with ℓ bits takes at most ℓ additional equality tests. If the oracle accepts a raw tuple precisely when it lies in A⊆{0,1}*, with every nonbit tuple rejected, at most 2ℓ tests suffice. Stop on the first nonbit. Thus the total overhead is bounded by one or two times the sum of query lengths along the chosen path. In particular it is linear in the original budget when that budget charges all query lengths. It is polynomial when both the number and the maximum length of the queries are polynomially bounded. Explicit work-tape queries in the preceding paragraph incur no such extra scan.

## A generic-path argument for raw tuples

Even if copying a wide raw tuple is free, this oracle cannot bypass the factor argument for Γ. Assume its length and addresses are fixed along a discrete path, all queried coordinates are inputs, constants or previously computed field registers, and division is defined wherever used. Compare execution on the generic ambient input (X,Y) with execution on the generic graph input (X,P(X)). The two computations take place over their respective rational function fields. For a finite raw tuple, ambient or graph generic membership in A is true exactly when its entries are identically a fixed bit vector w with w∈A. Indeed any nonconstant entry differs from both bits on a nonempty open subset, and there are finitely many entries. The finite path is realized on a nonempty Zariski-open subset in each irreducible input space, since its denominator, inequality and oracle conditions each hold on such a subset. Generic ambient inputs off Γ reject and generic graph inputs accept.

There must be a first divergence. On its common prefix all field registers are rational expressions whose denominators remain nonzero on the generic graph. An ambient rational identity along the prefix remains an identity under Y=P(X). Hence if a field equality is the first divergence, its difference is zero generically on Γ but nonzero in the ambient space. If a raw oracle call is the first divergence, ambient acceptance would force its entire bit vector also to be identical on Γ. Therefore the graph accepts this query, giving a fixed bit vector w there, whereas the ambient tuple differs from w. At least one register r satisfies r=wⱼ on Γ and r≠wⱼ generically in the ambient space. In both cases the numerator F of the difference is a nonzero polynomial divisible by Y−P(X), by polynomial division in Y. Representing each rational register by numerator and denominator costs a constant number of division-free arithmetic gates per arithmetic instruction on the common prefix. Thus F has an O(T+1)-size circuit when the machine takes T arithmetic operations and tests, independently of the raw query width.

Bürgisser's factor theorem in characteristic zero bounds the approximative circuit complexity of an irreducible factor polynomially in its degree and the circuit complexity of a nonzero multiple. Here the factor Y−P has degree m for m≥1, and specialization at Y=0 yields −P. The border-complexity lower bound on P therefore gives T≥2^(c·(log₂ m)^(4/3)) for some c>0 and all sufficiently large m, using the same quantitative deduction as PR 249. This is a statement about tree depth and border complexity over ℂ. [Bürgisser, The Complexity of Factors of Multivariate Polynomials](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06828v1)

## What finite inputs and evaluations give

On a fully bit-encoded input of L bits, **every** language restricted to that length has a nonuniform decision tree of depth at most L. Read all bits and label each leaf with its answer. Its size can be exponential. On a complex coordinate promised to range over a known alphabet of k distinct values, k−1 equalities suffice to identify it by exclusion. Thus a fixed finite grid admits a finite exact lookup tree, and on Boolean matrices with the candidate permanent value also supplied in binary the depth bound is simply the total encoded length. These observations explain why restricting the graph algorithm to a finite set does not transfer its complex-input depth lower bound to a uniform word RAM.

Interpolation allows reconstruction if enough exact evaluations are available. For example the permutation-supported multilinear polynomials Σσ cσ ∏ᵢ Xᵢ,σ(i) form an m!-dimensional vector space. In a black-box reconstruction of an *unknown member* any fewer than m! fixed-point evaluations leave a nonzero kernel polynomial. The same holds for adaptive numerical probes on their all-zero response path. The zero polynomial and a nonzero kernel member give the same transcript but differ at some complex input. This calculation does not prove an evaluation lower bound for the particular permanent with its known coefficients. An existence test for a nonzero permutation term is also insufficient to infer the sum: the two terms of the permanent of ((1,1),(1,−1)) cancel.

A procedure that extracts arbitrary bits of a complex number, or an oracle that directly interprets a complex tuple as a polynomial feasibility question, has a different interface. In particular, limiting the *output* of an oracle to one bit places no restriction on its complex-input power: graph membership itself has one output bit. The arguments above concern discrete query languages and equality-BSS arithmetic. They give no lower bound for SAT on finite, encoded inputs.
