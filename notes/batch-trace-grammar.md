# A shared grammar for newly finished RAM runs

**Research journal, 24 November 2026.** If a batch of chronological RAM traces shares a short concatenation grammar, a verifier can check their memory accesses without expanding the grammar or receiving previous-write pointers. Small initial entropy and a common halt time do not ensure that such a grammar exists. On one efficient literal universal machine, at infinitely many lengths, at least half the labels in each sufficiently large interval immediately preceding the first missing word have unique eligible producers whose traces obey the lower bound. Four-symbol register projections already suffice.

## Checking the compressed batch

A binary straight-line grammar has terminals holding individual records, rules concatenating two previously defined strings, and a root for each claimed trace. The names, claimed output words and their root associations are supplied explicitly. Let $s$ be the total encoded size of these data and the grammar, for at most $B$ time steps per root. Assume ordinary bounded-size native records with $O(\operatorname{polylog} B)$ bits, zero initial work memory and separate read-only inputs. An input stored in work memory can instead be initialized by explicit writes. Expand any instruction with multiple events into a fixed number of ordered event slots.

Fix one address $a$. A trace segment induces a partial function on the initial value of this one cell. It is either impossible or has the form “optionally require input value $u$, then either retain the input or replace it by $v$.” A read at $a$ requires its recorded value and retains it. A write at $a$ replaces it. Other events act as the identity. These summaries compose in constant many word operations. In composing two segments, a required second input is checked against a first replacement when present, and otherwise becomes a requirement on the original input. Conflicting requirements give the impossible summary. The last replacement, if any, determines the final value. Consequently one bottom-up pass over the shared grammar checks the projected reads for any choice of address and root. All addresses passing from their initial values is equivalent to full work-memory consistency. Lean 4.19 checks the composition law, including contradictory reads and overwrites. [Lean proof](../lean/GrammarCell.lean)

Compute expansion lengths in topological order, rejecting an oversized reachable root or rule. Given a root and a time, descend by subtree lengths to retrieve that record and its successor. Checking initial and terminal states, times, actual input consumption, claimed output bits and legal CPU transitions can use universally chosen positions and roots. For variable native word width, the local check receives the true position. Use output counters in the records when an instruction emits a bit; their increments and agreement with the supplied output are checked locally. A well-formed batch is therefore valid precisely when there is no bad root, position or address. The deterministic verifier for a selected challenge takes $s\operatorname{polylog}(s+B)$ time; its execution can be converted to an explicit quasilinear-size SAT instance for the existence of a violation. Under deterministic linear-time SAT, checking the supplied grammar costs $s\operatorname{polylog}(s+B)$. Guessing the grammar and then checking all violations instead takes a fixed number of quantifier blocks at the same scale under the hypothesis. This uses the usual uniform bounded-RAM reduction. ## Tiny descriptions and many distinct substrings

Fix $q\ge2$ and $L=2^q$. Take any $K$ distinct binary payloads of **the same** positive length $n\le q$. Use a cyclic order-$q$ binary de Bruijn word $u$ of length $L$, so its cyclic substrings of length $q$ comprise all $2^q$ binary words once. For payload $p$, the marked record at $i=0,\ldots,L+q-2$ is the two-bit value

$$
(u_{i\bmod L},\;p_{i\bmod n}).
$$

For $0\le i<L$ all $KL$ length-$q$ substrings across the chosen traces are distinct. Projecting onto the first bit identifies $i$. Holding $i$ fixed, the second bits contain a full cyclic copy of $p$ starting at a known offset, so they identify $p$.

A shared grammar with $g$ binary concatenation rules produces at most $g(q-1)$ distinct length-$q$ substrings across *all* roots. For any occurrence descend until its interval crosses a child boundary. The rule at that boundary and the number of its symbols strictly left of the boundary, from $1$ through $q-1$, determine the substring uniquely. Thus $g\ge KL/(q-1)$. With two fixed-width references to earlier symbols per rule the encoded grammar uses $\Omega(KL)$ bits: $\log_2 g=\Omega(q)$ already when $K=1$. The claim concerns explicit rules; an algorithm can describe this particular grammar much more briefly. Projecting full native traces onto marked records and erasing all other events sends each terminal to one or zero letters and preserves every concatenation. The lower bound therefore also holds for full-record grammars. In a finite check on all 35 parameter pairs with $2\le q\le8$, $1\le n\le q$, the full batches had exactly the predicted counts. The largest had 65,536 distinct eight-record substrings. The substring-count method is closely related to known lower bounds for dictionary compression via string attractors. [Kempa and Prezza](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10964)

One fixed RAM implements the batch program. It reads a self-delimiting $n$ and a payload of $n$ bits and may take $q=n+1$. It constructs a common de Bruijn cycle by traversing the graph of $(q-1)$-bit vertices with two outgoing edges corresponding to appending 0 or 1. An Euler tour visits each of its $L$ edges once. Stacks and two outgoing-edge flags per vertex suffice for $O(L)$ word operations on a logarithmic-word RAM at this time scale. For a growing-width simulator one can first warm up for $O(\sqrt L\,\operatorname{poly}(q))$ steps counting with bits, after which $q$-bit indices fit. Writing the marked register at each position, advancing the payload index cyclically, and accumulating a bitwise XOR of all its marked values takes another $O(L+q)$ word operations. The halt time can be identical for all inputs of the same length by giving both bit values the same instruction schedule. Include the checksum in the final output if desired so that every marked value feeds an output through the dynamic arithmetic trace. A fixed interpreter can simulate these steps with its usual polylogarithmic overhead. Conditional on $n,p$, the generated trace has constant ordinary description length. Under a uniform draw from the batch its Shannon entropy is at most $\log_2 K$. [Eulerian construction](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0020019005002395)

## Actual first arrivals preceding the canonical missing word

Fix an integer $\lambda\ge2$, for example $3$ or $20$, and use the deadline $T(m)=2^{\lambda m}$. The following single native stream machine is efficient and literally universal. All outputs are published only at halt.

* On header $0$, read an exact halting program $p$ of a fixed ordinary optimal prefix machine and recover $r=r(p,u)$ by the injective phase pairing with its runtime $u$. Set $m=2^r$, $\kappa=|p|+2$, $\ell=m-\kappa$. Read $\ell$ raw bits $z$, skipping the finitely many ineligible sizes. The full name has exactly $m-1$ bits. Set $q=\lambda m-e$ for a fixed large integer $e$, and perform the marked de Bruijn computation above with $z$ as the cyclic payload. Publish $0^\kappa z$.
* On header $1^H0$, simulate the fixed efficient universal native machine $V$ on its unmodified input. Other headers diverge. This fixed literal compiler preserves the simulation overhead of $V$.

The special routine is part of the specified native machine, so its Euler traversal and marked loop have a direct constant-word-operation implementation. Its setup time is polynomial in $m$ apart from the optional width warm-up. Choose $e$ so its physical halt time is at most $T(m)$ at sufficiently large lengths and at least $cT(m)$ for a fixed $c>0$. Its instruction schedule is independent of the bits of $z$. Thus for every fixed $a>0$ it finishes in $(T(m)/m^a,T(m)]$ eventually. At a given nonempty phase these special programs produce exactly the initial interval $[0,N)$ of $m$-bit outputs, with $N=2^\ell$. Distinct special names for this length have distinct outputs, since $m$ identifies $r$, which identifies $p$.

There are infinitely many $p$ that are shortest for their own ordinary outputs. For these $p$, $K(m)\ge |p|-O(1)$: given $m$ one can recover the ordinary output from the pairing and simulation. On the other hand, the total $V$-program weight for words of length $m$ is at most $C2^{-K(m)}$ by universal semimeasure domination of the length marginal. Every distinct generic output having a name of length less than $m$ costs at least $2^{1-m}$ of full-name weight. The corresponding special cohort has full-name weight $N2^{1-m}=2^{-|p|-1}$. Choose $H$ once, large enough that the total generic weight at this length is at most a quarter of that cohort weight on every such phase. Consequently the number $G$ of distinct generic outputs with a name shorter than $m$, **at any time**, is at most $N/4$. [Related thin-compiler estimate](programmable-clock-spectrum.md)

Let $v$ be the first $m$-bit word missing by $T(m)$ from all names shorter than $m$. Then $N\le v\le N+G$. In every interval $[v-K,v)$ with $N/2\le K\le v$, at most $G$ words can lack a unique special eligible producer: each exception needs a distinct generic output. At least $K-G\ge K/2$ words in the interval therefore have the prescribed marked traces as their *only* eligible histories, and their names are shortest even with unlimited time. Since $q\ge\ell$ eventually, a shared grammar for these histories requires at least

$$
\frac{(K-G)2^{\lambda m-e}}{\lambda m-e-1}
$$

binary concatenations. With the explicit fixed-width rule encoding it requires $\Omega(KT(m))$ bits. The same bound up to constants persists if fewer than $K/4$ interval labels are discarded before choosing producers. This conclusion is unconditional and concerns genuine canonical predecessors of this machine. All payloads have an inexpensive semantic description here. A Boolean verifier could exploit the special program directly; the grammar lower bound is limited to certification by explicit chronological record grammars. For the original unrestricted SAT question, no proof that short semantic certificates must also fail has been found.
