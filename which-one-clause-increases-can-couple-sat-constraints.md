# Which one-clause increases can couple 3-SAT constraints?

A local operation deletes $r$ proper ternary clauses and inserts $r+1$. It can use any number of fresh existential variables. Require it to give **exactly the old relation** on the variables of the deleted clauses, so it works inside any surrounding formula and under all future pins. Require the inserted rows to be irredundant for this projection. Deleting any one must admit some previously forbidden boundary assignment. These assumptions allow the ordinary split and are forced for a universal replacement used to preserve minimality on a minimally unsatisfiable host whenever each inserted row remains essential there.

For $r$ variable-disjoint deleted clauses, **every such operation copies $r-1$ clauses and splits the last on one fresh variable**. Two old clauses sharing just one literal of the same polarity likewise cannot both be replaced without retaining one of them. Two common literals suffice for a genuinely different one-clause increase. It can be iterated from the smallest UNSAT seed while keeping every variable's occurrence count at least three. The one-clause step is measured in clauses, with a separate calculation for the encoded word-RAM clock below.

## Rigidity on disjoint clauses

Complement terminal names so that all old clauses are positive. A proper terminal-only clause implied by the old relation must be one of the original rows. Indeed, falsify that candidate by setting its positive names to zero and every other name to one. Unless it contains an entire old row, all old clauses hold.

Let $d$ be the number of old rows missing from the output. Irredundancy precludes duplicate rows. There are $d+1$ other rows, each containing an auxiliary literal. Each of the $3d$ terminal variables of the missing rows is essential and must occur somewhere in those $d+1$ rows. Thus $3d\le2(d+1)$ and $d\le2$. If $d=0$, the extra row can be removed, since a fresh literal can always be set to satisfy it.

If $d=2$, each of the three new rows has two terminal variables and one auxiliary. All six terminal names are distinct across the rows. Each terminal occurs positively since its occurrence is unique and the projected relation is increasing and depends on it. Set one missing triple to zero and all the other terminals to one. At most one new row can have both its terminals set to zero. Its single auxiliary literal is satisfiable. This contradicts rejection.

If $d=1$, set the missing triple to zero and satisfy every retained row. Both new clauses must together reject every auxiliary assignment. Each contains an auxiliary, so each must reduce to a unit, and the two units must be opposites on the same fresh variable. Indeed two nonempty proper clauses can cover the whole cube by their falsifying sets only when each excludes half and the halves are complementary. Neither new clause can contain a literal on any of the retained rows' variables. Its value could be set true while still satisfying that retained ternary row and keeping the missing triple zero. Each of the four remaining slots must therefore be a positive literal of the missing triple. All three names occur and a proper clause cannot repeat a name. Up to signs and order, the replacement is
$$
   (a\lor b\lor z),\quad(\neg z\lor b\lor c).
$$
The opposite deduction works for every context by existential resolution. Greedy inverse splits recover the unique terminal formula up to signed renaming, in linear incidence work. Thus allowing an adversary to choose an entire disjoint batch, new variable names and new row order at every generation does not enlarge this closure. ## One shared literal versus two

Let the two old clauses be $A=(p,a,b)$ and $B=(p,c,d)$ on five different positive names. If neither old clause is retained, all three new rows contain an auxiliary, by the terminal-only implicate argument. The five essential terminals occupy at least five slots. Thus there are three or four auxiliary slots in total. Every used auxiliary must occur in both signs. Otherwise give it its satisfying value and remove any row containing it without changing the projection.

With three auxiliary slots all three rows contain the same fresh variable once. Eliminating it gives at most two nontrivial terminal clauses. These must be exactly $A$ and $B$ after deduplicating literals. To see this, every nontautological implied clause contains $A$ or $B$. An assignment falsifying just $A$ forces a clause containing $A$ and not $B$, and the symmetric test forces the other. Any extra literal in the former can be set true with $A$ false and $B$ true, unless it duplicates a literal of $A$. This would satisfy both clauses. Yet the two resolvents contain the two signed terminal literals on the side of the pivot occurring once. As $A,B$ share only $p$, this is impossible.

With four auxiliary slots there must be two fresh names, each occurring twice, once in each sign. One row contains both, and each appears in exactly one of the other two rows. Resolving out the two names yields a single clause on the five distinct once-occurring terminals. Such a clause cannot be the joint relation of $A$ and $B$. This proves that at least one of these one-overlap parents must survive.

For two common literals take $(p,q,a)$ and $(p,q,b)$. Use the replacement
$$
    (p,q,z),\qquad(\neg z,p,a),\qquad(\neg z,p,b).
$$
Resolving on $z$ gives precisely the original pair. A private assignment failing just the first parent has $p=q=a=0$ and $b=1$. It extends with $z=1$ to fail just the first of the two side rows. The other parent's private assignment handles the second. Either parent's private assignment, extended with $z=0$, fails just the central row. Private models of other rows extend by the projection identity. These conclusions also hold if $a$ and $b$ are opposite literals of one name.

The last two rows again share two literals. Reapply the replacement, taking $p_{i+1}=\neg z_i$ and $q_{i+1}=p_i$, with $a,b$ unchanged. Start with all eight signed clauses on three names and select $(p,q,a),(p,q,\neg a)$. This yields a minimally unsatisfiable proper formula at **every clause count $q'\ge8$**, with all used variables occurring at least three times. A fresh variable starts at degree three, gains one occurrence when its sign is used as the next $p$, and loses one when it is used as $q$ in the following round. The original $q$ loses one occurrence from its initial eight. All other degree changes respect the claimed minimum. No inverse split on a twice-occurring variable is available anywhere.

This particular sequence has a bounded-width primal graph. Keep the three seed variables together and add bags for successive rounds containing the seed variables and the newest three auxiliary names. Every central row and the last two side rows lie in a bag and each variable occurs in an interval of bags. The width is at most five. For arbitrary renaming and ordering a fixed-width decomposition algorithm and ordinary SAT dynamic programming still give one globally correct native $O(n)$ execution on these files. Thus this larger successor escapes the inverse split and preserves every private witness, while a different solver handles it within the linear deadline. ## The smallest replacement with one occurrence per port and no degree-two auxiliary

There is also a single-parent gadget that leaves the occurrence count of **every** old variable unchanged, uses fresh variables of degree four and preserves the private rows of any host. It uses five proper clauses. Five is optimal under the weaker restriction that every new variable occurs at least three times, when each of the three input ports occurs once, at least one fresh name is used, and the output is irredundant for the projection.

In the three-dimensional auxiliary cube with coordinates $x,y,z$, partition the vertices into the three edges $*00,0*1,11*$ and the two points $010,101$. For each cell use the clause that forbids membership in that cell. For the edge cells append the respective input literals $a,b,c$, obtaining
$$
 (a,y,z),\ (b,x,\neg z),\ (c,\neg x,\neg y),\ (x,\neg y,z),\ (\neg x,y,\neg z).
$$
For every terminal setting the number of extensions is $2(a+b+c)$, interpreting the three literal values as integers. When all three are false every auxiliary point falsifies exactly one row. Deleting any specified row then creates one or two satisfying assignments. Each fresh name appears twice in each sign. At the all-false boundary, setting $X=x,Y=\neg y,Z=z$ gives the classical deficiency-two core consisting of $X\to Y\to Z\to X$ and the all-positive and all-negative clauses. Here its three implication clauses acquire separate ports, and the lower bound concerns the number of rows for that replacement. [Kullmann and Zhao on the deficiency-two cycle](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.0629) [Subcube partitions, Filmus et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.14685)

For optimality each once-occurring port must have the polarity of the old disjunction. A terminal-only row would be the original clause and make all other rows projectively redundant. At the all-false boundary, a row with $j$ terminal literals excludes a fraction $2^{j-3}$ of the auxiliary cube, with $j\le2$. With at most four rows and three terminal slots overall, the only placement with total excluded measure at least one is exactly four rows with terminal counts $2,1,0,0$. The excluded measures are $1/2,1/4,1/8,1/8$, so these four subcubes partition the cube. The last three cells all fix the first cell's coordinate $x$ to its opposite value, or they would meet it. The two eighths also fix the second cell's other coordinate $y$ to its opposite value. Their remaining coordinates must be the same third name, with opposite values, or those eighths would meet. That name occurs only twice. This rules out every smaller qualifying gadget, for any number of available auxiliary names. A disconnected all-auxiliary subsystem beside the original row cannot be irredundant for the projection.

Even this five-row gadget cannot yield hard native runs merely by iterating on clauses from a fixed seed. Attach a new bag containing its three terminals and three auxiliaries to any old bag containing the replaced clause. Each successor has treewidth at most the larger of the seed width and five. This applies to every choice of the next clause, every signed renaming and every row order. More generally the same gluing works for bounded-size fresh gadgets attached only to the three variables of a single clause. Work beyond this entire collection must couple old regions in a way that survives such a decomposition.

If the three ports occupy distinct rows, every assignment with $j$ true ports has at least $j$ extensions. An eleven-row gadget attains that lower bound with every row essential. Its precise minimum and the resulting multiplicity on uniquely satisfiable files are proved separately.
