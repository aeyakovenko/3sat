# What symmetry costs for SAT on unordered incidence structures

On sparse instances with $v$ variables, proper 3-SAT requires $2^{\Omega(v)}$ gates in a symmetric Boolean threshold circuit. The theorem covers circuits chosen separately at each size, at arbitrary depth, and already holds for 3-XOR. In the same representation, 2-SAT and Horn SAT have polynomial-time uniform, polynomial-size symmetric AND/OR/NOT circuits. The exponential lower bound is a published result of Atserias, Dawar and Ochremiak. The two subgroup consequences below specify how much of the symmetry assumption one can remove by elementary reductions. [Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak, §5.2, Theorems 2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

## Inputs and circuit symmetry

There are $D$ domain points, including variables and clauses. A signed incidence presentation has unary sorts and two binary relations $P(c,x),N(c,x)$ for positive and negative occurrences. Each proper ternary clause touches three distinct variables. Repeated clauses may have distinct clause points. A circuit input is a bit for each possible relation tuple. Consequently $D$, the number $v$ of variables, the dense relation-table length $\Theta(D^2)$, and the length of a sparse DIMACS file are different parameters. The hard families have $D=\Theta(v)$. [Atserias–Dawar, §3.1](https://www.cs.upc.edu/~atserias/papers/definable-inapproximability/definable-inapproximability.pdf)

The circuit is symmetric if each permutation of the domain points acts on relation coordinates and extends to a wiring automorphism preserving gate types and the output. Unary sorts are input relations, so their members move along with the input. No linear order on domain points is supplied. The Boolean threshold basis in the theorem includes NOT, unbounded AND and OR, and gates testing whether at least a specified number of their inputs are true. Size counts gates. The condition is on the architecture of a single circuit across all labelings. Correctness of its output under relabeling follows for every SAT decider irrespective of its architecture. [Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak, §2.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

The published theorem uses three named *local* incidence ports and unary predicates for the clause's sign pattern. Forgetting ports to obtain $P,N$ has a constant-size formula per tuple. For example, $P(c,x)$ is the disjunction, over the three ports, that the port contains $x$ and its sign in the clause type is positive. Unary sorts are supplied or derived from the clause-type predicates. These formulas give an equivariant circuit of $O(D^2)$ gates. Composing it with a symmetric circuit on signed incidences gives a symmetric circuit on the published presentation with that additive overhead. Thus the exponential bound applies also when the three positions in a clause are completely unordered. [Published model and Theorem 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

## The parity reduction and the two tractable fragments

A parameter-free interpretation can implement the parity reduction even if the input XOR equation has three *unordered* neighbors. Replace $x+y+z=b$ over $\mathbb F_2$ by a clause for each of its four falsifying assignments. A variable occurs negatively precisely when the forbidden assignment sets it to one. There is one clause with equal signs and three with mixed signs. For $b=0$ the equal-sign clause is all negative and each mixed clause has one negative literal. For $b=1$ the equal-sign clause is all positive and each mixed clause has one positive literal. Represent the equal-sign clause by its equation $e$ and each mixed clause by $(e,u)$, where $u$ is the unique minority-sign neighbor. Keep the variable points in a separate copy. The tags, incidence and signs use equality, the equation type and the original incidence relation. This finite-copy interpretation uses at most pairs before encoding its constant tags. It creates exactly $v+4m$ points for $m$ equations. Each assignment extends without auxiliary variables and violates exactly one of the four clauses for each failed equation. This gives the usual four-clause reduction without selecting an ordering of any triple. Atserias and Dawar already use the reduction to obtain lower bounds even with a satisfiability gap. [Atserias–Dawar, Lemma 1 and Theorems 8–9 in the linked version](https://www.cs.upc.edu/~atserias/papers/definable-inapproximability/definable-inapproximability.pdf)

* **2-SAT.** Use two tagged copies of each variable for its two literals. For every binary clause $a\lor b$, add implications $\bar a\to b$ and $\bar b\to a$. A unit $a$ adds $\bar a\to a$. Reject an empty clause immediately. On the implication graph compute reachability by a least fixed point. Unsatisfiability is equivalent to mutual reachability of some literal and its complement. For the reverse direction, contract the strongly connected components and take a topological ordering. When each literal and its complement lie in different components, setting a literal true precisely when its component comes later than its complement's satisfies every implication. The order is used only to prove existence of an assignment. The decision test itself only computes reachability.
* **Horn SAT.** On the Horn promise, view each clause as an implication from the set of variables occurring negatively to its optional positive head. Starting at the empty set, repeatedly add each head whose entire body belongs to the current set. This positive fixed point is contained in every model. It is itself a model exactly when it contains no complete body of a headless clause. Testing the body requires a universal quantifier over incident negative variables, and so also handles unbounded clause size in the incidence representation. The empty body handles positive units and an empty headless clause forces rejection.

Both definitions unroll into polynomial-size circuits by assigning gates to tuples of domain points and to iteration numbers. Use at most $2D$ rounds to extend reachability by one edge at a time on the literal graph, or $D$ rounds to close the Horn variables. Structural permutations act on tuples and leave iteration numbers fixed. This gives the claimed uniformity directly. More generally, Anderson and Dawar identify polynomial-time uniform polynomial-size symmetric majority circuits with fixed-point logic with counting (FPC). Their support theorem gives a constant-size support for each gate of a rigid polynomial-size symmetric circuit. A support fixes a gate under every permutation fixing the support pointwise. Constant support alone does not bound the time required by an ordered RAM. For the stronger *nonuniform* SAT lower bound, Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak translate small symmetric circuits through symmetric linear programs to bounded-variable counting formulas at each size and apply the linear counting-width bound for SAT. Uniform FPC inexpressibility alone would leave size-dependent advice unaccounted for. [Anderson–Dawar, §§4–5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00224-016-9692-2) · [Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak, Lemmas 1 and 5 and Theorems 2–3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

## How much symmetry can a small circuit retain?

Write $G=S_D$ and let $H\le G$ consist of the domain permutations that lift to automorphisms of an $s$-gate circuit fixing its output. Suppose the circuit is correct on all labelings of every instance in the promise. Take one relabeled copy per left coset of $H$ and OR the outputs. Each copy is correct on the same promise. For $g\in G$ and a coset representative $a$, write $ga=a'h$, with $a'$ the representative of the image coset and $h\in H$. A lift of $h$ identifies the relabeled wiring in the two copies, so $g$ lifts to an automorphism of the combined circuit. The new circuit has at most $1+[G:H]s$ gates. On the sparse hard sizes this gives
$$
1+[G:H]s\;\ge\;2^{\Omega(v)}.
$$
This conclusion requires no uniform choice of group lifts and no effective construction of the coset representatives.

Suppose $H$ contains every permutation of a set of $r$ interchangeable points fixing all other points individually. For this statement take the promise of all well-formed instances on $D$ points, allowing isolated variable points, including all instances with the fixed linear clause bound of the hard family. Place a hard instance entirely inside the interchangeable set, with a fixed number $d\le r$ of active points, and fill the remaining $D-d$ positions with isolated variables. All relations outside the active structure specialize to constants. Every permutation on the active points extends by the identity to an available circuit automorphism. The resulting circuit solves the original hard problem on these $d$ points and is fully symmetric there. Constants can be shared. A basis without constant gates can generate them symmetrically from the OR of all active input bits and its negation using polynomially many gates. Theorem 3 in the cited version supplies hard sparse sizes for every sufficiently large variable count, with a fixed linear bound on domain size, so one can take $d=\Theta(r)$. The resulting lower bound on $s$ is $2^{\Omega(r)}$. In particular a polynomial-size family deciding this promise can retain full interchangeability on at most $O(\log D)$ points fixing the rest. The argument uses the ability to place both variable and clause points inside the interchangeable set. A model that gives every clause a fixed address while allowing only variables to move needs a separate analysis. [Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak, §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

A word RAM receives a linear sequence of bytes, can branch on addresses, and may use numeric identifiers to choose pivots. For example, deterministic Gaussian elimination decides 3-XOR in polynomial time despite the same exponential symmetric circuit lower bound. A transfer to unrestricted RAM time would require a theorem converting each relevant fast computation into a circuit retaining quantified structural symmetry at suitably small cost. The incidence theorem supplies no such conversion. [Atserias–Dawar–Ochremiak, §5.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.07825)

## Choiceless polynomial time with counting

The September 2026 preprint on choiceless polynomial time with counting (CPT) gives a separate corollary for unordered proper 3-SAT. On its separating grid structures, $X_t$ is one vertex's configuration block. Lemma 4 gives $|X_t|=3^{2f(v)+d(v)-1}\le3^{29}$, since at most twelve faces and six edges meet a vertex. Hence each row in equations (2) and (4) uses at most $3^{29}+1$ unknowns, including the possible $\mu_y$. Each configuration selects one edge state per incident edge, so even the sum defining its row coefficient in (1) involves at most six selected states on these inputs. The query on arbitrary structures permits wider rows. Proposition 5 and the proof of Theorem 1 give opposite answers on pairs that no single CPT program distinguishes for all grid sizes. [Preprint, §§1.1, 2.2–2.3 and 7](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/3344da59991ed94f9ba711217b29c1d760badfa6/preprints/Choiceless-polynomial-time-with-counting-does-not-capture-polynomial-time-September-23-2026/paper.pdf)

Introduce three Boolean indicators per tagged field unknown and enforce exactly one. For every row, list every ordering of its distinct unknowns and every assignment of field values to that list that violates the equation. Forbid that assignment by a disjunction of the corresponding negated indicators. Convert each such disjunction into proper 3-CNF with a private chain indexed by the row, list, values and chain position. For example, for literals $\ell_i$ use $(\neg z_{i-1}\lor\ell_i\lor z_i)$ with $z_0$ true and $z_s$ false, padding short pins with false anchors. A fixed gadget on three anchors can force them all false. Including every ordering makes all tags and clauses equivariant. The width bound is a fixed constant, so these tuples and finite choices form a polynomial-size choiceless interpretation. It can return a fixed formula on inputs exceeding the local bounds. On the hard pairs the 3-CNF is satisfiable exactly when the source system is consistent. A CPT decider for unordered proper 3-SAT would therefore distinguish the hard pairs. This is a corollary of the preprint's nondefinability theorem, with no numerical circuit-size lower bound or running-time consequence for an ordered RAM. [Preprint, §6 on interpretation programs](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/3344da59991ed94f9ba711217b29c1d760badfa6/preprints/Choiceless-polynomial-time-with-counting-does-not-capture-polynomial-time-September-23-2026/paper.pdf)
