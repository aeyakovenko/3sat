# A single simple permutation carries the unrestricted SAT time question

Let $P(\pi)$ be the graph on $1,\ldots,m$ whose edges join consecutive entries of the identity order or of the input permutation $\pi$. This is the incidence graph of a permutation in the pattern-matching literature. Call $\pi$ *simple* when the only nonempty sets consecutive in both orders are singletons and the whole set. The input is a self-delimiting length and $m$ packed, fixed-width indices, in all $N=\Theta(m\log(m+2))$ bits for $m\ge1$. [Permutation incidence graphs](https://www.cs.bme.hu/~dmarx/papers/marx-bodlaender-festschrift.pdf)

**Theorem.** Deciding whether $P(\pi)$ is 3-colorable for **simple** $\pi$ is equivalent to ordinary explicit CNF-SAT under uniform deterministic reductions with linear running time and linear encoded output length on an unrestricted logarithmic-word RAM. In particular an $O(N^j)$ decision algorithm exists for one problem exactly when one exists for the other, for $j=1,2,3$. Both paths in the reduction are supplied by the one permutation. They have disjoint edge sets.

There is a uniform verdict-preserving way to enlarge any pair of paths to such a simple permutation. A program can also undo this particular enlargement in linear time without receiving the old input or its construction history. The result gives a concrete search space for the unrestricted lower bound. It supplies no superlinear bound on actual deciders. Ordinary simple extensions that preserve a permutation as a *pattern* have a substantial literature. Inserting an entry between consecutive old entries can erase a graph edge, so pattern containment alone does not provide this reduction. Related prior work proves 3-colorability hard on 4-regular Hamiltonian graphs. Priority for the precise supplied-orders, simple-permutation and bit-length statement here is unestablished. [Brignall, Ruškuc and Vatter](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.4378) · [Cavallaro and Fluschnik](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08470)

## Encoding the graph by two orders

Use the earlier linear reduction from ordinary SAT to an explicitly listed simple graph $G$ for 3-coloring. On a source of $n$ bits there are $O(n/\log(n+2)+1)$ graph items after normalizing and removing duplicate clauses. [Graph coloring gadgets and physical accounting](branching-in-equations-over-six-permutations.md#encoding-arbitrary-sat-with-three-occurrences)

Here is a linear-item conversion for any $v$-vertex, $e$-edge graph. Give each vertex a port. For every incidence append a splitter to its vertex's port chain. With incoming port $a$, outgoing port $b$, data vertex $c$, and private centers $u,v'$, put $a,u,v',b$ consecutively on a red path and $a,v',c,u,b$ on a blue path. The centers are adjacent, and each of $a,b,c$ is adjacent to both centers. Thus all three ports have the same color and every chosen port color extends. Identify each outgoing port with the next incoming port on its chain. Join the two data vertices of each source edge by a red edge. The red pieces are the vertex chains and the disjoint edge pairs. The blue pieces are the vertex chains. On an isolated vertex both chains consist of its single port.

For clarity, both families can be completed to edge-disjoint Hamilton cycles before cutting one closing edge from each. There are $r=v+e$ red pieces and $v$ blue pieces for $v>0$. In each red cyclic gap $i$ insert two seven-vertex blocks $A_i,B_i$ in that order. In the blue gaps distribute the $r$ pairs, at least one per gap, and visit each pair as $B_i,A_i$. Inside each block the two paths from endpoint 0 to endpoint 6 are
$$
                  0,1,2,3,4,5,6 \quad\text{and}\quad 0,3,5,1,4,2,6 .
$$
They have no common edge. Equal and unequal prescribed endpoints both extend: in position order $0,\ldots,6$ use the colorings 0121020 and 0101201 and permute the colors. Each block endpoint has only two neighbors outside its own block. After fixing all the original colors, color the block endpoints in any order, avoiding their already colored outside neighbors, and extend each block. The edges between blocks are disjoint across the two orders because the pair is reversed, the order of distinct pairs is the same, and each cyclic blue gap begins and ends next to an original vertex. The red and blue edges inside the splitters are also disjoint. Cut the closing edges incident to blocks to obtain paths. The splitter edges and the source inequalities all survive. A global coloring restricts to a valid source coloring, and every source coloring extends. The output has $15v+22e$ vertices. The empty graph can use one isolated placeholder. Consecutive IDs, incidence arrays and the inverse red order are constructible in linear word work. [Generator and extension audit](two_hamilton_paths_sat_audit.py)

## A larger simple instance with the same verdict

Start with red and blue paths on $m$ entries, with red labeled in its natural order. Append $d=2$ fresh entries for even $m$ and $d=3$ for odd $m$ to both paths in the same order. Close just the blue path into a cycle. Every old coloring extends. The first new color can avoid both old end colors, and the last can avoid its predecessor and the first old blue color. The padded size $b=m+d$ is even. Treat the empty source separately or use the even two-vertex cycle with its two directed adjacencies.

For each padded vertex $i$ put the following seven vertices consecutively in a new red path:
$$
                     s_i, a_i, b_i, m_i, c_i, d_i, t_i .
$$
Prepend a fresh $x$ and append a fresh $y$ to this path. The new blue path has a low part followed by $x,y$ and a high part. Assign the edges of the old even blue cycle alternately type $s$ or $t$. For a directed edge $i\to j$ put the four-vertex segment $b_i,s_i,s_j,b_j$ in the low part if its type is $s$. For type $t$ put $c_i,t_i,t_j,c_j$. Concatenate these segments in old cycle order. The high part consists of $a_i,m_i,d_i$ for each $i$. Take these in increasing order of $i$ when $d=2$, decreasing order when $d=3$. Both orders work for coloring. Each vertex appears exactly once in the blue order, and the final size is $7b+2$.

In the combined graph, $s_i,m_i$ are both adjacent to the adjacent pair $a_i,b_i$, so they share a color. Likewise $m_i,t_i$ share a color because of the adjacent pair $c_i,d_i$. The intervening red edges and the middle edges of the low segments therefore enforce both old orders.

For the converse, fix a padded coloring at $s_i,m_i,t_i$. Each of the four remaining vertices of block $i$ has a list of the two colors different from its terminal color. Give $x$ the two colors different from the first terminal and $y$ those different from the last. The remaining edges on list vertices split into two matchings. One consists of the pairs $a_i b_i$, $c_i d_i$ and $xy$. The other consists of the joins between consecutive blue segments, the join from the last low segment to $x$, and the join from $y$ to the first high segment. Paths and even cycles are colorable from arbitrary lists of size two. On a cycle with unequal consecutive lists start at a vertex with a color absent from its predecessor's list, then go around greedily. If all lists agree, alternate the two colors. This extends every old coloring.

To see simplicity, include $x$ in the low half and $y$ in the high half. They are last low and first high in blue, and the two **endpoints** in red. Any common interval meeting both halves contains both endpoints, hence the entire red path. In red the high vertices are isolated within the high half. The only consecutive low pairs are $x,s_1$ and $t_i,s_{i+1}$. The blue low neighbor of $x$ is a spare, and all blue terminal-to-terminal edges are $s$–$s$ or $t$–$t$. None of the low pairs are blue edges. This also proves the paths are edge-disjoint, since a shared edge would be a two-element common interval. [Successor and history-free decoder](simple_permutation_successor.py)

To recognize this particular image, recover $b$ from the length, read the high part to determine $d$, and read the source terminal of each four-entry low segment. Remove the last $d$ padded vertices and regenerate the proposed output to check it byte for byte. This takes $O(m)$ word work on packed indices. A solver that repeats this operation on verified images and falls back to a total 3-color solver handles arbitrarily many iterations from a fixed finite set of seeds in $O(N)$ time. Each successful peel reduces the number of entries by a constant factor.

Pulling all parts together, the forward conversion writes $O(n)$ physical bits in $O(n)$ native steps. In the reverse direction use three color flags per vertex, enforce exactly one and forbid equal colors at each adjacent pair. There are $O(m)$ clauses and dense names, costing $O(N)$ bits and construction time. A promised simple input suffices for hardness. If the language must reject malformed or nonsimple permutations, verify range and uniqueness with an indexed array and use the linear-time representation of common intervals of two orders to test simplicity before constructing the SAT instance. [Bergeron, Chauve, de Montgolfier and Raffinot](https://doi.org/10.1137/060651331)

## A necessary cost on an actual RAM run

There is a canonical negative simple permutation for each odd wheel (an odd rim of $h\ge3$ vertices, all joined to a hub). This forces a measurable cost on **every** correct deterministic RAM program on that very input. The wheel is not 3-colorable: its hub leaves only two colors for an odd cycle. Deleting a rim edge breaks that cycle. Deleting a spoke lets its rim vertex take the hub color. In both cases the graph becomes 3-colorable.

For each wheel edge $(u,v)$ add a disjoint private edge $(u',v')$ before the first encoding. To disable the old edge, swap the two entries for the incidence data of $v$ and $v'$ in the blue order only. Red keeps the two inequality pairs, but the blue center adjacencies put the two data colors in their exchanged splitter chains. Equivalently, name the data by their new chains: the red inequalities now connect $u$ to $v'$ and $u'$ to $v$. Private colors extend independently, and both paths otherwise impose the same constraints as before. Each edge has its own pair of possible swap positions. Thus on this cube every nonempty set of swaps gives a positive input, and the all-enabled input is negative.

Apply the simple-permutation successor. Every old blue entry has its own four positions among the low records: two where its cycle vertex is the source and two where it is the destination. Their *positions* depend only on the old entry's index, and their *values* only on the old entry's value and the segment type. The high part, padding and header are fixed. Consequently the sets of fields changed by distinct edge switches remain disjoint even after the successor. A program must read a word intersecting the changed fields for **each** edge on its run on the all-enabled input. Otherwise its run on the corresponding positive neighbor is identical. Each input word intersects only constantly many fixed-width fields, and a RAM instruction makes only constantly many word accesses. On this input the program needs $\Omega(h)=\Omega(N/\log N)$ executed instructions.

The word bound is tight on the entire constructed cube for a single globally correct program. For the specified ordering of the wheel the first construction has $15(h+1)+74(2h)=163h+15$ vertices including the private edges, hence the simple output has $1141h+121$ entries. Recover the candidate $h$, regenerate the baseline and swap pairs, and check in linear word work that the decoded permutation differs only by legal pair swaps. Return whether any switch was disabled. Fall back to a total decider on other inputs. This directly tests the desired growth of execution time: enlarging the wheel gives linearly many independent reasons to read particular **words**, while its explicit bit length grows by a further logarithmic factor. A superlinear bound still requires work beyond these compulsory reads.

### The same mandatory reads on proper DIMACS files

Render the simple graphs above as ordinary ASCII 3-SAT. Use dense names $X_{i,c}=3i+c$ for zero-based vertices and colors $c=1,2,3$, plus $Z=3m+1$ for a graph of $m$ entries. Require at least one color at each vertex. For every consecutive pair $u,v$ in each order and each color $c$, write both clauses $\neg X_{u,c}\lor\neg X_{v,c}\lor Z$ and $\neg X_{u,c}\lor\neg X_{v,c}\lor\neg Z$. They forbid a common chosen color. Conversely any satisfying assignment yields a proper coloring by choosing one true flag at every vertex. Every clause has exactly three distinct variables.

Left-pad each entire signed decimal token with spaces to a common $O(\log m)$-byte width. The header and all offsets are fixed across the cube. Changing a blue-order field affects only its own endpoint-token positions in the clauses for its incident edges. Two adjacent permutation positions have distinct literal slots even when they appear in the same clause. Consequently the different wheel-edge switches have disjoint sets of affected token fields; each RAM word intersects only constantly many fields. **Every correct deterministic SAT program takes $\Omega(N/\log N)$ executed steps on the same explicit negative file** at each designated length, now with $N$ the physical DIMACS bit length.

The tight upper bound on this file family also includes physical parsing. From the canonical header recover the candidate wheel size. Precompute fixed-width packed spellings of all signed names in $O(m)$ word steps. Generate a name $10q+d$ by appending digit $d$ to its parent's signed spelling and dropping the leading padding byte, with the initial positive and negative prefixes handled separately. The slot width leaves room for the sign of the largest name, so the discarded byte is always a space when a child is generated. Each spelling occupies a constant number of RAM words. One differing endpoint slot per switch recovers its candidate bit. Regenerate the complete candidate file and compare it word by word, with $O(m)$ word operations in total. On a match return the known wheel verdict, otherwise use a total SAT solver. The bounded family therefore cannot yield a stronger time lower bound by counting the independent switches.
